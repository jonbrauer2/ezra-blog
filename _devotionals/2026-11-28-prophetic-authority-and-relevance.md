On November 9, 1989, East Germany's press spokesperson stood before the cameras and fumbled his own government's new travel policy. Asked when it would take effect, Günter Schabowski shuffled his papers and guessed: *"As far as I know, this enters into force . . . immediately, without delay."* Within minutes, crowds were flowing toward the checkpoints, demanding the border open as announced. Within weeks, a wall that had divided a nation for twenty-eight years was in pieces. Schabowski had no power to raise a single gate — but the crowd believed his words carried the state's authority, and they acted on them. People have often been delegated authority to speak for a company or a country. The question this week's lesson presses is heavier: what about those who speak for God?

Friday's devotional left us at a table, tasting the prophetic word to see that it is good. Before the meal, the lesson asks about the voice itself — what authority it actually holds. And it sharpens the question with uncomfortable cases: prophets who never wrote a page of Scripture. Nathan. John the Baptist. *"Yes,"* the lesson says plainly, *"even Ellen G. White."*

The Memory Text answers before the week begins: *"For the word of God is living and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the division of soul and spirit, and of joints and marrow, and is a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart"* (Hebrews 4:12, NKJV). April's devotional heard that verse as comfort — the word coming alive on the day it was needed most. This week it returns as a claim about authority. Schabowski's sentence needed a government behind it to matter, and he still got the policy wrong. This Word carries its own — living, not archived; active, not awaiting approval; and it does what no decree can: it reads the reader.

Moses knew the difference from the inside. At the bush he pleaded inadequacy — *"Oh, Lord, I am not eloquent"* — and tried to decline the commission altogether: *"send, I pray thee, by the hand of him whom thou wilt send"* (Exodus 4:10, 13, ASV). God's answer was no crash course in public speaking: *"Who hath made man's mouth? . . . Now therefore go, and I will be with thy mouth, and teach thee what thou shalt speak"* (Exodus 4:11, 12, ASV). Aaron would stand beside him as *"a mouth"* (Exodus 4:16) — a chain of representation in which the authority never belonged to the speaker at all.

That is why Nathan, who never wrote a canonical line, could break a king with one sentence — May's devotional recalled the visit; the authority was not Nathan's. And October's devotional already walked beside John the Baptist — no scroll, no pulpit, yet ranked first by the One who sent him.

One chain of voices, one Source. The question this week is not whether a messenger impresses us, but whether the living Word stands behind him — and, since that Word discerns the thoughts and intents of the heart, what it will find in ours when it arrives.

The word is living. What is it reading in you this Sabbath?
