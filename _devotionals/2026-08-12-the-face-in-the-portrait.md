Lay the fifteen verbs of 1 Corinthians 13 next to the life of Jesus and something strange happens. Every line matches. Patient — yes, with disciples who kept missing the point. Kind — yes, with the woman at the well and the tax collector in the tree. Not seeking His own — yes, all the way to a garden where His own will bent to His Father's. Bearing all things, enduring all things — yes, and yes, all the way to a cross.

Paul's chapter on love is not primarily a rulebook. It is a portrait. And the face in the portrait is unmistakable once you see it.

Jesus made the connection explicit in the upper room the night He was betrayed: *"A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love one another."* (John 13:34, NKJV) The commandment was not new because love had never been commanded before — Moses had commanded love a thousand years earlier. It was new because the standard was new. *As I have loved you.* Not: as you love yourselves. Not: as you find natural. As I have loved you.

Which means the standard is not your natural emotional capacity. The standard is Christ's capacity, offered as the pattern into which you are being conformed. And that is either terrifying or freeing, depending on how you read it. Terrifying if you think the pattern is an achievement you have to reach. Freeing if you understand that the pattern is a gift you receive by remaining in Him.

John, decades later, writes down the definition his generation had lived with: *"In this is love, not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son to be the propitiation for our sins."* (1 John 4:10, NKJV) Love does not start with our capacity. Love starts with the Cross. The definition begins at Calvary and works outward, and every subsequent act of Christian love is a small echo of that original act. We do not manufacture love. We participate in it.

Try this exercise. Read 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 slowly, and this time substitute "Jesus" for "love." *Jesus suffers long and is kind; Jesus does not envy; Jesus does not parade Himself, is not puffed up; does not behave rudely, does not seek His own, is not provoked, thinks no evil; does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth; bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things.*

Somewhere in the reading, a line will hit differently. It will feel less like ethics and more like a face. That is the point. That is who you are being formed to bear.

Jesus finishes the sentence in John: *"By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another."* (John 13:35, NKJV) Not by your doctrine. Not by your polity. Not by your church attendance. Not even by your evangelistic vigor. By your love. That is the world's evidence, the disciple's mark, the visible sign that Christ is actually forming you into His likeness.

The portrait is Him. The invitation is to become a small version of it, hanging in the local gallery of your neighborhood, for anyone who wants to know what He looks like.
