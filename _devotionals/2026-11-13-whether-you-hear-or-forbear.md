Last Friday's devotional watched her answer God's command to write. This Friday the lesson turns the page and asks what happened afterward — when the messages left her pen and reached other people's ears.

The case is stranger than memory tends to keep it. Arthur White, her grandson and a former director of the Ellen G. White Estate, estimated she received hundreds of visions and prophetic dreams — some lasting minutes, some hours, at times witnessed by large groups, on occasion open to examination by her own skeptics. In 1868, James White described what onlookers saw: utterly unconscious of her surroundings, she did not breathe; her muscles became rigid and her joints fixed, yet her gestures stayed free and graceful; her eyes remained open, and afterward her sight failed temporarily before returning (*Life Incidents*, pp. 271–274). No one controlled when a vision came or how long it held — not even she herself.

And the hearers split, just as Israel had split all week. Some accepted God's counsels; others rejected them. We have spent seven days inside the ways of refusal — a good report disbelieved at the border, a roll sliced leaf by leaf into a fire, messengers mocked until there was no remedy. Here was the same collision in a modern key.

She carried the burden without self-defense: *"God has set me as a reprover of His people; and . . . as He has laid upon me the heavy burden, He will make those to whom this message is given responsible for the manner in which they treat it. . . . I shall warn and counsel and reprove and encourage as the Spirit of God dictates, whether men will hear or whether they will forbear. My duty is not to please myself, but to do the will of my heavenly Father, who has given me my work."*—*Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 4, pp. 231, 232.

That is Ezekiel's ancient sentence in a nineteenth-century voice — and it fixes the weight where God fixes it: on the treatment of the message, not the style of the messenger. Her counsel for skeptics was "long patience and brotherly love" (*Testimonies*, vol. 1, p. 328); her confidence was that honest searchers would find the proof where it belongs, in the messages themselves. God gives evidence enough, she wrote, "to balance our faith on the side of truth" (vol. 4, p. 231) — enough to decide by, never so much that deciding costs nothing.

So the quarter's question stands, and so does its invitation. Take one counsel you have quietly shelved — on health, on speech, on the uses of your time — and weigh it this week against Scripture alone. Where it stands with the Word, the hearing it awaits is yours to give.

—

*Ezra*
