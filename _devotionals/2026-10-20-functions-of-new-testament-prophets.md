Ask someone what a prophet's job is, and you will usually hear one word: predictions. But when Paul describes the function of prophecy, the future is not the headline. *"He who prophesies speaks edification and exhortation and comfort to men"* (1 Corinthians 14:3, NKJV). Three functions — build up, stir up, cheer up. That is the job description.

The first word is a builder's word. *Edification* literally means the act of building. In the body-of-Christ list, prophets stand second, right after apostles (1 Corinthians 12:27–28), because the church is a building still under construction, and it needs heaven's blueprints. Ephesians 4:11–13 puts prophets among apostles, evangelists, pastors, and teachers, *"for the equipping of the saints for the work of ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ, till we all come to the unity of the faith... to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ."* Notice the end point: full-grown Christlikeness. Every true prophetic word is construction material for that building — and Jesus is the central focus of the message all the way through.

We met Judas and Silas yesterday; after the Jerusalem council they exercised exactly this ministry, and the record of their work reads like a one-sentence summary of everything above: they *“exhorted and strengthened the brethren.”* And the comfort was no afterthought. Paul says that when God's people prophesy one by one, all learn *“and all may be comforted”* (1 Corinthians 14:31). Prophets do warn — sometimes sternly — but even the warnings are construction work: clearing the site so the building can rise. In times of tribulation and persecution, the prophetic gift has been a wellspring of encouragement and hope.

There is a reason we can lean on such words. In Revelation 22:6 Jesus is revealed as *“Lord God of the holy prophets,”* whose *“words are faithful and true.”* In a world where we can trust so little — the headlines, the advertising, the promises — what comes from God's servants, when they speak for God, is faithful and true. Prophets build with materials that hold.

And the building has a goal. That phrase — *“the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ”* — is precisely what Ellen White described as God's aim: *“Higher than the highest human thought can reach is God's ideal for His children. Godliness—godlikeness—is the goal to be reached.”*—Ellen G. White, *Education*, p. 18. The prophetic gift is God's construction crew, working toward that ideal one life at a time.

So two questions for today. When you speak into someone's life — at home, at church, at work — does it build up or tear down? And when a message claims God's authority, apply Paul's test before anything else: does it edify, exhort, and comfort in Christ, or does it merely make noise? True prophecy leaves the body of Christ measurably stronger than it found it.
