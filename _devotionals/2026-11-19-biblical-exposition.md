Yesterday we walked the path of interpretation — words, context, the whole of Scripture. Today the lesson asks the question the path itself cannot answer: where is it supposed to end?

Consider the traveler on the desert road. He was a court official of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians — powerful, educated, devout, heading home from worship in Jerusalem. He owned something most people of his century never touched: his own copy of the scroll of Isaiah, and he was reading it aloud as the chariot rolled on. Every advantage a reader could want. Yet when the Spirit sent Philip jogging alongside to ask, "Do you understand what you are reading?" his honest answer was, "How can I, unless someone guides me?" (Acts 8:30–31, NKJV). Possession, reverence, and hunger are not the same thing as understanding.

Here is the surprise the lesson holds up. Jesus Christ was, is, and always will be "the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6, NKJV) — He needed to borrow from no one. Yet when He stood up to preach, He was a thoroughly biblical preacher. "Now after John was put in prison, Jesus came to Galilee, preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God, and saying, 'The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand'" (Mark 1:14–15, NKJV). *Fulfilled* is Daniel's word — the messianic clock of Daniel 9:24–27, the weeks running out on schedule, heaven's calendar arriving exactly when the prophecy said it would. He quoted Exodus on the mountain, Isaiah in Nazareth: "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your ears." The Word made flesh quoted the written Word — and showed every future reader where all of it goes.

Philip did not improvise an opinion at the chariot window. Beginning at that Scripture, he preached Jesus to him (Acts 8:35) — and the exposition ended at a roadside baptism, the official going on his way rejoicing (Acts 8:39; compare Psalm 51:12). The lesson draws out three lessons from this: simple reading does not automatically become deep understanding; in the study of Scripture we can, and should, learn from each other; and each of us carries a personal obligation to share sound Bible messages with others. Study has two aims — personal spiritual growth, and leading others into a saving relationship with Jesus, for it is the truth that sanctifies (John 17:17).

But note the balance. Philip could open the scroll; only the eunuch could believe for himself — and he answered for himself, at the water, before he rode on. Guidance is never permission to outsource conviction. Even when we need help understanding a text, we must always be convinced in our own minds of what we believe.

When you finish reading today, ask where the page landed. If it has not yet led you to Christ, keep reading — or be someone's Philip on the road. Every prophetic page has a destination. Read until you arrive.
