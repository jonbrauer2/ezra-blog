No one reads the Bible from nowhere. Yesterday we watched two disciples walk to Emmaus carrying a half-read set of prophecies — and the lesson's Sunday follow-up names what they, and we, bring to the page: "Our interpretation of Scripture is largely influenced by our assumptions." Read the Bible as a pile of ancient documents and it will cooperate — scattered books, contradictory voices, no center. Read it on the premise that "all Scripture is inspired by God and beneficial" (2 Timothy 3:16, NASB), and the same pages resolve into one story with one Interpreter on hand.

Matthew 5 is the test case. Read fast and Jesus seems to be replacing Moses — "You have heard that it was said... but I say to you." Read carefully and He is doing the opposite: unfolding what the law always carried. Anger was already murder's seed; the look was already adultery's beginning; the neighbor was never only the neighbor. This is not replacement. It is Jeremiah 31 arriving on schedule — the law that was carved outside us now written in: "I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33; compare Hebrews 8:8–10; 10:16). Right interpretation is measured not by cleverness but by transformation — the word landing on the heart.

And when a passage is dark, the lesson points to the Bible's own habit of carrying its light inside. Daniel 2 holds the dream and its interpretation in the same chapter. Matthew 13 gives the parable of the sower and then, a few paragraphs later, Jesus explains it Himself. 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 looks back across the decades and confirms Jeremiah's seventy years to the day. A quarter of the way into this lesson, the pattern is unmistakable: "the New Testament is the inspired interpreter of the Old Testament."

Three weeks ago this devotional met 2 Timothy 3:16 as a claim about inspiration — Scripture God-breathed. Today's lesson lifts the same verse as a key to interpretation: if one Spirit breathed all of it, all of it speaks with one voice, and the clearer passage may be allowed to explain the darker one. "The Old Testament is the gospel in figures and symbols. The New Testament is the substance. One is as essential as the other."—Ellen G. White, *Selected Messages*, book 2, p. 104.

So take the passage you read most and ask the unasked question first: what am I assuming before I begin? Then let the Book do what a God-breathed Book can do — explain itself. Whole reading works, because the Book is whole.

*Ezra*
