Half of Paul's portrait of love is a list of things love will not do. Envy, self-display, rudeness, self-seeking, irritability, ledger-keeping, celebration of wrongdoing. It reads uncomfortably like a diagnosis of the church he was writing to — and often, of the one looking back from the mirror.

The Greek word that stops you cold is in the middle of the list. *"Thinks no evil"* — the verb is *logizetai*, an accounting word. It is the language of bookkeeping. Love does not keep the ledger. Love does not tally offenses. Love does not maintain the running list of past wrongs, ready to be produced when the next argument requires evidence. *"Love… thinks no evil; does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth."* (1 Corinthians 13:5-6, NKJV)

This is not naivety. It is not pretending nothing happened. It is not the erasure of memory or the abandonment of appropriate boundaries. It is the active refusal to let the ledger define the relationship going forward. The wrong happened, and it was wrong, and love does not deny that. But love refuses to keep the wrong on file, ready to be pulled out and used as leverage in the next disagreement.

Most of us have kept such a ledger, at least once. A spouse who said the thing they should not have said. A parent whose absence in some critical year still stings. A friend who defaulted at the moment the friendship most needed them. A coworker whose credit-taking cost you something real. The ledger is not fantasy — the entries are accurate. But love asks whether the accuracy has to become the story.

Paul is describing the shape of a life the Spirit is producing. And the shape is a life where the ledgers get closed. Not because the entries were false, but because grace has begun to write in a different column.

Then there is the smaller phrase that hurts almost as much: *"not easily provoked."* Irritability is usually the outer sign of something deeper. Exhaustion. Fear. Self-protection. The person who is easily provoked has usually been running on empty for longer than they realize. Love addresses the root, not just the surface. Rest, prayer, honesty about what has been depleting you — these are not spiritual luxuries but the very conditions under which "not easily provoked" becomes possible.

Romans picks up the theme: *"Let love be without hypocrisy. Abhor what is evil. Cling to what is good. Be kindly affectionate to one another with brotherly love, in honor giving preference to one another."* (Romans 12:9-10, NKJV) Preference — the deliberate choice to weight another's dignity above your own claim to prominence. That is what love does. That is what the ledger-keeper cannot do, because the ledger keeps demanding to be honored.

Ellen White wrote that "when we lift up our petitions to God, we should have our hearts filled with tender pity and love for one another." Prayer with an unclosed ledger becomes strange work — you are asking God to forgive you while refusing to release the person you have written against.

What ledger have you been keeping about someone — a spouse, a parent, a coworker, a former friend? What would it cost you to close the account today? And what, quietly, might it open?
