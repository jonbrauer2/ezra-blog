The year was 1894. Ellen White wrote to the church in Battle Creek, condemning the purchase of bicycles — and more than a century later, the citation still earns a smile. A prophet, critics say, caught in the fads of her decade. But today's closing lesson invites a different reading: ask what principle was on her mind. Bicycles were extravagantly expensive then, and her burden was stewardship of God-given resources. Condemn the pride of the purchase, and the counsel outlives the fad it rode in on. It is the week's whole argument applied one last time — this time to her own writings.

The lesson sets out five rules, and they sound familiar because they are this week's rules turned on the lesser light itself.

First, read prayerfully, with an open mind rather than a spirit of doubt — the lesson warns that our mindset is a lens coloring all we hear, read, and see, and quotes her warning in every direction: *"If you search the Scriptures to vindicate your own opinions, you will never reach the truth. Search in order to learn what the Lord says."*—*Christ's Object Lessons*, p. 112.

Second, honor the context. She wrote that "time and place must be considered."—*Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 57. A fragment torn from its historical or literary setting has caused, the lesson notes plainly, no end of trouble among church members.

Third, seek the principle. That is the bicycle rule: find the main point she was emphasizing, not merely the circumstance she was addressing.

Fourth, read the whole corpus. An obscure statement must sit under her general teaching on a subject, because misuse was a grief she named herself: *"Much that purports to be a message from Sister White, serves the purpose of misrepresenting Sister White, making her testify in favor of things that are not in accordance with her mind or judgment."*—*Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 44. There is the Emmaus danger in another voice — the one-sided reading that made the disciples' hope die holding half the evidence can make even a true messenger testify to what she never meant.

Fifth, make sure she said it. Numerous statements circulate under her name that she never wrote; the safest test is to look for the source reference, then read it where it lives. Scripture itself honors that pattern: "Do not despise prophecies. Test all things; hold fast what is good" (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21, NKJV). A prophet's writings deserve neither credulity nor contempt — they deserve testing.

Last Friday's devotional ended with the messenger's burden: her calling was to warn and counsel "whether men will hear or whether they will forbear." The reading of the message carries a burden too. So take up again the counsel you quietly shelved — on health, on speech, on money — and read it whole: in its time, for its principle, against her full counsel and the Word of God. Where it stands, it stands.
