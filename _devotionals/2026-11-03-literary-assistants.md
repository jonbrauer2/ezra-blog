Somewhere in the middle of the long list of greetings at the close of Romans, one name stops and introduces itself: "I, Tertius, who wrote this epistle, greet you in the Lord" (Romans 16:22, NKJV). The letter to the Romans has carried more revival than perhaps any other page of Scripture — and it passed through this man's hand. That greeting is his only verse in the Bible.

He was not the first. When Jeremiah's scroll reached the palace, the officials put a pointed question to his scribe: "How did you write all these words?" Baruch's answer is one of the loveliest lines in prophecy: "He pronounced all these words to me," he said, "and I wrote them with ink in the book" (Jeremiah 36:17–18). His hand, not his words. And when the officials realized what the scroll contained, their next instruction was practical: go and hide, both Baruch and Jeremiah (Jeremiah 36:19). The scribe shared the prophet's danger without sharing the prophet's message — fidelity the page barely records.

Paul, for his part, dictated most of his letters, then took the pen for the final line: "This salutation by my own hand—Paul" (Colossians 4:18, NKJV). To the Thessalonians he explained why: it was "a sign in every epistle" (2 Thessalonians 3:17, NKJV). A letter "as if from us" had already unsettled that congregation (2 Thessalonians 2:2), so the apostle's own handwriting became the watermark — the guarantee that the true letter could always be told from a forgery.

The lesson's commentary notes that this arrangement shaped even the sound of Scripture: "Although it is a fact that the style of language of 2 Peter is different from that of the first epistle, this may reasonably be accounted for by the probability that Peter . . . employed secretarial aid in phrasing an epistle he was writing in Greek. If Paul, who was fully at home in Greek, used secretaries, as he quite evidently did, it is even more logical to think that Peter, whose native language was Aramaic, would have done so, and that these secretaries would have exerted a definite influence on the wording of his letters in Greek."—*The SDA Bible Commentary*, vol. 5, p. 186. The thoughts were inspired; the hands that carried them varied — which is why the same heaven sounds like a fisherman, a scholar, and a physician.

That is also the quiet invitation here. God's work has never been carried by the famous alone; it has been carried by faithful hands — the one who takes the minutes, drives the route, sets out the chairs, writes the card nobody signs. Tertius got one verse. Baruch nearly got arrested. Heaven keeps better records than the page does.

What has God placed in your hand this week that seems too small to matter? Write it down, show up, hold the pen steady. Somewhere the Author is still dictating — and He is looking for pens that don't correct Him.
