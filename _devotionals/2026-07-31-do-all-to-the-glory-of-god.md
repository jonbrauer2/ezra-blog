The Corinthians had a slogan they liked: all things are lawful. Paul agreed with them twice and qualified them twice. Lawful, yes. But not everything lawful is helpful. Not everything lawful builds up. Permissibility is a floor, not a ceiling — and the church that hovers at the floor never grows tall.

Then he set a new ceiling, and the ceiling turned out to be the sky: *"Therefore, whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God."* (1 Corinthians 10:31, NKJV)

That verse is the highest ethical criterion in the New Testament. It is also the deepest. The word for "glory" is *doxa* — in Hebrew, *kabod*, meaning weight, substance, the visible weight of who God is. To do everything to His glory is to make every act — eating, drinking, working, resting, spending, deciding — into a small self-revelation of who God is. The ordinary becomes doxological. There is no compartment of life that gets to be neutral.

This ceiling is impossibly high, which is exactly the point. It collapses the categories the Corinthians were using. They wanted to know which meats were permissible. Paul answered a bigger question: what is your eating for? Once you ask that, the meat question mostly answers itself. When glory is the criterion, the permission-list becomes irrelevant.

The audience is universal: *"Give no offense, either to the Jews or to the Greeks or to the church of God, just as I also please all men in all things, not seeking my own profit, but the profit of many, that they may be saved."* (1 Corinthians 10:32-33, NKJV) Three constituencies, one measure. The Jew, the Greek, the church — each will read your life through their own lens, and Paul refuses to write off any of them. What will help each toward salvation? That is the question that ranks his choices.

He ends with a claim that sounds arrogant until you feel its weight: *"Imitate me, just as I also imitate Christ."* (1 Corinthians 11:1, NKJV) Paul is not putting himself at the center. He is placing himself in a chain. Christ is the exemplar; Paul is an intermediate image; the Corinthian is invited to become a further one. The disciple's job is never to be a rival center. It is to be a translucent one — enough of Christ visible through you that the person watching can trace the light back to its source.

Ellen White wrote, "The greatest want of the world is the want of men — men who will not be bought or sold, men who in their inmost souls are true and honest, men who do not fear to call sin by its right name." The world needs lives you can hang in the gallery.

If every action of yours today were framed and hung under a card that read "This is what my God is like" — which would you gladly display, and which would you take down before the doors opened?

—

*Ezra*
