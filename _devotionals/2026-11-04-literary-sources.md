The Bible is not embarrassed by its own bibliography. Near the close of Ecclesiastes, the Preacher's method is laid on the table: "he pondered and sought out and set in order many proverbs. The Preacher sought to find acceptable words; and what was written was upright—words of truth" (Ecclesiastes 12:9–10, NKJV). Sought out. Pondered. Set in order. The wisest man in Scripture did not invent everything he recorded — the famous portrait of the virtuous woman that opens Proverbs 31 came to him "the utterance of his mother" (Proverbs 31:1), a king named Lemuel, otherwise unknown to history. And the texture of Proverbs carries echoes of Egyptian wisdom literature besides. Inspiration put all of it to work.

The historians did the same. "The rest of the acts of Solomon," we are told, "are they not written in the Book of the Acts of Solomon?" (1 Kings 11:41, NKJV) — a source we may never hold, cited for all to see. Ezra filled his book with copies of Persian letters and royal decrees pulled straight from the empire's archives (Ezra 4–7). Nehemiah "found a register of the genealogy" and wrote it into his narrative (Nehemiah 7:5–72, NKJV). Court records, family registers, official correspondence — heaven trusted all of it to carry the story.

Luke's "orderly account," which we have considered here before, is the New Testament's great example of the same principle. The lesson's commentary draws the lesson out: "It is clear from Luke's experience that Inspiration functions in a manner consistent with the natural operation of the mental faculties and does not set them aside. Here is an inspired writer who was led by the Holy Spirit to give diligent study to the available oral and written source materials on the life of Christ, and then to combine into a connected narrative the information thus gathered."—*The SDA Bible Commentary*, vol. 5, p. 669. The Spirit did not switch Luke's mind off; He switched it on.

And the borrowing sometimes crossed lines we would not dare. Jude opens his warning about false teachers with a quotation lifted from 1 Enoch 1:9 — a noncanonical book. Paul, without naming his sources, quotes the Greek poets twice: "for in Him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:28, NKJV), and even the blunt "Cretans are always liars" (Titus 1:12, NKJV). Truth was never the devil's property. Wherever a line was true — in a pagan hymn, a court archive, a mother's proverb — the Spirit could reach in and set it in the current of Scripture without the current ceasing to be God's Word.

This matters more than we admit, because we are quietly tempted to think God's work and homework are opposites — that preparation is for people short on the Spirit. The Bible says otherwise. The Book you carry to church is a library with footprints of study on every page: interview notes, copied decrees, cited annals, quoted poets. God honored diligence enough to build it into Scripture; He will honor it in your Sabbath School lesson, your sermon, your testimony too. Grace is not opposed to effort. It is opposed to earning — and study earns nothing, but it readies the vessel.

So do some digging this week. Follow a cross-reference you have always skipped. Find the place where Scripture cites its own sources, and notice how unafraid it is. And when you stumble on something beautiful and true in an unexpected place — a historian, a poet, a textbook — receive it with a smile, because you know whose truth it is. The Preacher's standard still governs everything written for God: acceptable words, "words of truth" (Ecclesiastes 12:10, NKJV).
