The Corinthian church had sorted itself into camps. Paul had baptized some of them. Apollos had preached beautifully to others. Peter's name carried weight for a third group. And then, tellingly, there was a fourth camp — the ones who said they were of Christ. Even that one was a problem. It claimed Him as exclusive property against the others.

Paul asks three questions that stop the argument by naming its foundation: *"Is Christ divided? Was Paul crucified for you? Or were you baptized in the name of Paul?"* (1 Corinthians 1:13, NKJV) The two defining events of Christian identity — Cross and baptism — belong to Christ alone. Not to a teacher, not to a tradition, not to a movement inside the movement. You were not crucified for by Paul. You were not baptized into Paul. So why are you being of Paul?

The verb Paul uses for unity is *katartizō* — the word fishermen used for mending nets. Unity is not new construction; it is restoration of proper condition. The net was always meant to hold together. When it tears, you do not invent a new material — you repair what was already there.

We forget how seriously Paul takes this. In Romans he lists *strife* among the works of the flesh, right next to murder and malice (Romans 1:29). Adventism, in its calmer moods, has often treated division as a pastoral inconvenience. Paul treats it as a spiritual emergency.

The ancient factions rarely disappear; they only change names. Today it is the theological camps we align with, the favorite podcasters we quote, the platforms we use to signal our tribe. The pattern is identical. Someone asks what kind of Adventist we are, and out comes a modifier — historic, progressive, mainstream, remnant, thoughtful — and the modifier is doing the identity work that only Christ should be doing.

*Ellen White* writes: *"The oneness and unity of God's truth-believing remnant people carries powerful conviction to the world."* (Testimonies, vol. 1, p. 327) Powerful conviction. Not persuasive argument, not tactical alliance — conviction. The world can smell competitive Christianity a mile away. It cannot ignore a church that has been repaired at the tear.

Finish the sentence honestly. In terms of your theological identity, you are most aligned with — whose name did you fill in? What would it look like today to say only, *I am of Christ,* and mean it?
