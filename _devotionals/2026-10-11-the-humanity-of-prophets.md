Fire from heaven doesn't usually get followed by a resignation letter. But that is exactly what happens between 1 Kings 18 and 1 Kings 19. One day Elijah is calling down fire on Mount Carmel and outrunning Ahab's chariot to Jezreel; a day or two later he is sprinting for his life from Jezebel, collapsing under a solitary tree in the wilderness, and praying to die: "It is enough! Now, Lord, take my life, for I am no better than my fathers!" (1 Kings 19:4, NKJV).

This week's lesson opens with that whiplash on purpose. Today's study asks us to sit with James's startling description of the great prophet: "Elijah was a man with a nature like ours" (James 5:17, NKJV). A nature like ours. The man through whom God did one of the Old Testament's most astonishing miracles got so low that he begged God to end his life.

Listen to what he says at Horeb: "I have been very zealous for the Lord God of hosts; for the children of Israel have forsaken Your covenant... I alone am left; and they seek to take my life" (1 Kings 19:10, NKJV). Notice what the lament is actually about — not just fear, though he was afraid. It reads like the grief of a man who believes his ministry has failed. Apostasy remained despite his best efforts, as if he alone were responsible for holding back the tide. If you have ever done everything you knew to do and watched it seem to come to nothing, you have already prayed this prayer.

And notice what God does not do. He does not lecture. He does not open with rebuke. First comes sleep. Then bread and water — twice. Only after Elijah is rested and fed does the Lord lead him to Horeb, where the whirlwind and the earthquake and the fire all give way to a still small voice — and a quiet correction of the math: you are not alone, Elijah; there are seven thousand whose knees have never bowed to Baal (1 Kings 19:18). Then comes work again — not the work Elijah would have chosen, but work that would outlast him: kings to anoint, a successor to train.

That is the pattern worth memorizing. God meets exhausted servants with tenderness first and assignments second — and the work never depended on one frail person to begin with.

Ellen White wrote, *"God never leads His children otherwise than they would choose to be led, if they could see the end from the beginning."*—*The Ministry of Healing*, p. 479. Elijah under the juniper tree could not see the end from the beginning. He could only see the burned altars and the empty crowd. God saw the day this depressed, exhausted man would stand on another mountain, in glory, talking with Jesus (Matthew 17:3).

Where has discouragement been doing your math for you? The prophet's humanity is the point — the same God who fed Elijah before He corrected him is not asking you to be stronger than Elijah was, only to let Him finish what He started.
