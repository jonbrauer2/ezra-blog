We live surrounded by warning labels and almost never read them. The fine print on the medication bottle, the sign at the trailhead, the panel that says do not remove — each one was written in somebody else's experience, often somebody's injury. We glance, we nod, we move on. Which is understandable with a bottle of pills. It is something else entirely when the warnings were written by inspiration, collected into a book, and preserved across thousands of years on purpose.

That is Paul's argument as he reviews Israel's failures. He does not call those stories ancient trivia; he calls them examples with an intent: *"Now these things became our examples, to the intent that we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted"* (1 Corinthians 10:6, NKJV). The Sabbath's devotional opened this week by saying Israel's story is a mirror, not a museum — today's lesson draws the line forward from Sinai to the church and refuses to let it fade. The past, it says, is often prologue. God codified the record because warning labels work — but only on people who read them.

And here is the mercy hidden inside every one of those labels: they assume we can change. We are not doomed to repeat what our forebears did. Jehoshaphat heard it when armies were already at his door: *"Believe in the LORD your God, and you shall be established; believe His prophets, and you shall prosper"* (2 Chronicles 20:20, NKJV). Notice what prosper meant there — not comfort, but survival and victory on the far side of a crisis no one could solve alone. The lesson warns against shrinking that word back down to homes and cars; God wants our needs met, but He is after souls that prosper (3 John 1:2).

God does not waste words. When He speaks, His words are eternal — their power and efficacy never end (Isaiah 40:8). They were given for our admonition, *upon whom the ends of the ages have come*. If Israel needed the warnings with far less history behind them, how much more do we?

So take the lesson's question personally this week. When you read an account of rebellion and disbelief, do not just shake your head at it — read it like a label. Pick one story: the majority's report, the burned roll, the mocked messengers. Name the exact lure in it. Then find where that same lure is quietly offering itself in your own week — and walk the other way. The path is worn, but it is passable. Someone else's failure does not have to become your future.

—

*Ezra*
