Job's losses come in waves. First: a messenger arrives to say his oxen and donkeys have been taken and the servants killed. Before he finishes speaking, a second messenger arrives. Then a third. Then a fourth. Fire, wind, death — everything is gone before the day is over. His children. His livestock. His workers. His wealth.

Then his health.

And then his three friends arrive — and spend the rest of the book attempting to convince him that this much suffering must mean this much sin.

They were wrong. The text tells us from the beginning that Job was blameless and upright, that God had allowed the suffering, and that the friends' theology — suffering equals punishment equals find the sin — was catastrophically mistaken. Yet it is, if we are honest, the most instinctive theology in the world. We apply it to others whenever their pain makes us uncomfortable. Sometimes we apply it to ourselves.

What Job refuses to do, through all of it, is agree with his friends' theory. He doesn't understand what is happening. He is not at peace. He is in genuine anguish, arguing with God across the book's long middle — not in defiance, but in the raw honesty of a man who refuses to pretend he's okay when he isn't.

And then, from the middle of his worst darkness, he says something extraordinary:

*"Oh, that my words were written! Oh, that they were inscribed in a book!... For I know that my Redeemer lives, and He shall stand at last on the earth... in my flesh I shall see God, whom I shall see for myself."* (Job 19:23-27, NKJV)

He did not understand what was happening. He could not see where it was going. He could not find God when he looked (Job 23:8-9). But underneath the incomprehension and the pain, there was a bedrock: *I know that my Redeemer lives.* Not I feel it. Not I hope it. *I know.*

The friends explained. Job endured. And God answered Job from the whirlwind, not with an explanation, but with a presence.

Sometimes the suffering is not explained. Sometimes the presence is the answer.

—

*Ezra*
