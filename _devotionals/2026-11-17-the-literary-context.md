Yesterday we watched a single prophecy carry two horizons at once. Today the lesson exposes the opposite failure — what happens when a verse is torn out of every horizon at all.

A popular preacher's anecdote tells of a man desperate for God's will who flips his Bible open and stabs a finger at the page. Matthew 27:5 — "And [Judas] went and hanged himself" (NKJV). Alarmed, he tries again: Luke 10:37 — "Go and do likewise." Both verses are true. Read that way, back to back, they are lethal. The anecdote is comic; the principle underneath it is not. Isolate a text from its literary context and you have opened Scripture to "all kinds of artificial interpretations" — and some of those interpretations are old enough to talk.

This was the enemy's opening move. In Eden, Satan did not deny God's word; he quoted it — or nearly. "Has God indeed said, 'You shall not eat of every tree of the garden'?" (Genesis 3:1, NKJV). He wrested the words from their original context, generalized them, and then supplied his own interpretation. Eve was not overcome by a lack of Scripture; she was overcome by Scripture bent. Some distort the Bible sincerely, out of ignorance. The serpent's motive was never innocent — and either way, the result is always detrimental.

He ran the same play on Christ — with the Bible in his hand. On the temple's pinnacle he quoted Psalm 91:11, 12, a true psalm of protection, twisted to imply that God guarantees rescue for those who deliberately fling themselves into danger. "Satan came as an angel of light in the wilderness of temptation to deceive Christ."—Ellen G. White, in *The Advent Review and Sabbath Herald,* December 18, 1888. And Christ's answer was not a new revelation but an old one, rightly read: "It is written" — Scripture in its context, wielded the way its Author intended.

Here is the sobering part. The difference between the tempter and the Savior was never whether Scripture is true. Both quoted it. The difference was context — a verse severed from the Book that carries it becomes a lie even when every word in it is accurate. The great controversy is fought not only over whether the Bible is God's Word, but over how it will be read. And the most dangerous misreadings are the ones we commit in service of conclusions we already loved.

So the discipline is not cleverness; it is whole reading. Read the verse with its chapter, the chapter with its book, the book with its Author.

The lesson's closing question is worth keeping in front of us all day: why must we resist pulling texts out of context to prove our points — no matter how correct our points are? Because a proof built on a torn verse is built on the serpent's method, not the Savior's.
