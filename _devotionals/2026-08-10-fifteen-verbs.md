Paul does not define love with an adjective. He does not offer a philosophical formula. He gives fifteen verbs — actions, choices, deliberate motions of a will. Love is not what you feel. Love is what you do.

*"Love suffers long and is kind; love does not envy; love does not parade itself, is not puffed up; does not behave rudely, does not seek its own, is not provoked, thinks no evil; does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth; bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things."* (1 Corinthians 13:4-7, NKJV)

Read it slowly. Notice the shape. The list begins with patience and kindness — the two verbs that describe how love holds itself under pressure and how love expresses itself toward the person creating the pressure. Then a run of what love does not do: envy, parade, puff, behave rudely, seek its own, get provoked, keep a ledger, celebrate wrongdoing. And then the closing quartet: bears, believes, hopes, endures.

That closing matters. Paul does not end with love's happiness. He ends with love's resilience. Bearing, believing, hoping, enduring — the verbs of a love that has been tested and has not broken. Love's signature is not sentiment. It is endurance under conditions that would justify quitting.

Which changes what love means, and it changes it in a very freeing direction. If love were primarily a feeling, then you would only be able to love the people who evoked the right emotion in you. But if love is a set of verbs, then love becomes possible with everyone the Spirit has put in your life — including the ones who make no emotional deposit, including the ones who withdraw more than they deposit, including the ones you would not have chosen. You can be patient with a person you do not enjoy. You can be kind to someone whose company drains you. You can refuse to keep a ledger of wrongs even when the ledger is very full. Every verb on Paul's list is available to a will that has been submitted to the Spirit, and none of them require the emotional accompaniment as a prerequisite.

Galatians reads like a commentary on this passage: *"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control."* (Galatians 5:22-23, NKJV) One fruit, nine flavors. You cannot have genuine patience without kindness. You cannot have kindness without goodness. They grow from the same Spirit-rooted life, and you cannot pick one from the branch while leaving the others behind.

Ellen White wrote that "the religion of Christ means more than the forgiveness of sin; it means taking away our sins, and filling the vacuum with the graces of the Holy Spirit." That is what fifteen verbs describe — the graces filling the vacuum where the old self used to live.

Pick one verb. Just one. Not fifteen, not five, not even two — one. And pick one specific person. This week, in that relationship, practice that verb. Not as aspiration. As action. That is how the more excellent way stops being a Bible chapter and starts being a life.
