Paul makes the entire incarnation into an economic sentence. Before he asks the Corinthians for a single coin, he holds before them a man who gave a universe.

*"For you know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that you through His poverty might become rich."* (2 Corinthians 8:9, NKJV)

The words are so familiar they can slide past without landing. But sit for a moment on the arithmetic. "Rich" means the pre-existent Son sharing the full glory of the Father — the One of whom Jesus prayed, *"the glory which I had with You before the world was"* (John 17:5, NKJV). "Poor" means the manger. It means Nazareth. It means the road-worn Rabbi who told a would-be follower, *"Foxes have holes and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head"* (Luke 9:58, NKJV). The God of the universe with less housing than a fox.

Between those two words — "rich" and "poor" — is the entire descent of Philippians 2. He *"made Himself of no reputation... humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross"* (Philippians 2:7-8, NKJV). Every step down was voluntary. Every step down had you in view.

And then the purpose clause. Paul does not stop with the descent; he tells you why. *"That you through His poverty might become rich."* The poverty was never the point. Your enrichment was. He did not empty Himself for the sake of the emptying. He emptied Himself for the sake of filling you.

This is the ground under all Christian generosity. Paul is not about to guilt the Corinthians into an offering. He is holding up the Cross and letting the Cross do its own work. Generosity, in Paul's mind, is not a religious add-on. It is the grammar of the gospel — a small echo of the enormous sentence Christ spoke when He entered our poverty.

*Ellen White* writes: *"Those whose hearts are filled with the love of Christ, will follow the example of Him who for our sake became poor, that through His poverty we might be made rich."* The generosity does not have to be manufactured. It follows love the way warmth follows fire.

When you consider what Christ actually gave up to reach you, what is the natural response of your heart? Not the "should" response — the actual one. Follow that.
