Paul builds the most impressive spiritual resume he can imagine — angelic tongues, prophetic insight, mountain-moving faith, sacrificial giving, martyrdom itself — and then walks up to the whole thing and pulls the plug. Without love, he says, all of it becomes noise.

*"Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I have become sounding brass or a clanging cymbal."* (1 Corinthians 13:1, NKJV)

The image is deliberate. In pagan worship, temples clanged bronze cymbals to summon the gods, to invoke ecstasy, to fill silence with religious racket. Paul takes the most spiritual-sounding gift the Corinthians prized — glossolalia — and drops it into that pagan soundscape. Without love, he says, this is what it becomes: empty noise, indistinguishable from the religion of any other altar. The critique lands the harder because it comes from Paul, who himself spoke in tongues more than any of them.

Then three "nothings" in three verses. *"I am nothing... it profits me nothing."* Prophecy plus knowledge plus faith without love — nothing. Give all your goods to the poor, burn at the stake, without love — nothing. The spiritual life without love is not merely diminished. It is voided. There is no partial credit for impressive gifts absent the atmosphere in which they were meant to operate.

This should terrify anyone who has been in ministry long enough to have accumulated a few credentials. It is possible to preach true sermons, teach accurate Sabbath School lessons, give generously, and administer competently — and be, in Paul's accounting, nothing. Because the credentials were never the currency. Love was.

Jesus said the same thing in a different key: *"And because lawlessness will abound, the love of many will grow cold."* (Matthew 24:12, NKJV) He was describing the end. Not a sudden loss of doctrine, not a dramatic apostasy. A quiet chill. A gradual cooling. The kind of temperature drop you don't notice at first because you are still moving, still praying, still attending, still functioning. But the warmth is going out of it.

Ellen White wrote that "the truth as it is in Jesus can be experienced, but never explained. Its height and breadth and depth surpass our knowledge... It is a warmth of love that penetrates the whole being." When the warmth thins, everything else keeps working — that is the strange danger. The machinery of a religious life can continue running long after the love has drained out of it.

Which is why the question today is uncomfortably specific. What form of your faith could be dismantled and replaced with empty noise if the love were quietly drained from it — and would anyone notice the difference? Your prayer life? Your Bible study? Your service? Your parenting? Your conversation with people you disagree with?

Where has your love grown just a little cold this year?

Turn the flame back up. There is no substitute.

—

*Ezra*
