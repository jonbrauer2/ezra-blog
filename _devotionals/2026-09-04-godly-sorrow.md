There are two kinds of sorrow after we do wrong, and they look very similar from the outside. Both include tears. Both include self-recrimination. Both include the word *sorry.* But they go in different directions.

Paul draws the line. *"Godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death."* (2 Corinthians 7:10, NKJV) One moves forward into repentance and life. The other circles back to itself and quietly rots.

Worldly sorrow is sorrow over consequences. Sorrow over being caught. Sorrow over damaged reputation, exposed weakness, loss of standing. It orbits the self. It asks: how do I look now? And when the pressure eases, it forgets what it was so upset about. It leaves you the same person, only more careful next time to not be seen.

Godly sorrow is sorrow over the thing itself. Sorrow over the rupture with God, over the harm done to another, over the fact that you have grieved the Spirit that loves you. It looks past the consequences to the actual injury. It does not primarily ask *what will people think?* It asks *what have I done, and how do I turn?* And because it is honest, it moves. It does not stay in the shame. It walks into repentance and out again the other side, changed.

Paul watches it happen in the Corinthians and marvels. *"For observe this very thing, that you sorrowed in a godly manner: what diligence it produced in you, what clearing of yourselves, what indignation, what fear, what vehement desire, what zeal, what vindication!"* (2 Corinthians 7:11, NKJV) A whole-body turning. Not a passive despair. An active, moving repentance that reordered how they lived.

The door into that kind of sorrow is not shame. Shame closes doors. Paul says elsewhere what actually opens the door. *"Do you despise the riches of His goodness, forbearance, and longsuffering, not knowing that the goodness of God leads you to repentance?"* (Romans 2:4, NKJV) Kindness leads you in. Not condemnation. The realization that the God you have wronged is still holding the door open for you.

David wrote from inside godly sorrow after his great failure. *"The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and a contrite heart — these, O God, You will not despise."* (Psalm 51:17, NKJV) Not the sacrifice of a performance. The sacrifice of an actually broken heart, which turned out to be what God had wanted all along.

Ellen White writes: *"Genuine repentance springs from a sense of the offensive character of sin. Godly sorrow is experienced when the heart is contrite because of unfaithfulness to a Saviour so pitiful and so full of love."*

When you last sinned and felt sorrow, was it closer to grief over the rupture or fear of the consequence? What would godly sorrow — moving forward without regret — look like today?
