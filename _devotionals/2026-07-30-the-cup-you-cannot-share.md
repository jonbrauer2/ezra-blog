The Corinthians had convinced themselves the idol feast was just food. Paul agreed that the idol was nothing. And then he said the participation was still fatal — because worship is not about the object; it is about the allegiance.

*"The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ?"* (1 Corinthians 10:16, NKJV) The Greek word is *koinōnia* — fellowship, communion, shared participation. When you drink the cup, you are not performing a symbolic gesture. You are entering a bond. The act creates the allegiance it enacts.

Which is why Paul draws the line where he does: *"You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot partake of the Lord's table and of the table of demons."* (1 Corinthians 10:21, NKJV) The verb is not "should not." It is *ou dynasthe* — you are not able. The exclusivity is ontological, not disciplinary. Two altars in one heart is not double devotion; it is the annihilation of both.

Isaiah heard the same refusal from the mouth of God centuries earlier: *"I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images."* (Isaiah 42:8, NKJV) People sometimes read that as jealousy in the small sense — a possessive God unwilling to share the spotlight. It is not. It is ontology. Only God is worthy of absolute allegiance because only God is God. To give His weight to something less is not generosity; it is a category error that damages the worshiper more than it insults the worshiped.

Ellen White wrote that "by idolatry Paul meant not only the worship of idols, but self-serving, love of ease, the gratification of appetite and passion." The idols of our century rarely require a temple. They require the same thing they have always required: a divided heart with two cups on the table and the pretense that we can raise them both.

Every worship is exclusive by nature. The Sabbath is not one form of worship among many; it is the sign of an undivided allegiance. The Communion cup is not one meal among many; it is a covenant meal that forbids the other table.

Somewhere in your life a second cup has crept back onto the table. Not necessarily a shrine or a temple — more likely a habit, an appetite, a relationship, a use of time that has begun quietly to hold what only God should hold. Name it. The Lord's cup will not share the table with it, and He is inviting you to His.

Which cup will you set down before the sun sets tonight?
