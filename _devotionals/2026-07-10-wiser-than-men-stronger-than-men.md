The sentence is comparative in form, but the claim is absolute. God's foolishness is not slightly wiser than human wisdom. It is different in kind.

*"Because the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men."* (1 Corinthians 1:25, NKJV) The rhetorical shape almost trips you: the words *foolishness* and *weakness* placed next to God, as though Paul is willing to speak of Him in the very categories the world uses to insult Him. And then the reversal — the "foolishness" is wisdom, the "weakness" is strength.

Where do you see this? Gethsemane. A man in a garden at night, face on the ground, sweating drops of blood. *"Not as I will, but as You will."* (Matthew 26:39, NKJV) The strongest act in human history looked, from every visible angle, like surrender. He was arrested by torchlight. He did not resist. He did not summon legions. The world saw a defeat. Heaven saw the pivot on which the universe turned.

Paul puts the pattern into hymn form: *"He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."* (Philippians 2:8, NKJV) The descent is the demonstration. What looks like defeat is coronation. What looks like foolishness is the deepest wisdom the universe has ever seen.

Jesus Himself gave us the image most directly: *"unless a grain of wheat falls into the ground and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it produces much grain."* (John 12:24, NKJV) The grain that refuses to fall stays a grain. That is all. It keeps its shape, its solitude, its intact self — and it produces nothing. The grain that falls loses everything a grain values. And multiplies.

There is a grain-of-wheat pattern to real fruitfulness. It is not the pattern of the world, which teaches accumulation, protection, self-promotion, resistance. It is a pattern of letting go — of the reputation you were curating, the score you were keeping, the outcome you were engineering, the version of yourself you were trying to preserve.

*Ellen White* writes: *"To live for self is to perish. Covetousness, the desire of benefit for self's sake, cuts the soul off from life."* (Christ's Object Lessons, p. 258) The refusal to fall is not neutrality. It is a slow death disguised as safety.

Where in your life are you clinging to being alone, when the invitation is to fall into fruitfulness? What one grain, today, might you let go?

*Ezra*
