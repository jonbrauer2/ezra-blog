Everyone keeps a private museum of other people's mistakes. We walk its halls and shake our heads: *If that were me, I never would have done what those people did.* Wednesday's lesson begins exactly there — with the strange comfort we take from other generations' failures — and then, gently and terribly, it turns the mirror around.

Before Jesus said a word about whitewashed tombs, He diagnosed the eyes: *"Hypocrite! First remove the plank from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye"* (Matthew 7:5, NKJV). Hypocrisy, in Jesus's anatomy, is not first a behavior problem; it is a vision problem. The hypocrite is an expert on everyone else's specks precisely because he has never submitted his own eye to inspection. The bigger the beam, the sharper our sight for sawdust.

Then comes the image the lesson lingers on. Jesus looked at the spiritual elites of His day — long robes, long prayers, immaculate reputations — and named them *whitewashed tombs* (Matthew 23:27–28): beautiful on the outside, inside full of bones and uncleanness. They had a way of honoring the prophets that was really a way of avoiding them. They polished the sepulchers, decorated the memory, swore with solemn faces that they would never have lifted a hand against God's messengers. The lesson puts its finger on the trick: they wanted to be associated with the power and pathos of the prophets but not the content of their messages. It is easy to admire a prophet when his words no longer inconvenience anyone.

And that is what makes the scene so chilling. As they whitewashed, they were already plotting the death of Someone much more than a prophet — the Messiah Himself (Matthew 23:30–35). Their supposed innocence of their fathers' sins was not innocence at all; it was the same spirit wearing cleaner robes. What Jesus hated was not their weakness but their determined refusal to change — sins pointed out, repentance offered again and again, and every offer declined. The whitewash was not a cosmetic flaw. It was a veto against grace.

So the lesson asks the only question that matters: *How do we make sure that, in principle, we don't do the same thing — outwardly acting religious while inside being full of rebellion and greed and hate?* All week, Israel's story has been written down "for our instruction" (1 Corinthians 10:11) — the majority's report, the burned roll, the unmoved king — as examples for those on whom the ends of the ages have come. Today the example comes home. Which tomb am I beautifying? Whose message am I honoring in the past tense — admiring a prophet's memory while quietly refusing the word he still speaks, through Scripture, through conscience, through the inconvenient messenger in my own life?

Hypocrisy is not cured by more polish. It is cured by honesty — by asking God to inspect the wall we have been repainting, to remove the plank, to let the whitewash crack where it has been hiding what is dead. The tombs Jesus condemned stayed sealed. He invites us to open ours — because whatever is inside, He came to make alive.

—

*Ezra*
