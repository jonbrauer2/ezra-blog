Yesterday we watched heaven pass its message down a chain of messengers all the way to Patmos. But today's lesson starts even earlier — because long before a prophet ever lifted a pen, God had already published. David looked up and saw the printing press: *"The heavens declare the glory of God; and the firmament shows His handiwork"* (Psalm 19:1, NKJV). Paul pressed the point further — God's eternal power and divine nature are "clearly perceived... understood by what has been made" (Romans 1:20, NASB). Theologians call it *general* revelation: a word spoken to everyone, everywhere, in a language that requires no translation.

And notice *why* He speaks at all. We are fallen people in a wounded world, facing death, suffering, and trials — and God refuses to let us guess about any of it. He speaks because He wants us to know why we are here, why things are so badly broken, and why, despite it all, we can hope in His love. Revelation is not a God broadcasting His own glory into the void. It is a Father making sure His hurting children are not left to figure things out alone.

Look closely at the fabric of the world and you will find mercy woven into its very mechanics. *"God's healing power runs all through nature. If a tree is cut, if a human being is wounded or breaks a bone, nature begins at once to repair the injury. Even before the need exists, the healing agencies are in readiness; and as soon as a part is wounded, every energy is bent to the work of restoration."*—Ellen G. White, *Education*, p. 113. Restoration is not an afterthought bolted onto nature. It is a signature. The repair crews were staffed before the accident — that is the punctuation of a God who intended healing from the beginning.

And yet the book of nature is a strange one to read alone. Since Eden (Genesis 3:14–19), the same field holds wheat and tares (Matthew 13:24–30). The sky that wrote the Milky Way also wrote cancer cells; the sea that dazzles at noon drowns fishermen; the loveliest places on earth bury villages. Beauty and brutality are bound in a single volume, and sin has scribbled across the pages. Read nature by itself and you end up with one of two errors: sentimental optimism, chanting "everything happens for a reason" over a scarred planet, or cold despair, concluding that power without love made the stars. The lesson states the remedy plainly: nature's ambiguous message "can be properly understood only in light of God's special revelation."

So God gave a second book — better, a second Word. The incarnate Word, Jesus Christ, and the Written Word that testifies of Him (John 5:39). Scripture is the inspired record of creation, fall, redemption, and restoration — always reliable, because in its pages it is God speaking (2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Peter 1:19–21). And at the cross, the two voices of nature finally make sense in one frame: the world is beloved enough to be died for, and broken enough to need it.

So read the whole Book, and you will read the world rightly. When tonight's sky comes out wearing every star, stand under it and say David's line. But then open the page the stars cannot give you. Nature will tell you God is great; only Scripture will tell you He is good — and that He is speaking, still, to you.
