Yesterday's devotional closed by saying that study "readies the vessel." Fair enough — but pause on the word *vessel*. Paul's chosen container for the gospel is not a golden reliquary or a crystal vial. It is a clay pot: cheap, common, and liable to crack. "But we have this treasure in earthen vessels, that the excellence of the power may be of God and not of us" (2 Corinthians 4:7, NKJV). The treasure is "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God" (2 Corinthians 4:6, NKJV) — and God deliberately stored it in pottery.

Walk down the prophets' row and look at the pots. Moses was a fugitive with a stutter who lost his temper at the rock. David's great sins are written in the pages of his own book. Jonah bought a ticket in the opposite direction from his calling. Peter denied his Lord three times in the high priest's courtyard. God did not preserve His messengers from failure, and He did not airbrush their failures out of the record either. The Bible reads like an honest book because it is one.

The principle reaches from the messengers to their writings. Their words were not exempt from "the imperfections of human understanding and communication." A handful of verses are hard to harmonize — so few, the lesson's Thursday thought observes, that it takes real determination to stumble over them. Some are copyists' slips from centuries of hand-copying; some trace to the court records and sources the inspired writers used. And the first response is not alarm but yesterday's homework: ask whether further study might resolve it.

Monday's devotional paused on the union of the divine and the human in Scripture. Thursday's lesson presses that same truth down into the pots themselves: "The union of the divine and the human, manifest in Christ, exists also in the Bible. The truths revealed are all 'given by inspiration of God;' yet they are expressed in the words of men and are adapted to human needs."—Ellen G. White, *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 5, p. 747.

Why clay and not angels? Because if sinless messengers had carried the message, some glory would have stuck to the carrier. Cracked pots put the light on display: the power visibly belongs to the treasure, not the container. That gives us two things. First, confidence — the essence of the message does not stand or fall on a copyist's slip, and a verse I cannot yet harmonize does not unravel a Book whose center is Christ. Second, calling — the plainest person can carry this treasure, and your cracks do not disqualify you. They are often the very places the light gets out.

So when a hard verse trips you this week, don't look away and don't panic. Study it honestly; if no answer comes, let the essence carry you — the treasure has survived earthenware before. And hear the comfort under the doctrine: the Lord who trusted His written word to cracked pots trusts His living word to you.

*Ezra*
