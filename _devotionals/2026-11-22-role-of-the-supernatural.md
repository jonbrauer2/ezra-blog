The modern mind is embarrassed by the supernatural. We can tolerate a prophet as a good writer, a brave reformer, a sharp social critic — categories our century already respects. But visions? Dreams? A message arriving from outside the speaker? That is where the credibility stakes sit, and the Bible does not apologize for them. At Hazeroth the Lord said of the prophetic gift: *"Hear now My words: If there is a prophet among you, I, the Lord, make Myself known to him in a vision; I speak to him in a dream"* (Numbers 12:6, NKJV).

October's devotional read that verse as a lesson in method — how heaven speaks. Today's lesson reads it as a claim: the prophetic gift is supernatural at its root, a direct communication from God that goes beyond any mere impression or idea. And the claim carries a range. Daniel saw by night vision (Daniel 8:1). Isaiah's book opens on "the vision... which he saw concerning Judah and Jerusalem" (Isaiah 1:1). And in Damascus, heaven intercepted not an apostle but "a certain disciple at Damascus named Ananias" (Acts 9:10) — no title, no office, no famous name, just a disciple given a vision that sent him to Saul. The medium is God's choice; the authority is God's, never the messenger's personality.

But the supernatural is the medium, not the test. Saturday's devotional watched Daniel collapse when the vision came — strength gone — while his companions, who saw only the fringes of it, fled in terror. Physical phenomena marked real contact with the Divine. Yet Deuteronomy 13 already made the decisive point: a wonder can come true and the prophet still be false, because the enemy of God can also demonstrate the supernatural. So the lesson states the caution plainly: no single piece of evidence confirms a prophetic ministry. Supernatural manifestations are not the test of the true gift — all the biblical tests must be present.

Like the diamond. Yesterday's heat-and-water test proved nothing on its own; a stone proves itself by passing several. That is why this week keeps its bench loaded — and why Jesus' warning targets the disguise: wolves in sheep's clothing (Matthew 7:15). Sheep's clothing works precisely because it looks like what the flock already trusts. Impressive is not the same as authentic; the fleece is what sells.

So the question is not whether heaven speaks supernaturally — Scripture says that is how prophecy arrives. The question is what we do with a voice that claims to have spoken for Him: hold it to the tests, all of them, before trusting it with our love, our loyalty, or our Sabbath morning. Where are you most tempted to accept a voice because it dazzles rather than because it passes the bench?
