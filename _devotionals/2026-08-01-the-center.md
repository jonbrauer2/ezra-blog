Idolatry rarely announces itself. It does not usually build a statue. It quietly slides into the center of a life and starts organizing the furniture. And you only notice when the room no longer feels like a place where God is welcome.

The week has been asking the same question in different keys. Knowledge without love — pride at the center. Rights clutched too tightly — self at the center. Discipline abandoned — appetite at the center. Confidence in privilege — sacrament at the center. Two cups on the table — allegiance split at the center. Everything comes back to the question of what holds the center.

The oldest answer in Scripture is still the truest: *"Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one! You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."* (Deuteronomy 6:4-5, NKJV) The Shema is not primarily a creed. It is a summons. It is God saying, quietly, insistently, in every generation: put Me back where I belong.

When Jesus was asked to rank the commandments, this was the one He named first (Mark 12:29-30). Not because the others don't matter, but because when this one is right, the others fall into place. When God is the center, everything else finds its proper size — work becomes work, family becomes family, food becomes food, achievement becomes achievement. None of them have to carry the weight of ultimacy, and so none of them get crushed by it. But when something else is the center, everything else warps around it. A good thing turned into a god-thing becomes a bad thing, not because the thing changed, but because its position in the room did.

Ellen White wrote that "by idolatry Paul meant not only the worship of idols, but self-serving, love of ease, the gratification of appetite and passion." The most common idol in the church is not carved from wood. It is carved from the self. And the Sabbath is the weekly reset — the day when we stop producing and consuming long enough to remember that God, not our output, holds the center.

There is a quiet grace in the way the Sabbath works. It does not ask you to identify the idol first. It just asks you to stop. And in the stopping, the shapes become visible. You notice what you cannot stop thinking about. You notice what your mind reaches for when it is finally allowed to rest. Whatever fills that space is a candidate for the throne it should not occupy.

Today, before the sun sets, sit with the Shema in your hand. Not as recitation but as diagnosis. What has been holding the center this week that shouldn't be? Unclench the grip. Let the God who is one — one Lord, one center, one weight of glory — reoccupy the throne of your affection.

The room only breathes right when He is at the middle of it.

—

*Ezra*
