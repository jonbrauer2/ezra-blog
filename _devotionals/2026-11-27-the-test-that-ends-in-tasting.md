A diamond can survive a jeweler's whole workshop — the scratch test, the refractometer, the drop in cold water. It can pass everything and still tell you nothing at dinner, because a diamond has no taste. It is the hardest natural thing on earth and, literally, flavorless. Six days this week, the lesson has laid prophetic claims on a bench of tests — the heart, the supernatural, harmony with Scripture, the confession about Christ, the fruit, the fulfilled word. Friday's reading adds no seventh instrument. It changes rooms. The bench certifies the gem; the psalmist sets the table.

Fridays this quarter have walked beside Ellen White — her call, her disappointment, her books. Today's lesson keeps the appointment with a different task: it reviews the arguments early Sabbath-keeping Adventists built to defend her prophetic gift after the first December 1844 vision drew fire. First, the theological case for perpetuity — the Spirit poured out on all flesh (Joel 2), the remnant keeping God's commandments and holding the testimony of Jesus (Revelation 12:17), the gift visible in the last days as an identifying mark of God's end-time people. October's devotional quoted those verses as the Spirit's résumé; today's lesson reads them as identity papers for a people. Second, the fruit — her gift bore "good fruit," unifying believers in the late-1840s Sabbath Conferences when they disagreed on doctrine; the bench's fruit test, satisfied. Third, the supernatural — visions that reported facts unknown to her, strength beyond nature, timing she could not control. Arguments, and sound ones.

Then the lesson does something quieter than any argument. It quotes a psalm: *"Oh, taste and see that the LORD is good; Blessed is the man who trusts in Him!"* (Psalm 34:8, NKJV). Not "argue and see." Not "audit and see." Taste. Every test this week guarded the listener against a false voice; not one of them can tell you the true voice is good. A court can authenticate a letter; only reading it tells you what the Writer is like. The most powerful "proof" of the prophetic gift, the lesson says plainly, is personal experience with the writings themselves — a case no one else can argue for you.

It suggests where to begin: the small, most-read book, *Steps to Christ* — the one October's devotional found framed at both ends by "God is love" — and then the five Conflict of the Ages volumes that trace the controversy from Eden lost to Eden restored. And the manner matters as much as the menu: prayerfully read. That phrase is the seventh test — reading with the heart's door open, expecting the Writer to answer for Himself.

So the week ends where every honest test should: at a table. The fence of tests exists to make the feast safe to approach. Whatever voice you have spent six days examining, take the last step the psalmist prescribes — read it on your knees, and see. The bench can tell you the gem is real. Only tasting will tell you it is good.

What will you taste this Sabbath — and have you brought your Bible to the table?
