There is a kind of theological correctness that leaves the room colder than it found it. You can be right about God and wrong about the person sitting across from you, and Paul says the church has to notice.

The Corinthians had it worked out: idols are nothing, meat is meat, and the mature believer is free to eat what he pleases. On the doctrine, Paul agreed with them. On the temperament, he did not. *"Knowledge puffs up, but love edifies."* (1 Corinthians 8:1, NKJV) The Greek verb Paul uses for "puffs up" is *physioi* — inflated, ballooned outward. It is the posture of someone who has grown large in his own estimation. Love, by contrast, does the opposite: it builds up — *oikodomeō*, the word for construction. Pride pushes outward from a hollow center. Love gathers, houses, roofs, shelters.

Then Paul lands a sentence that ought to hang in every classroom and every pulpit: *"If anyone thinks that he knows anything, he knows nothing yet as he ought to know."* (1 Corinthians 8:2, NKJV) The moment your knowledge stops feeling provisional is the moment it has begun to lie to you. Real learning always keeps an edge of humility because it is always brushing up against how much remains unknown.

The next verse turns the whole subject on its head: *"But if anyone loves God, this one is known by Him."* (1 Corinthians 8:3, NKJV) The passive voice matters. Assurance does not rest on the tightness of your grip on Him. It rests on the tightness of His grip on you. You are known. That changes what knowledge is for.

Ellen White wrote that "the strongest argument in favor of the gospel is a loving and lovable Christian." Doctrine that leaves people less loved has not done its job, no matter how sound the syllogisms. Theology is not neutral information; it is meant to build the church.

Somewhere in your life today is a conversation where you are technically correct and pastorally reckless. Someone who is wrong about a matter you happen to be right about — and whose faith is nonetheless fragile, or new, or wounded. Hold your conviction the way a nurse holds a scalpel: precisely, but always for the patient's healing, never for your own display.

Where has being right been making you less useful?

—

*Ezra*
