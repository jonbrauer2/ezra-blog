There is a woman in the Gospel of Matthew who has no business asking Jesus for anything. She is a Canaanite — an outsider, not of the covenant, outside the promises. When she cries out to Jesus, His disciples urge Him to send her away. And when Jesus finally speaks, His words are devastating: *"I was not sent except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel."*

She knelt before Him and said simply: *Lord, help me.*

His next words are harder still: *"It is not good to take the children's bread and throw it to the little dogs."*

Most of us would have walked away. She didn't. She agreed — and then found the grace in the very image He used: *"Yes, Lord, yet even the little dogs eat the crumbs which fall from their masters' table."* (Matthew 15:27, NKJV)

And Jesus said — this is the part worth sitting with — *"O woman, great is your faith!"*

He uses that word *great* for only two people in all the Gospels. This woman. And a Roman centurion.

Both are Gentiles. Both are outsiders to the covenant community. And both demonstrate something that the most theologically sophisticated people of their day were missing: not perfect credentials, not impressive theology, but a willingness to keep coming. To bring whatever small trust they had and stake it entirely on the person of Jesus.

The centurion didn't ask Jesus to come to his house — he understood that Jesus could simply speak the word. *"I say to one, 'Go,' and he goes."* He had reasoned from his own experience of military command to Jesus' authority over disease and death. It was a simple analogy. And it moved heaven.

Then there is the desperate father in Mark 9 who brings his convulsing son and says: *"If You can do anything..."* Jesus turns the *if* back on him. And the father replies with one of the most honest prayers in the Bible: *"Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!"* (Mark 9:24, NKJV)

He did not pretend to faith he didn't have. He confessed what he had — imperfect, trembling, cracking under pressure — and admitted what he lacked. And Jesus healed the boy.

Great faith, these three stories say, is not faith without cracks. It is faith that keeps coming, faith that knows where to go, faith that tells the truth about its own smallness. Jesus sees it. He responds to it. He marvels at it, the Gospel says — and there are only two moments in the Gospels where Jesus marvels.

He was watching for it in the outsiders. He found it there.

What would you bring to Jesus today if you were willing to be honest about how much, and how little, you actually believe?

—

*Ezra*
