The strangest credential in Scripture belongs to Balaam: a prophet for hire, standing on a hillside with a king's payment in view, commanded to curse Israel — and able only to bless. God can put a true word even in a rented mouth. But listen to what the word says, because it is the ground under every other test this week holds: *"God is not a man, that He should lie, nor a son of man, that He should repent. Has He said, and will He not do? Or has He spoken, and will He not make it good?"* (Numbers 23:19, NKJV).

That verse is the reason harmony matters. If God neither lies nor changes, then His word spoken in one century cannot contradict His word spoken in another — not because consistency is a literary style, but because consistency is the trace of a truthful Character. The standard above the messenger stands on the character behind the message.

That is what the lesson means by harmony with the Bible. Isaiah said it first: to the law and to the testimony — if a voice does not speak according to this word, there is no light in it. A true prophet never contradicts Scripture, never minimizes it, never subtly draws attention away from it. Peter called the prophetic word "more sure" than a voice from heaven — October's devotional quoted him on that — and Paul adds that what was written before was written for our learning. James supplies the character note: with God there is no variableness, no shadow of turning.

And Scripture models the harmony it demands. The first prophecy — spoken by God Himself in Eden, in Genesis 3:15 — sketched the whole plan of salvation in a sentence. The sacrificial system embodied it. Then, step by step, prophecy clarified it: a Lamb, a virgin's Son, a suffering Servant, a cross, a crown. Truth unfolded, generation by generation, each age understanding more than the last. But notice the direction of the deepening: new light extended the old light; it never overturned it. A later prophet could add clarity. None could say, "Forget what God said before."

So the bench this week gains its third test. Yesterday's devotional left the diamond on the bench and the tests stacked beside it; today's asks the simplest and most unforgiving question of the set: does the message harmonize with the Book? When a voice claims God has moved on — updated His standard, revised His word, outgrown His old self — something is wrong at the root. The light may be new; the Character behind it is not.

What voice near you claims that God has changed His mind? Hold it to the page. A true word will bend over the same Scripture you do.
