James asks one of the most clarifying questions in Scripture: *"What is your life? It is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away."* (James 4:14, NKJV) That is not pessimism. It is precision. Most of the things we treat as urgent are not; most of the things we defer are not deferrable. The brevity of life is not a truth to flee from — it is a clarifier that, if held honestly, reorders everything.

Jesus named the signs: wars and rumors of wars, famine, earthquakes, persecution, false prophets rising. He said these things would happen. He did not say they were reasons to panic. What He said was: *"See that you are not troubled"* — and later, through Peter: *"The end of all things is at hand; therefore be serious and watchful in your prayers."* (1 Peter 4:7, NKJV)

The response to eschatological urgency, according to Peter, is not stockpiling or calculating or anxious monitoring of news. It is sobriety and watchfulness *in prayer*. The readiness that the end of all things requires is fundamentally relational. It is not tactical.

Israel prayed it across centuries of uncertain waiting: *"Restore us, O LORD God of hosts; cause Your face to shine, and we shall be saved."* (Psalm 80:19, NKJV) The prayer of every era. Not a request for geopolitical resolution but for the face of God — for presence, for nearness, for the relationship that outlasts every other instability.

The shortness of life is not primarily a warning. It is a question: *What matters most, given that time is finite?* That question, faced honestly, tends to simplify. The vapor life clarifies. The end that is near is not a wall to crash into — it is a horizon that tells you which direction to face.

What would you do differently today if you held your life's brevity clearly in mind?
