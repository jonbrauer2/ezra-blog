Paul's opponents in Corinth mocked him. His letters, they said, were forceful, but the man himself was underwhelming — a poor speaker, an unimpressive presence, easy to dismiss in the room. *"His letters,' they say, 'are weighty and powerful, but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible.'"* (2 Corinthians 10:10, NKJV) They had mistaken his meekness for spinelessness. They were about to learn the difference.

Paul opens his defense with a curious phrase: *"I, Paul, myself am pleading with you by the meekness and gentleness of Christ."* (v. 1) He does not lead with authority. He leads with the tone of the Master. And then he lets them know what is coming. *"For though we walk in the flesh, we do not war according to the flesh. For the weapons of our warfare are not carnal but mighty in God for pulling down strongholds."* (v. 3-4, NKJV)

Meekness, in Christ, is not weakness. It is strength under control. Jesus said of Himself, *"I am gentle and lowly in heart"* (Matthew 11:29, NKJV), and He also drove the merchants from the temple with a whip of cords. The gentleness and the courage were not opposites in Him; they were the two hands of the same person, coordinated by love. The gentleness never became passivity. The courage never became cruelty. Both served the mission.

Paul's opponents thought he lacked backbone because he had refused to fight on their terms. They wanted rhetorical combat, political maneuvering, sharp elbows in the room. Paul had refused. Not because he could not — his letters showed he could — but because those were not his weapons. His weapons were the proclaimed Word, prayer, honesty, patience, and the strange readiness to suffer rather than dominate. And Paul was clear: those weapons were mighty in God. Not clever. Not persuasive by human standards. Mighty.

He describes the target with precision: *"casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God, bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ."* (v. 5, NKJV) The strongholds are not people. They are arguments — mental fortresses, spiritual systems, ideas that hold minds captive to something less than Christ. The goal is not to defeat opponents. The goal is to release the captives.

Whose gentleness have you mistaken for weakness — including your own? What would Christlike gentleness with clear conviction look like in the difficult conversation you have been avoiding?
