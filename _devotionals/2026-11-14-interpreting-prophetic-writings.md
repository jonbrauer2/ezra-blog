They said it on the road, to a Stranger they did not recognize: *“We were hoping that it was He who was going to redeem Israel”* (Luke 24:21, NKJV). Every word in that sentence is grief. Hoping — past tense. Israel — a kingdom they wanted back, not a Lamb they needed. The two disciples walking to Emmaus were not unbelievers; they believed the prophecies. They just believed half of them.

The lesson puts the diagnosis plainly: their false expectation “derived from a biased, one-sided interpretation of the Messianic prophecies.” They had gathered the crown texts — throne, victory, Rome thrown down — and left the suffering texts on the table. A messiah of their own assembly failed them exactly as it must. A few weeks ago this devotional met that same verse to greet the Prophet who was the *subject* of Moses; a new lesson begins at the same scene for a different reason — not who the Word is, but how it is read.

Notice what Jesus did not do. He gave no new revelation. He opened the old one: *“Beginning with Moses and with all the Prophets, He explained to them the things written about Himself in all the Scriptures”* (Luke 24:27, NASB). All the Scriptures — including the parts the disciples had skipped. And the skipped parts, it turns out, were carrying the fire: *“Did not our hearts burn within us while He talked with us on the road, while He opened to us the Scriptures?”* (Luke 24:32). Later, with the wider circle of disciples, “He opened their understanding, that they might comprehend the Scriptures” (Luke 24:45, NKJV). One-sided reading had produced downcast faces; whole reading produced burning hearts.

The lesson is not shy about the stakes: “A false understanding of prophecy can lead to spiritual disaster.” Last week asked whether we would hear the prophetic word. This week asks the question underneath it — whether we will read it *whole*. The disappointment on that road was not prophecy's failure. It was the reader's edit.

So take the prophecy you have read most — Daniel, Revelation, the Olivet discourse, whatever you return to — and this week read the parts you tend to skip. The verses we shelve are the ones that ambush our hopes. Christ's method still works: begin at Moses, keep going through all the Prophets, and refuse to stop at the texts that flatter us. The burning is waiting in the other half.
