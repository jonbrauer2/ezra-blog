We have the repentance story backwards. We think it goes: feel bad enough → decide to change → then maybe God will be willing to receive you. The sequence in Scripture is almost exactly reversed.

*"The goodness of God leads you to repentance."* (Romans 2:4, NKJV)

Not the severity. Not the ultimatum. The goodness. Which means repentance is not the thing that opens God's heart toward you — it is His open heart that produces repentance in you. Grace does not follow repentance as a reward. Grace precedes repentance as the cause.

Peter articulated this when he described what happened with the Gentiles: *"God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance leading to life."* (Acts 11:18, NKJV) Granted. Repentance was a gift the Gentiles received, not an achievement they produced. Something was done to them — in them — that made turning possible. The Spirit moved, the heart softened, the will turned. God's work, exercised by the human will.

This is not passive. The prodigal son did not sit in the pigpen and wait for his father to drag him home. He got up. He rehearsed the speech. He walked. But the whole getting-up was preceded by the memory of his father's house — *"how many of my father's hired servants have more than enough bread"* — and that memory was a gift he had not earned. Grace in the form of a persistent, accurate picture of what home was like.

And God's longsuffering is precisely this: the days of patience are doors held open. *"The Lord is not slack concerning His promise, as some count slackness, but is longsuffering toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance."* (2 Peter 3:9, NKJV) Every ordinary day is an extension of that invitation.

*"If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness."* (1 John 1:9, NKJV) The promise is unconditional. Not: if we confess with sufficient remorse. If we confess.

What has His goodness been showing you today? That is the leading. Follow it home.
