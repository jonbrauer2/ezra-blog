The jeweler's bench holds its sixth test, and it is the one everyone expects a prophet's test to be: prediction. Saturday's devotional watched Hananiah die for a comfortable lie; today the lesson turns to the very chapter that tells his story and finds in it the test he failed. The principle is stated in Jeremiah 28:9 — when a prophet's word comes to pass, then the LORD has truly sent him. Prediction is where the claim finally touches the ground.

The test itself comes from Moses: *"When a prophet speaks in the name of the Lord, if the thing does not happen or come to pass, that is the thing which the Lord has not spoken; the prophet has spoken it presumptuously; you shall not be afraid of him"* (Deuteronomy 18:22, NKJV). August's devotional filed this verse in a row of four tests; today's lesson slows down and lets it stand alone. Simple to state — and, the lesson immediately admits, challenging to apply. Some prophecies are conditional. They might not come to pass as spoken, if the conditions are met.

That fine print has a story, and it belongs to a city that should not have survived. Jonah's sermon was barely a sentence: *"Yet forty days, and Nineveh shall be overthrown!"* (Jonah 3:4, NKJV). An hourglass turned over on an enemy capital — and the sand ran out, and the city still stood. Had the test failed? Had the prophet spoken presumptuously after all? The people had believed God, proclaimed a fast, and turned from their evil ways, and God did not do what He had said. The destruction never landed — because the warning worked.

Jeremiah 18:7–10 explains it. When God declares He will pull a nation down and that nation turns from its evil, He relents of the disaster He planned. When He declares He will build and plant, and the nation does evil, He reconsiders the good He promised. Prophecies of doom and blessing are threaded on response. The prediction test is not a mechanical sieve but a moral instrument: God knows the future, and we do not, and our choices become part of how the future unfolds. Nineveh's reprieve did not falsify Jonah; it fulfilled the purpose of the warning. The threat was the invitation.

Notice the fine print is not a loophole for false voices. Hananiah's word never came true, and his end came on schedule; conditional prophecy never sheltered him (one nod to Saturday's story — today it is Jonah, not Hananiah, who stands in the dock, and Jonah's word was vindicated by the very mercy it provoked). But generations later Nineveh forgot the warning that had saved it, became cruel again, and this time the ruin came. The same prophecy had both a yes and a not-yet, and the difference was made by the people who heard it.

So the bench's sixth test reads like the rest: not a trick for catching voices out, but a way love verifies a treasure. When a warning word arrives — through a prophet, a sermon, a conscience — the countdown it starts is not fate with a clock on it; it is mercy with a clock on it. The forty days are still running somewhere in every life that is hearing this. What warning has God put in front of you — and what would your turning do to the deadline?
