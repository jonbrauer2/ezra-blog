Yesterday we watched what happens when a verse is torn out of its setting. Today the lesson asks the kinder question: how is a text read so that it gives up its meaning?

A lawyer stands up to test Jesus. It is the oldest test in the world — put the Teacher on the spot, watch Him fumble. Instead, Jesus hands the question back: "What's written in God's Law? How do you interpret it?" (Luke 10:26, Message). Notice what the Lord did not say. He did not say *what do you feel* it means, or *what have you heard* it means. He pointed the scholar back to the text itself — and to the act of reading. The same book lay open in every hand in Israel; the readings differed, and the readings were the difference.

The lesson lays out a working path for reading. Begin with the words themselves — what did the author actually say? Then follow them into their literary context, the paragraph and book that carry them. Widen out to the whole of Scripture, and to the historical-cultural world in which they were spoken. And finally, hold every conclusion up against the wider teaching of the Bible, letting the clear passages interpret the dark ones. The four Gospels model this constantly: set Matthew beside Luke and each sheds light on the other.

Watch what that method does with a single word. "Be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect" (Matthew 5:48, paraphrasing NKJV) read alone sounds like a demand for flawlessness — a standard no honest reader can meet. But set it beside its parallel, Luke 6:36: "Be merciful, just as your Father is also merciful." The context does not shrink the standard; it makes the standard recognizable. The perfection God asks for is a mercy that copies its Father — loving the enemy, doing good to the one who does not deserve it. A verse can be true and still be misread, and the misreading can crush the very person the verse was written to heal.

The lesson names the two qualities this requires: thoroughness and humility. "None of us owns truth; we all see through a glass darkly" (1 Corinthians 13:12). So build on what is plain, and let time and the Spirit clarify the rest — short of eternity, there will always be unanswered questions, and that is not a failure of the Book but a feature of our finiteness.

The lesson leaves us with a question worth carrying today: think of a time you didn't understand something that greatly bothered you — until, later, it was explained. How should that remembered experience teach you to trust God with the passages that, for now, you still cannot harmonize?

Read it whole. The meaning is in the text, waiting for a humble reader.
