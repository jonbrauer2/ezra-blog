The jeweler's bench holds its fifth test this week, and it is the patient one. The diamond's own proving was sudden — heat it, drop it in cold water, watch the fake shatter on contact. But the test Jesus named in the Sermon on the Mount does not work that way: *"By their fruits you will know them"* (Matthew 7:20, NKJV). Fruit is not struck like a flint or dropped like a stone; it ripens. The lesson puts it plainly — the quality of a fruit can be judged only over a period of time. The first four tests examined a message in the moment. This one watches a life, and a ministry, across a season.

Jesus set the test inside His longest recorded sermon, where He was laying the foundations of the church, and He aimed it at prophets: watch what the voice produces. The lesson reads the fruit on two sides. There is the fruit of the messenger's character — does the life draw people toward God or away from Him? And there is the fruit of the message itself — what does the teaching yield in the people who live by it? A claim can impress for an afternoon. Fruit takes years to fake, and that is precisely why Jesus chose it as evidence.

The test has an uncomfortable edge: being a prophet does not make a person sinless or personally infallible. Scripture records the flaws without flinching. Jonah ran from his calling. Paul and Barnabas contended so sharply over Mark that they parted directions for a season (Acts 15:36–39). Peter — the church's boldest voice — stood to be condemned at Antioch when he wavered before the party of the circumcision (Galatians 2:11–14). God alone is flawless; His messengers never are. The evidence is not perfection but the trend. Prophets, like all of us, carry weaknesses — but the direction of their lives must bend toward God, and a lifestyle that blatantly contradicts the message puts the whole ministry under suspicion.

When the fruit is good, the gallery is worth walking through. Haggai's faithfulness stirred a stalled people — a temple rebuild paused for nineteen years started again (Haggai 1:12). Isaiah's unflinching ministry steadied four kings of Judah and kept pointing past the turmoil to the Messiah (Isaiah 39:5–6; 53:4–6). There was Daniel's heartfelt confession (Daniel 9:15) and Jeremiah weeping over the people he could not stop warning (Jeremiah 9:1). Moses led Israel through the wilderness, and even his failures are preserved on the same pages (Deuteronomy 34:10–12). The record that keeps the failures is the same record that keeps the fruit — and that honesty is part of Scripture's credibility.

The lesson asks a question that stings: how often have men or women of God disappointed you? It is nearly a universal wound — a teacher fell, a leader failed, and something in us wanted to conclude the whole thing was hollow. Today's test answers differently. Disappointment in a flawed messenger is not proof the message was false; it is proof the messenger was human, exactly as the Book said. Keep watching the fruit — the trend of the life, the yield of the teaching. If over the years it bends toward God, the stone on the bench is proving itself the slow way, the way fruit has always proved itself. What has the long watch shown you?
