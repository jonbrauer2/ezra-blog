Paul had planned two visits. He made one. His critics used the changed itinerary as evidence against him. They called him a yes-and-no apostle, unreliable, hedging.

Paul's defense is not that he never changes plans. His defense is that when he changes them, it is out of love, not fickleness. And then he does something unexpected. He anchors his own reliability in God's. *"For all the promises of God in Him are Yes, and in Him Amen, to the glory of God through us."* (2 Corinthians 1:20, NKJV)

Every promise. Not most. Not most of the important ones. Every one. And in Christ they are Yes — affirmed, kept, guaranteed. And Amen — settled, sealed, so be it. Paul is saying: I cannot promise better than the God I serve. My reliability is downstream of His. If I falter, He does not.

That is bracing to receive when you have been let down. When someone said they would come and did not. When something was promised and quietly withdrawn. When you tried to be a person of your word and discovered how much easier the smaller version of yourself is to inhabit. Every human yes eventually cracks. Every human amen eventually fades. Only one voice has never taken a promise back.

But there is a second thing to notice. Paul closes the paragraph with this: *"Not that we have dominion over your faith, but are fellow workers for your joy."* (2 Corinthians 1:24, NKJV) He refuses the role of controller. His changed plan was not manipulation. It was discernment shaped by love. He would rather look inconsistent than harm them by a visit made at the wrong moment.

That is what love sometimes looks like. It changes the plan because it sees more of the room than it did before. It is willing to be misread rather than to force a schedule that does not serve. It moves at the pace the person actually needs, not at the pace that would clear the calendar fastest.

Jesus makes the same point about ordinary Christian speech in the Sermon on the Mount. *"Let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No.' For whatever is more than these is from the evil one."* (Matthew 5:37, NKJV) Yes and No are not manipulation tools. They are truth instruments. When you say yes, mean yes. When you must change the yes into a no, do it in love and in the open, so the person hears care and not caprice.

Ellen White writes: *"His plans were not conceived hastily... but they were of the nature of those which God can approve."*

When did you last change a plan out of genuine love for someone else, even at the cost of being misread? What did the change cost you, and what did it protect in them?
