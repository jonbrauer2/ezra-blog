There is a moment coming that will not be a process. It will not be a gradual improvement, a slow ascent, a stage of spiritual formation. It will be instantaneous.

*"Behold, I tell you a mystery: We shall not all sleep, but we shall all be changed — in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet."* (1 Corinthians 15:51-52, NKJV)

The blink of an eye. Not a decade of refinement. Not a purgatorial waiting. The transformation from mortal to immortal happens in the length of time it takes light to cross a room. Every believer, living or dead, is changed together at the same signal. We do not help finish it. We do not qualify for it by degrees. It is done to us at the trumpet.

For those who have died, there is no long conscious tunnel. No centuries of waiting in some intermediate place. Their next conscious moment is resurrection morning. The saint who closed her eyes in AD 90 and the saint who closes his eyes tomorrow open them together, in the same instant, when the voice sounds.

Paul then borrows a taunt from the prophets. *"O Death, where is your sting? O Hades, where is your victory?"* (1 Corinthians 15:55, NKJV) Notice the tone. This is not wishful thinking about a still-fearsome enemy. It is not whistling in the dark. It is the language you use over a defeated foe — the boot on the neck, the mocking question, the enemy who lost.

Which is worth stopping on. Because most of us still speak to death the way you speak to a monster in the closet. We keep our distance. We change the subject. We treat it as the one enemy who wins in the end.

Paul insists the enemy has already lost. The sting was drawn at the cross. The victory was announced at the empty tomb. What remains is only the interval before the trumpet. *"Thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ."* (1 Corinthians 15:57, NKJV) The victory is given. Not earned, not accessed by advanced faith, not restricted to the strongest. Given.

Ellen White writes: *"The very same voice that called Lazarus from the tomb will call the sleeping saints out of their graves."*

Grief is real. Loss is real. We do not pretend otherwise. But underneath the grief a taunt is entitled to sit — the taunt of a people whose enemy has been openly disarmed, whose separations are temporary, whose next great signal is a trumpet.

Where does your fear of death still come from? What would it take to let the taunt of 1 Corinthians 15:55 become your own?
