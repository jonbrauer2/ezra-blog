After the golden calf, after the worst collective betrayal in Israel's history, Moses climbed back up the mountain with two blank tablets. What he encountered there was not God's anger — or not only that. God passed before Moses and proclaimed His own name:

*"The LORD, the LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin."* (Exodus 34:6-7, NKJV)

These are God's own words about Himself. In the moment of greatest breach, He introduces Himself not as Judge but as the God whose defining character is mercy. Compassion. Patience. Abounding goodness.

Israel did not come to this knowledge by studying God's acts from a safe distance. They came to it at the worst possible time, as people who had no standing to expect grace. And God answered by naming Himself.

Psalm 103 picks up the same vocabulary: *"The LORD is merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in mercy."* (Psalm 103:8, NKJV) The psalmist is not guessing at God's character. He has the testimony of Sinai. He knows the name.

The tax collector in Luke 18 seems to have known it too. He would not so much as lift his eyes to heaven. He beat his chest. And he prayed the shortest, most complete prayer in the Gospels: *"God, be merciful to me, a sinner."* (Luke 18:13, NKJV) No extras. No explanation. No bargaining. One sentence — and he went home justified.

Ellen White puts the weight of this simply: when we approach God in repentance, we are not approaching a reluctant deity who must be convinced to extend grace. We are approaching the God who defined Himself at Sinai as "abounding in goodness." The grace does not need to be coaxed out of Him. It is who He is.

*"Behold, I stand at the door and knock."* (Revelation 3:20, NKJV) He is the one standing outside. He is the one who has come to the door. The posture is His, and it is toward you.

What image of God do you carry when you approach Him after you've failed? Take it back to Exodus 34.
