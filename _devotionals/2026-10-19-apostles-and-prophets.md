Two words carry enormous freight in the New Testament: *apostle* and *prophet*. We tend to blur them together, but the early church kept them distinct. An apostle — the Greek word is *apostolos* — was “one sent forth,” a messenger with a commission. The Twelve held that office, and Paul claimed it too, a dozen times across his letters: *“called to be an apostle of Jesus Christ through the will of God”* (1 Corinthians 1:1, NKJV).

Notice what Paul never claims: inheritance. Under the old system, a priest was a priest because his father was a priest; the office ran down the family line like an estate. Not so with apostles and prophets. God appointed them by His will — one by one, by name, by call. When Paul’s appointment was disputed, he didn’t appeal to a pedigree; he appealed to the calling and its fruit (1 Corinthians 9:1–2). And in the same opening breath of Romans he anchored his message to the older voices: the gospel of God, *“which He promised before through His prophets in the Holy Scriptures”* (Romans 1:1–2, NKJV). The apostle’s newest message stood on the prophets’ oldest words.

That pairing runs all through the New Testament. Paul speaks of the mystery *“revealed unto his holy apostles and prophets by the Spirit”* (Ephesians 3:5, KJV) — apostles receiving revelation just as prophets did: the same Spirit, the same inspiration. Peter does the same at the end of his life, telling believers to remember *“the words which were spoken before by the holy prophets, and the commandment of us the apostles of the Lord and Saviour”* (2 Peter 3:2, KJV). Two streams. One river.

Yet the streams were not the same office. At the Jerusalem council we meet *“Judas and Silas, who were themselves prophets,”* who *“exhorted and strengthened the brethren with many words”* (Acts 15:32, NKJV) — and neither was an apostle. The prophetic gift was never the private property of the Twelve. God’s voice reached His church through many mouths, and every one of them was measured against the same Stone.

That is the picture Paul paints: a church *“built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Jesus Christ Himself being the chief corner stone”* (Ephesians 2:20, NKJV). As we saw yesterday, their message had one center — Christ. Their offices differed; their Foundation was identical.

And here is where it becomes practical. The Bible is the only official record we have of what the apostles and prophets said about Jesus — His life, death, resurrection, ministry, and return. That is why it, and not the loudest voice or the latest teacher, must hold final authority. Ellen White wrote, *“The Bible, and the Bible alone, is to be the basis of our doctrines.”* —Ellen G. White, *Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 416.

So test the voices you let near your soul. When someone claims new light, ask where they stand relative to the Foundation. A true messenger will point you back to the written Word; a counterfeit will ask you to move the cornerstone.
