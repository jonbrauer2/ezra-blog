The jeweler's bench holds its fourth test this morning. Monday's test bent a message against the whole Book and asked whether it harmonized. Today's is narrower and sharper: not whether a voice agrees with the Book, but what it says about the Book's center.

The lesson opens with an uncomfortable observation: plenty of voices pass a softer version of this test. Other major world religions acknowledge that a man named Jesus lived and taught. Spiritual teachers of every stripe will call Him holy, wise, even divine-adjacent. Agreement that Jesus existed is not the test. John presses for a confession: *"Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God, and every spirit that does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is not of God"* (1 John 4:2–3, NKJV). The week's charter verse commanded us to test the spirits; John's next line hands us the first concrete criterion.

When John wrote, he was answering teachers who claimed Jesus only seemed to wear a body — deity passing through, humanity as a costume. But the test cannot be boxed into first-century terms, because the confession it demands is larger than an opinion about anatomy. To confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is to confess the whole truth about Him: God incarnate and truly human, born of heaven yet weary enough to sleep in a boat. The one who knew no sin was made to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him (2 Corinthians 5:21). He is the resurrection and the life (John 11:25). He is our High Priest, touched with the feeling of our infirmities (Hebrews 4:14–15). He has gone to prepare a place for us (John 14:1–3), and there is salvation in no other name (Acts 4:12).

That is why the lesson insists Jesus must be the center of a prophet's teaching, not an ornament attached to it. October's devotional watched Ellen White hold a compass with two points — lead people to Jesus, point them to His Word. Today's test asks the prior question: what does a voice confess about the One it claims to serve? A prophet truly lifting up Christ will not gather a following for himself. The lesson pictures the genuine prophetic word as a spotlight sweeping darkness out of us until Jesus, the Morning Star, dawns in the heart.

So the bench gains its fourth test, and it may be the strangest: a message can harmonize with Scripture, run on wonders, and still fail here — if Christ is peripheral. Where does Jesus stand in the voices you hear — center, or margins? Hold every claim to that question first.
