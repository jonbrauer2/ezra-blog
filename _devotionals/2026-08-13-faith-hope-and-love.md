Three virtues abide, Paul says at the end of the chapter. Faith, hope, love. But the greatest of them is love — and once you understand why, the whole hierarchy of the spiritual life reorders itself.

*"And now abide faith, hope, love, these three; but the greatest of these is love."* (1 Corinthians 13:13, NKJV)

Faith is a bridge. Hope is a path. But love is the country on the other side. When you cross the bridge, you no longer need it. When you finish walking the path, it is behind you. Faith will one day resolve into sight — you will not need to believe in the God you can see. Hope will one day resolve into possession — you will not need to hope for what you already have. But love does not resolve into anything else, because love was never a means to something. Love was the destination itself.

Paul had already prepared the ground for this claim earlier in the chapter. Every spiritual gift is provisional. *"Whether there are prophecies, they will fail; whether there are tongues, they will cease; whether there is knowledge, it will vanish away."* (1 Corinthians 13:8, NKJV) The tools have a shelf life. They serve us in this age, in the age of the partial, and then they are set aside. *"For we know in part, and we prophesy in part; but when that which is perfect has come, then that which is in part will be done away."* (1 Corinthians 13:9-10, NKJV) Partial is not deficient. It is the shape of pre-eschaton knowing. It is what the child's understanding is — genuine, appropriate, incomplete, on its way to something fuller.

And then the mirror sentence: *"For now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face; now I know in part, but then I shall know just as I also am known."* (1 Corinthians 13:12, NKJV) There is the assurance that always slips past readers in a hurry. *As I am known.* You do not have to work up your knowledge of God to justify your existence in His presence. He knows you. Already. Completely. That knowledge is the ground under everything you will ever come to know of Him. Your knowing is a response to His knowing, not a prerequisite.

Paul told the Thessalonians the same three virtues in the opening of his first letter to them: *"remembering without ceasing your work of faith, labor of love, and patience of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ."* (1 Thessalonians 1:3, NKJV) Notice how they cluster. Faith produces work. Love produces labor. Hope produces patience. The three virtues are never idle. They are always generating something in the life of the person who holds them.

Romans adds one more layer: *"The love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who was given to us."* (Romans 5:5, NKJV) Where does the love come from? Not from within. It is poured in from outside — the Spirit's deposit into the human heart. Which is why the greatest virtue is not the hardest to obtain. It is the one most freely offered, if we will stop trying to manufacture it and start receiving it.

Here is the reorienting question. If every spiritual practice you invested in this year ended tonight — except for the love it produced — would you invest the same way tomorrow? Would you pray differently? Give differently? Serve differently? Whatever you would change, change it.

Love is the only thing that survives the audit.
