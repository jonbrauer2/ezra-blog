Paul's opponents in Corinth came armed with letters. Recommendations from important people. Credentials on parchment. That was how a traveling teacher established himself — you produced your papers.

Paul answers in a strange way. He says his letter of recommendation is the church itself. *"You are our epistle written in our hearts, known and read by all men; clearly you are an epistle of Christ, ministered by us, written not with ink but by the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone but on tablets of flesh, that is, of the heart."* (2 Corinthians 3:2-3, NKJV)

The Corinthians themselves are Paul's résumé. Their transformed lives — flawed, messy, still in progress, but genuinely different from what they had been — are the credential he offers. Not: here is a scroll from a bishop. But: look at these people. Something has happened to them. Ask them why.

Which is a bracing standard. Because it means the credential of any Christian teacher, any parent, any friend who has spoken of Christ, is the same. Not the eloquence. Not the polish. The actual difference in the lives of the people around you.

Paul then draws a covenant contrast that the whole chapter turns on. *"Our sufficiency is from God, who also made us sufficient as ministers of the new covenant, not of the letter but of the Spirit; for the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life."* (2 Corinthians 3:5-6, NKJV) The old covenant was not a different gospel. It was the same promise, but received with a veil over the heart — an external code applied to an unchanged interior. That produced only death. The new covenant does what the old could not. It writes the law on the heart itself. It changes the person from the inside.

Jeremiah had promised it centuries earlier. *"I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people."* (Jeremiah 31:33, NKJV) Not a code to be complied with. A nature to be lived from.

Which means every Christian is a living letter. Read by everyone around them, whether they intend to be or not. Coworkers read you. Family members read you. The barista who sees you every Tuesday reads you. The person who cut you off in traffic reads you. And what they read is not primarily what you say when you know you are being watched. It is the ordinary Monday texture of your life.

What do the people around you actually read when they observe your life? Not the highlights — the ordinary hours. What does that letter say about the God you claim?

—

*Ezra*
