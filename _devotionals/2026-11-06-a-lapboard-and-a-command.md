Six days we have traced how the prophetic word reached paper: the command to write, the assistants who took dictation, the sources and research folded into sacred books, the treasure carried in cracked pots. Friday's lesson closes the week by turning the lens on a modern case study — Ellen White herself — and the parallel is remarkably exact.

Begin where she began: with a command she felt unfit to obey. Reflecting in 1909 on the aftermath of an accident that had nearly taken her life, she recalled: *"Because of the accident which had nearly cost me my life, I had been feeble in health and unable to write. . . . But one night the angel of the Lord came to my bedside and said to me, 'You must write out the things that I give you.' I said, 'I can not write.' Again the command was given, 'Write out the things I give you.' I thought I would try, and taking up a lapboard from the table, I began to write, and found that I could trace the words easily."*—The General Conference Bulletin, May 31, 1909.

Every day of this week lives in that one paragraph. The command itself is Sunday's theme in miniature — God did not merely permit the writing of His word, He ordered it: *"Write in a book for yourself all the words that I have spoken to you"* (Jeremiah 30:2, NKJV). And notice *whom* He ordered — a woman bedridden and feeble, an earthen vessel if there ever was one, three days after Thursday's lesson.

The rest of the week follows. Like Tertius with Paul, she worked with literary assistants — secretaries who did what editors do, expressing her messages clearly without inserting thoughts of their own; not ghostwriters, not coauthors. Like Luke and Paul with their sources, she quoted and adapted other literary works — openly recommending to her readers some of the very books she drew from, finding in others' language apt expressions for truths already given her. And when the hard questions came — questions like yesterday's cracked-pot theme — her answer was Thursday's point in her own words: *"In regard to infallibility,"* she wrote, *"I never claimed it; God alone is infallible."*—*Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 37.

But the deepest lesson is the shape of her obedience. The command came at night, to a woman in bed who answered, "I can not write" — and was not let off. She did not argue, and she did not wait to feel able. She reached for the nearest thing within arm's reach, a lapboard, and attempted one sentence. The words traced easily. That is usually how God's assignments begin: not with ability proven in advance, but with willingness attempted in weakness.

So take the question home: what has God asked of you that you have answered "I cannot"? Stop waiting to feel equal to it. Reach for the lapboard — the small, available first step — and begin to trace the words. He gives what He commands.

—

*Ezra*
