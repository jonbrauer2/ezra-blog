Some verses sound wrong on first hearing. Here is one: in the new heavens and the new earth — the very world where Scripture says death itself will die — Isaiah writes that "the child shall die one hundred years old" (Isaiah 65:20, NKJV). Death, in the deathless world? A sinner, still accursed, where righteousness dwells? If you read Isaiah 65 and walk away troubled, you are not faithless. You are only reading fast.

The lesson's Monday question slows us down, and the answer it gives is not an explanation that explains the verse away but a setting that sets it free. Isaiah 65:20 is symbolic language. What child is one hundred years old? Read in its historical setting, the verse was poetry announcing the covenant blessing Israel would know if she remained faithful — long lives, and no infants carried away too soon (compare Deuteronomy 28). And Isaiah, like Jesus linking more imminent events with later ones in Matthew 24, is doing what prophecy so often does: joining horizons. What God would do then is a precursor of what God will do finally. The new creation is not only future — it "has entered suddenly into the present through God's glorious work of redemption and forgiveness."

One prophecy can carry two fulfillments the way one range of hills carries ridge after ridge — the near horizon close enough to touch, the far horizon fading into glory. Isaiah's near horizon was blessing in the land; his far horizon is the new heavens and earth where death and sorrow finally cease (Isaiah 25:8; 26:19; 65:19). Flatten the ridges into one and you lose your way in both directions: read the far into the near and you will manufacture dates and manufacture disappointments; read only the near and you will settle for what God meant as a foretaste — as ancient Israel did, holding the promises so tightly that the Messiah standing in front of them was unrecognizable. It is the same one-sided edit that sent two disciples to Emmaus hoping in the past tense.

Then the lesson turns the principle around. *"Greet one another with a holy kiss"* — read without its setting, and you have a command nobody keeps; read within its setting, and you find a principle nobody may drop. In Paul's world, family greeted family with a kiss, and Paul blesses the church with the warmth of family: the biblical principle is to greet believers warmly and make them feel part of the family of God. In some cultures a kiss would be inappropriate — a handshake, a hug, a bow. The expression translates. The welcome does not.

Both readings are one discipline. Do not literalize the symbolic; do not discard the principle inside the cultural. The historical-cultural context is not the skeptic's tool for loosening the text — it is how Scripture is read honestly: every passage honored in the world it was spoken into, and carried faithfully into ours.

So take the verse that sounds strangest to you, and before asking what it means to you, ask what it meant to its first hearers — when, where, to whom. Yesterday we let the Book interpret itself; today let the setting have its say. The far horizon is still there, waiting behind the near one.
