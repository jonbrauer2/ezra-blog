## 🔑 Central Theme

The Corinthian church's sexual scandals were not separate from its theological confusion — they were downstream of it. When the gospel is emptied of the Cross (lesson 2) and Christian community fractures into status-seeking factions (lesson 3), the moral consequences are predictable: the boundaries between church and world dissolve, and the church begins to tolerate — even celebrate — what the surrounding culture normalized. This lesson asks the hard question: **How does the gospel create a genuinely distinct moral community without becoming either moralistic or permissive?**

Paul's answer is not simply a list of prohibitions. He grounds sexual ethics in three theological affirmations: (1) **identity** — you are washed, sanctified, justified (*1 Cor. 6:11*); (2) **union** — you are members of Christ's body (*1 Cor. 6:15–17*); (3) **indwelling** — your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (*1 Cor. 6:19–20*). The prohibition is the last thing Paul says, not the first. The foundation is *who you are*, not merely what you are not allowed to do.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 5:1–5 — Church Discipline and Its Purpose

- **"His father's wife" (v. 1):** This is stepmother — the marriage partner of his father, either still married or recently divorced/widowed. This was prohibited by both Jewish law (Lev. 18:7–8; Deut. 22:30) and Roman law (Gaius, *Institutes* 1.63). Cicero called such a relationship *scelus incestum* — criminal incest. The scandal was not merely internal; it was publicly recognizable as extreme even to pagans.
- **"And you are arrogant!" (v. 2):** The Greek *pephysiōmenoi* — puffed up, inflated — is the same word used for the factionalists in chapter 4. The Corinthians' tolerance of this sin was not mere negligence; it was a form of pride, likely the sophistication of those who thought themselves "beyond" conventional morality (cf. "all things are lawful," 1 Cor. 6:12). Boasting about cultural open-mindedness is not a modern invention.
- **"Deliver this man to Satan" (v. 5):** Not a curse but a description of what church exclusion means — the person re-enters the domain outside the church's protective sphere. The *purpose* is "that his spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord." Discipline is restorative, not punitive. The probable connection to 2 Corinthians 2:5–10 suggests Paul's approach worked.

### 1 Corinthians 5:6–8 — The Leaven Metaphor

- **"A little leaven leavens the whole lump":** Paul draws on a Jewish metaphor for moral contagion. In Passover observance, all leaven (*chametz*, Hebrew for "leavened matter") was removed from the house before the feast — a purification ritual. Paul applies this to the church: the incestuous man, unchecked, will corrupt the whole community. The body metaphor (1 Cor. 12) and the leaven metaphor teach the same thing: what happens to one member affects the whole.
- **"Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (v. 7, ESV):** The Passover typology is explicit and the sequence matters. The sacrifice *precedes* the leaven-removal. Moral purity is not a precondition for grace; it is a *consequence* of grace. Because the Lamb has already been sacrificed, the old leaven of malice and immorality must go.

### 1 Corinthians 6:12 — "All Things Are Lawful"

- This appears to be a Corinthian slogan — Paul quotes it twice (6:12; 10:23) and qualifies it both times: "All things are lawful for me, but not all things are helpful. All things are lawful for me, but I will not be mastered by anything."
- **"Mastered (*exousiasō*):"** Paul uses the root of *exousia* (authority/freedom) — irony deliberate. Using freedom to indulge an appetite is exactly how the appetite gains authority over you. The slave who insists on doing whatever he pleases is not free; he has simply changed masters. True freedom is the capacity to say no.

### 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — The Temple Metaphor

- **"Your bodies are temples (*naos*) of the Holy Spirit":** *Naos* is the inner sanctuary — the Holy of Holies — not the outer court (*hieron*). This is the most intimate spatial metaphor Paul could use. The Shekinah that dwelt in the Most Holy Place now dwells in the bodies of believers.
- **"You are not your own":** A statement of purchase. *Ēgorasthēte timēs* — "you were bought with a price" — is the language of manumission (purchasing a slave's freedom). God paid the price of Christ's life to make believers His own. This is not dehumanizing; it is the most radical liberation possible. But it means the body's use belongs to the new owner.
- **"Glorify God in your body":** The body is not incidental to salvation — it is the theater of glorification. Gnostic and Neoplatonist views dismissed the body as morally irrelevant (hence "the body is for sex, sex for the body"). Paul insists the body is the site of worship. The resurrection (v. 14) seals this: God will raise these very bodies, which means they matter eternally.

### 2 Corinthians 2:5–10 — Restoration after Discipline

- The man disciplined in 1 Corinthians 5 appears to be "the one who has caused grief" in 2 Corinthians 2:5. Paul now urges *forgiveness* and *comfort* — not continued exclusion. "You should rather turn to forgive and comfort him, or he may be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow" (v. 7, ESV).
- **The arc:** Discipline (1 Cor. 5) → Repentance → Restoration (2 Cor. 2). The purpose of the discipline was achieved. This is the blueprint for church discipline: confrontation, consequence, restoration. All three stages are required; stopping at any one distorts the whole.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On Moral Dissonance and Cultural Accommodation

1. **The Corinthians were "arrogant" (*pephysiōmenoi*) about tolerating the incestuous man — as if their open-mindedness was a sign of spiritual sophistication. What contemporary versions of this do you recognize?** What is the difference between genuine compassion for a struggling person and the communal pride of tolerating what Scripture calls sin?

2. **The lesson's introduction notes that Sunday worship crept into the church not from Scripture but from the surrounding culture — and the Corinthians' sexual ethics followed the same pattern.** What specific aspects of 21st-century Adventist practice might future generations identify as having entered through cultural osmosis rather than biblical reflection?

3. **Paul connects doctrinal disorder in chapters 1–4 (factionalism, empty gospel, status-seeking) directly to moral disorder in chapters 5–7.** Is this a simple cause-effect relationship, or is something more complex going on? What is the mechanism by which theological confusion opens the door to moral failure?

4. **"What things are clearly condemned in Scripture that we, as a church, are in danger of tolerating — all in the name of 'love' and 'acceptance'?" (The lesson's own question.)** This is asking for genuine self-examination, not a critique of others. What is your honest answer about your own congregation?

### On Church Discipline

5. **Paul's language in 1 Corinthians 5 — "remove from among you," "deliver to Satan," "do not eat with such a one" — sounds harsh to modern ears. Yet his stated goal is the person's eventual salvation (v. 5) and the community's health (vv. 6–8). How do you hold restorative intent and painful process together in practice?** When does discipline become punitive rather than corrective?

6. **2 Corinthians 2:5–10 shows the arc working — discipline led to repentance, and Paul now urges full restoration. The church so often gets stuck at one stage: either skipping discipline entirely, performing discipline without any pathway to restoration, or refusing to complete the restoration once repentance has come.** Which failure is most common in your experience? Why?

7. **Paul tells the Corinthians not to "associate with" or "eat with" sexually immoral church members (5:9–11) — but explicitly exempts unbelievers from this rule, "otherwise you would need to go out of the world" (5:10, ESV).** What is the pastoral logic of this distinction? Where does appropriate firmness with a wayward believer end and pharisaical separation from the world begin?

8. **Paul asks seven "do you not know?" questions in 1 Corinthians 5–6, each demanding an emphatic affirmative answer.** What does this rhetorical pattern reveal about the source of the Corinthians' problem? Is their failure one of information (they don't know), or formation (they know but have become numb to it)?

### On Sexual Ethics and Christian Identity

9. **Paul structures his argument in 1 Corinthians 6:11–20 with identity first and prohibition last: you are washed, sanctified, justified — *therefore* flee sexual immorality. Why does the sequence matter theologically?** What happens to churches — and to individuals — that lead with the prohibition rather than the identity?

10. **"All things are lawful for me, but I will not be mastered by anything" (6:12). Paul is quoting a Corinthian slogan about freedom and inverting it — the very act of using freedom to indulge an appetite is how the appetite gains mastery.** Where is the line between Christian freedom and being enslaved to an appetite? How do you apply this beyond sexuality — to food, technology, entertainment, financial ambition?

11. **"The body is for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (6:13). This is one of the most striking affirmations in the New Testament about physical embodiment.** How does it simultaneously counter Gnostic dismissal of the body (bodies don't matter spiritually) and hedonistic affirmation of the body (bodies are for pleasure)? What is the specifically Pauline theology of embodiment?

12. **"Your bodies are temples (*naos*) of the Holy Spirit" — *naos* is the inner sanctuary, the Holy of Holies.** If taken seriously as a theological claim about divine indwelling rather than merely a health rule, how does this reshape how Christians should think about physical illness, disability, aging, and ultimately the resurrection body?

### On Marriage and Singleness

13. **1 Corinthians 7 appears to elevate singleness above marriage — "it is good for a man not to touch a woman" (7:1, NKJV). Yet Paul elsewhere honors marriage strongly (1 Tim. 4:1–3; Eph. 5:25–32; Heb. 13:4).** He is responding to specific pastoral questions in Corinth, not issuing a universal hierarchy. How do we read situational pastoral instruction in canonical Scripture without either over-applying or dismissing it?

14. **Paul tells those already in unequally yoked relationships not to seek separation (7:12–16) — "the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife" (v. 14, ESV).** This pastoral accommodation of existing situations stands in some tension with the principle of not forming such relationships in the first place. How do you hold both?

15. **How do you articulate a biblical sexual ethic — which is increasingly experienced as exclusionary by the surrounding culture — with both theological fidelity and genuine pastoral compassion?** Is there a way to hold the position without making people feel condemned before they have had a chance to hear the gospel?

### On Holiness and Integrated Formation

16. **Paul's vice lists in 1 Corinthians 5:10–11 and 6:9–10 group sexual immorality with idolatry, greed, and drunkenness — and 1 Corinthians 10:7–8 (drawing on Exodus 32) shows the sequence: idolatry → feasting → sexual immorality.** Ellen White makes the same connection between physical appetite and moral character. Does this pattern hold up under contemporary neuroscience and behavioral psychology? What does it mean for a theology of spiritual formation?

17. **Paul says in 6:3 that "we shall judge angels" — he uses this as an argument that the church should handle its own internal disputes rather than taking them to secular courts.** What is the theological basis for this claim? What does it imply about the church's eschatological role, and does it apply straightforwardly to contemporary church governance?

18. **The Passover typology in 5:7–8 is a compressed theological argument: Passover Lamb sacrificed → remove the leaven → feast on truth. Paul moves from sexual scandal to the Passover to the Lord's Supper in three verses.** What does this typological move teach us about how Old Testament ritual informs contemporary ethical reflection? What are the limits of this kind of typological reading?

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. On Physical Appetite and Moral Character

> *"It is impossible for any to enjoy the blessing of sanctification while they are selfish and gluttonous. . . . Persistent wrong habits in excessive eating and drinking will enfeeble every function of the body. In the gratification of perverted appetite and passion even professed Christians cripple nature in her work and lessen physical, mental, and moral power."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Sanctified Life*, pp. 25, 26

**Context:** Commenting on the connection Paul draws between vice lists and physical indulgence — idolatrous festivals were "marked with excessive eating and drinking, which opened the door for sexual immorality" (cf. 1 Cor. 10:7–8; Exod. 32:1–6). EGW traces the same logic Paul traces: physical appetite and moral character are not separate departments.

**Discussion prompt:** Is the connection EGW draws between gluttony and sanctification a moralistic guilt trip about diet, or a serious anthropological claim about how the whole person is formed? What would a non-moralistic, gospel-grounded body-formation theology look like in practice?

---

### 2. On Spiritual Fullness as the Antidote

> *"When one is fully emptied of self, when every false god is cast out of the soul, the vacuum is supplied by the inflowing of the Spirit of Christ. Such a one has the faith which works by love and purifies the soul from every moral and spiritual defilement."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Home Missionary*, November 1889

**Context:** This quote addresses the mechanism of moral transformation — not willpower but displacement. "False gods cast out" creates a vacuum; the Spirit fills it. This is EGW's pneumatological account of sanctification.

**Discussion prompt:** If moral transformation works by displacement — Spirit filling the space left by expelled idols — what are the "false gods" occupying space in your own life right now? What is the practical process of casting them out, and how is that different from simply trying harder?

---

### 3. On the Holy Spirit's Constant Work

> *"God is seeking to exalt us to His high, pure, heavenly standard. For this purpose His Spirit is constantly striving with us. . . . Our natural tendencies, unless corrected by the Holy Spirit of God, have in them the seeds of moral death."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Manuscript 12*, 1888

**Context:** Written against the backdrop of Adventism's 1888 discussions on righteousness by faith, this quote holds together anthropological realism ("seeds of moral death" in natural tendencies) and pneumatological optimism ("constantly striving").

**Discussion prompt:** The word "constantly" is load-bearing here — the Spirit's striving is not intermittent or conditional on your spiritual performance. How does it change your self-assessment when the Spirit's work is constant even when your responsiveness is not? What does it mean practically to cooperate with work that is already underway?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

### Individual

- **Temple audit:** "You are not your own; you were bought at a price" (6:20). Identify one habit involving your body — eating, sleeping, exercise, screen time, sexual choices — and write an honest one-paragraph account of who that habit is currently serving. Is this glorifying God in your body, or is something else being mastered?
- **Leaven inventory:** Using the Passover metaphor (5:7–8), ask: what old leaven am I carrying from the culture that has not yet been swept out? Name one specific attitude or practice you have tolerated in yourself that you would readily name as "leaven" in someone else.
- **Joseph exercise:** The lesson cites Joseph fleeing Potiphar's wife (Gen. 39). "Flee" is active and physical — not an inner resolution but a change of location. Identify one situation in your current life where the right move is not to resist but to remove yourself entirely.

### Sabbath School

- **Arrogance check:** The Corinthians were proud of tolerating the incestuous man. As a class, name one area where your congregation has taken pride in a form of "open-mindedness" that may be cultural accommodation rather than gospel generosity. This exercise requires mutual trust — begin by having the teacher model the confession.
- **Discipline arc roleplay:** Assign three groups the three stages — discipline, repentance, restoration — and have each group describe what their stage looks like done well and done badly. Combine findings: what would a full, healthy arc look like in a real contemporary situation?
- **"Do you not know?" questions:** Read through Paul's seven *ouk oidate* ("do you not know?") questions in 1 Cor. 5–6 together. For each, discuss: Is this a question about knowledge or formation? What would it take for the class to truly "know" this in a way that changes behavior?

### Intellectual

- **Typology and ethics:** Paul's move from sexual scandal to Passover typology to Lord's Supper (5:6–8) is a compressed example of typological ethical reasoning. Read Exodus 12:1–20 and then 1 Corinthians 5:6–8 back to back. What is preserved in the type, what is fulfilled and transformed, and what cannot be carried forward? This is a test case for how to use OT ritual for contemporary ethics.
- **Neuroscience and formation:** Look up research on "habit formation" and "neural pathways" in behavioral psychology. How does the contemporary science of habit formation intersect with Paul's argument that bodily practices shape identity? Where does the science support a body-formation theology, and where does it stop short?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **Grace as the ground of holiness:** The Passover typology (5:7–8) insists the sacrifice precedes the leaven-removal. Holiness is not the precondition for belonging to Christ; belonging to Christ is the ground for holiness. Getting this sequence wrong produces either legalism (earn your belonging) or license (grace covers everything anyway).
- **The body in salvation:** Against Gnosticism (the body is irrelevant) and hedonism (the body is for pleasure), Paul insists the body is the site of worship, the temple of the Spirit, and the locus of glorification. The resurrection (6:14) anchors this: God will raise these bodies, which means they matter eternally.
- **Discipline as love:** Church discipline in 1 Corinthians 5, followed by restoration in 2 Corinthians 2, is the template for pastoral care that takes both sin and personhood seriously. The aim is never punishment; it is salvation and community health simultaneously.
- **Freedom and mastery:** The Corinthian slogan "all things are lawful" reveals that freedom from law can become slavery to appetite. True freedom is the capacity for self-governance under the Spirit — not absence of constraint but the presence of the right master.
- **Integrated formation:** The connection of physical appetite and moral character (vice lists, EGW, Exodus 32 pattern) anticipates contemporary neuroscience — bodily habits shape moral dispositions. Formation is whole-person, not merely spiritual or intellectual.
- **"Do you not know?":** Paul's seven rhetorical questions in chapters 5–6 suggest the Corinthians' failure is not primarily informational but formational. They knew the truths; they had ceased to live from them. Knowledge that does not form the person is inert.
