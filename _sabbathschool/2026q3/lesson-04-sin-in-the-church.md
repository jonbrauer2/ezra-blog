## Central Theme

The Corinthian church's sexual scandals were downstream of its theological confusion. When the gospel is emptied of the Cross and Christian community fractures into status-seeking factions, moral consequences follow: the boundaries between church and world dissolve. This lesson asks: **How does the gospel create a genuinely distinct moral community without becoming either moralistic or permissive?**

Paul's answer grounds sexual ethics in three theological affirmations: (1) **identity** — you are washed, sanctified, justified (6:11); (2) **union** — you are members of Christ's body (6:15–17); (3) **indwelling** — your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (6:19–20). The prohibition comes last. The foundation is *who you are*, not merely what you are not allowed to do.

## Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 5:1–5 — Church Discipline and Its Purpose
- **"His father's wife" (v. 1):** Stepmother — prohibited by both Jewish law (Lev. 18:7–8) and Roman law. Cicero called such a relationship *scelus incestum* — criminal incest.
- **"And you are arrogant!" (v. 2):** *Pephysiōmenoi* — puffed up. Same word used for the factionalists of chapter 4. Their tolerance was not negligence but a form of pride — the sophistication of those who thought themselves "beyond" conventional morality.
- **"Deliver this man to Satan" (v. 5):** Not a curse but a description of church exclusion — the person re-enters the domain outside the church's sphere. The *purpose* is "that his spirit may be saved." Discipline is restorative, not punitive.

### 1 Corinthians 5:6–8 — The Leaven Metaphor
- **"A little leaven leavens the whole lump":** Moral contagion. At Passover, all leaven was removed before the feast. Paul applies this to the church's moral situation.
- **"Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (v. 7, ESV):** The sacrifice *precedes* the leaven-removal. Grace is the ground of holiness, not its rival.

### 1 Corinthians 6:12 — "All Things Are Lawful"
- A Corinthian slogan Paul quotes and qualifies twice (6:12; 10:23): "All things are lawful for me, but I will not be mastered by anything."
- **"Mastered (*exousiasō*)":** The irony — using freedom to indulge an appetite is exactly how the appetite gains authority over you. True freedom is the capacity to say no.

### 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 — The Temple Metaphor
- **"Your bodies are temples (*naos*)":** The inner sanctuary — Holy of Holies — not the outer court. Most intimate spatial metaphor for divine indwelling.
- **"You are not your own":** *Ēgorasthēte timēs* — "bought with a price," language of manumission. God paid the price of Christ's life. The body's use belongs to the new owner.
- **"Glorify God in your body":** Against Gnosticism (the body is irrelevant) and hedonism (the body is for pleasure) — the body is the site of worship.

## Deep Discussion Questions

### On Moral Dissonance and Cultural Accommodation
1. **The Corinthians were "arrogant" (*pephysiōmenoi*) about tolerating the incestuous man. What might have made them proud of their tolerance? What contemporary versions of this do you recognize — where churches take pride in "open-mindedness" about matters Paul would call leaven?**

2. **What specific aspects of 21st-century Adventist practice might future generations identify as having crept in from culture rather than Scripture?**

3. **"What things are clearly condemned in Scripture that we, as a church, are in danger of tolerating in the name of 'love' and 'acceptance'?" What is your honest answer?**

4. **The lesson connects moral disorder in chapters 5–7 to theological confusion in chapters 1–4 — factionalism opened the door. Is this a cause-effect relationship? How does doctrinal disorder lead to moral disorder?**

### On Church Discipline
5. **Paul's goal in discipline is "that his spirit may be saved" (5:5) and the community's purity (5:6–8). How do you hold these two purposes in tension? When does discipline become punitive rather than restorative?**

6. **2 Corinthians 2:5–10 describes the apparently successful restoration of the man from 1 Corinthians 5. The arc: discipline → repentance → restoration. Why does the church so often get stuck at one of these three stages?**

7. **Paul says don't associate with immoral "brothers" but explicitly does not mean unbelievers (5:10). How does this distinction shape Adventist social ethics? Where does appropriate separation end and pharisaism begin?**

### On Sexual Ethics and Christian Identity
8. **Paul grounds the prohibition against sexual immorality in theological identity — washed, sanctified, temple of the Spirit — *before* the ethical command. Why does the order matter? What happens when churches lead with prohibition rather than identity?**

9. **"All things are lawful for me, but I will not be mastered by anything" (6:12). Where is the line between Christian freedom and being mastered by an appetite? How do you apply this to areas beyond sexuality — food, technology, entertainment?**

10. **"The body is for the Lord, and the Lord for the body" (6:13). How does this counter both Gnostic dismissal of the body and hedonistic affirmation of it? What is the Pauline view of embodiment?**

11. **"Your bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit" — if taken as a theological statement about divine indwelling, not merely a health rule, how does it reshape how Christians think about disability, aging, illness, and the resurrection body?**

### On Marriage and Singleness
12. **1 Corinthians 7 appears to give singleness higher status than marriage, yet Paul elsewhere affirms marriage strongly (1 Tim. 4:1–3, Eph. 5:25–32). He is responding to a specific pastoral situation. How do we read situational pastoral instruction in canonical Scripture? Does context limit the text's application?**

13. **How do you articulate a biblical sexual ethic — which is increasingly countercultural — with both fidelity and genuine compassion in a context where it is experienced as exclusionary?**

### Harder Questions
14. **EGW connects sexual immorality to gluttony (vice lists combine them; Exodus 32 pattern: idolatry → feasting → immorality). Does the body-formation theology she implies — physical appetite shapes moral character — hold up under contemporary neuroscience?**

15. **"When one is fully emptied of self... the vacuum is supplied by the inflowing of the Spirit of Christ" (EGW). Moral transformation through displacement, not willpower. What is the practical process of "emptying self" and being filled with the Spirit?**

16. **Paul tells believers already in unequally yoked relationships not to seek separation (7:12–16) — "the unbelieving husband is made holy because of his wife." How do you hold Paul's pastoral accommodation of existing situations alongside the principle of not entering such relationships in the first place?**

## Practical Application

**Individual:** "You are not your own; you were bought at a price." Identify one habit involving your body — eating, sleeping, exercise, screen time, sexual choices — and ask: who is this habit serving? Is this glorifying God in my body, or is something else being mastered?

**In Sabbath School:** The Corinthians were proud of tolerating the incestuous man. Consider where your church community has taken pride in a form of "open-mindedness" that may be cultural accommodation. Name it honestly — this requires trust and gentleness.

**Intellectually:** Read 1 Corinthians 5:6–8: Paul moves from sexual scandal to the Passover typology to the Lord's Supper in three verses. Trace the logic: Passover Lamb → remove the leaven → feast on truth. What does this structure reveal about how Paul thinks about holiness and grace?

## Theological Threads

- **Grace as the ground of holiness:** The Passover typology insists the sacrifice precedes the leaven-removal. Holiness is not the precondition for belonging to Christ.
- **The body in salvation:** Against Gnosticism and hedonism, Paul insists the body is the site of worship, temple of the Spirit, locus of glorification.
- **Discipline as love:** 1 Corinthians 5 → 2 Corinthians 2: discipline → repentance → restoration. The blueprint for pastoral care that takes both sin and personhood seriously.
- **Freedom and mastery:** The Corinthian slogan reveals that freedom from law can become slavery to appetite. True freedom is the capacity for self-governance under the Spirit.
- **Integrated formation:** The connection between physical appetite and moral character anticipates contemporary neuroscience — bodily habits shape moral dispositions.

## Lesson Summary

**Core assertion:** Christian sexual ethics is not primarily a list of prohibitions but a theology of identity — *who you are* determines what you do. The body is a temple of the Holy Spirit. You were purchased at enormous cost.

**Key tensions:**
1. Holiness ↔ Legalism (identity precedes prohibition)
2. Grace ↔ License (freedom does not mean freedom *to* sin)
3. Discipline ↔ Restoration (both necessary; neither optional)
4. Sexual ethics ↔ Cultural accommodation (Corinth collapsed the boundary; Paul insists on distinction)

**The bottom line:** Your body is not a private matter between you and your impulses — it is a question of stewardship of something that belongs to Another. This is not oppression; it is the most radical form of dignity the gospel offers.
