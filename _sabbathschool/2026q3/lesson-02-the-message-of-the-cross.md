## 🔑 Central Theme

The Cross is not simply one doctrine among many — it is Paul's answer to *every* problem in the Corinthian church. Divisions, immorality, lawsuits, spiritual pride: each disorder, in Paul's diagnosis, is a symptom of the same disease — the eclipse of the Cross. This lesson asks a question that cuts deeper than historical theology: **Why does the Cross seem like foolishness, and what does that reveal about the human condition?**

Paul is not arguing that the Cross is counterintuitive but ultimately rational once you understand it. He is arguing that the Cross exposes the limits of human rationality itself. The category of *mōria* (foolishness) is not an intellectual deficit but a moral and relational one — the posture of a creature in rebellion against its Creator, for whom the very idea of a crucified God is offensive rather than redemptive. The Corinthians' factionalism and status games were not incidental; they were the social expression of this deeper foolishness — the refusal to organize life around the shame and grace of the Cross.

Cicero said Romans should not even think about the cross as an instrument of punishment. Paul says it is the instrument of cosmic reconciliation. These two positions cannot both be right — and which one you inhabit determines everything about your sociology, your epistemology, and your hope.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### [1 Corinthians 1:17–18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.17) — Wisdom of Words vs. the Foolishness of the Cross

- **Context:** Paul is addressing the factionalism at Corinth (1:10–17) — people aligning behind celebrity preachers (Paul, Apollos, Cephas, Christ). His diagnosis is that rhetorical culture — the Greek love of eloquent, polished speech — has colonized the church and is subtly evacuating the Cross of its power.
- **"Not with wisdom of words":** This does not condemn eloquence per se but the substitution of rhetorical performance for the scandalous content of the Cross. In Greco-Roman culture, the paid orator's value lay in the power of his speech, independent of truth. Paul refuses to be that kind of speaker with the gospel.
- **"Lest the cross of Christ be emptied (*kenōthē*) of its power":** The verb *kenoō* — to empty, to make void — is the same root as the famous *kenōsis* in [Philippians 2:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Phil2.7) ("he emptied himself"). Elegant rhetoric *empties* the Cross in the same way Christ *emptied* himself to take on flesh — but the results are inverse. Christ's self-emptying was the source of salvation; rhetorical self-filling evacuates it.
- **"The message of the cross is foolishness (*mōria*)":** The Greek *mōria* (foolishness) is not simple intellectual error but morally laden stupidity — the kind of thinking that leads to self-destruction. Paul uses this word six times in [1 Cor. 1:18–31](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.18), saturating the argument. The word family in the NT occurs most densely here — this is Paul's sustained meditation on foolishness as a category.

### [1 Corinthians 1:22–24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.22) — Jews, Greeks, and the Crucified Messiah

- **"Jews seek a sign, Greeks seek wisdom":** Two epistemological postures — the Jew who demands miraculous proof of divine sanction (*sēmeion*, a cosmic endorsement), the Greek who demands logical coherence with accepted philosophical frameworks. The Cross satisfies neither demand in the expected way, and yet exceeds both.
- **"Christ crucified: a stumbling block (*skandalon*) to Jews":** *Skandalon* is the word for a trap's trigger — something that causes a fall. [Deuteronomy 21:23](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut21.23) decreed that "anyone hung on a tree is under God's curse." A cursed Messiah was not merely surprising; it was a theological contradiction. The disciples' own repeated rejection of the passion predictions ([Mark 8:31–32](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mark8.31); [9:30–32](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mark9.30); [10:32–34](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mark10.32)) shows how deep this revulsion ran — even among those who loved Jesus.
- **"Foolishness to Gentiles":** Rome reserved crucifixion for slaves and traitors. To follow a crucified man was social and intellectual suicide — the ancient equivalent of building a movement around an executed war criminal.
- **"But to those who are called — Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God":** The Greek *dynatois* (to the called) is passive — calling is received, not achieved. The reversal: what appears as weakness is strength; what appears as foolishness is wisdom. This is not a rhetorical trick but a theological claim about how reality is structured by God.

### [1 Corinthians 1:26–29](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.26) — The Sociology of Grace

- **Context:** Paul invites the Corinthians to look at themselves as evidence of how grace works. This is unusual apologetics — not philosophical argument but sociological observation.
- Paul's point: "not many wise according to the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called" (v. 26, NKJV). The church at Corinth was mostly composed of the powerless, lower classes, freed slaves.
- **"God has chosen the foolish things of the world to shame the wise":** *Kataischynō* — to put to shame, to humiliate. God's methodology in the Cross is consistently counterintuitive: the last first, the weak strong, the excluded included, the cursed blessed. This is not romanticism about the poor; it is an argument about how God works to prevent human boasting.
- **"That no flesh should boast in His presence" (v. 29):** The entire economy of grace — Cross, calling, community — is designed to eliminate the competitive pride that was tearing Corinth apart. This is the direct structural link between the theology of the Cross (1:18–25) and the sociology of the church (1:26–31).

### [Colossians 1:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Col1.20) and [1 Peter 2:24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Pet2.24) — What the Cross Accomplishes

- **[Col. 1:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Col1.20):** *apokatallaxai* (to reconcile fully) — the prefix *apo-* intensifies the action, indicating complete, thorough restoration, not partial repair. "Through the blood of his cross" — the instrument is specific. The scope is cosmic: "all things, whether things on earth or things in heaven." The Cross is not only about human salvation; it addresses a disruption that reached the entire created order.
- **[1 Pet. 2:24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Pet2.24):** "He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree (*xylon* — wood/tree)." Peter deliberately echoes the LXX of [Deuteronomy 21:23](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut21.23) (*xylon* for "tree"), reframing the curse: the instrument of divine judgment becomes the instrument of divine liberation. The *skandalon* becomes the *sotēria* (salvation). The tree of shame becomes the tree of sanctification — "that we might die to sin and live to righteousness."

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Theology of the Cross

1. **Paul says the Cross can be "emptied of its power" by the very act of eloquent proclamation ([1 Cor. 1:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.17)) — meaning a message can be technically accurate and simultaneously fatal to its own content.** Have you experienced preaching or teaching so polished that you engaged with ideas rather than with the crucified Christ? What is the difference between thinking *about* the Cross and actually *encountering* it?

2. **The Cross, Paul says, displays both "the worst of man and the best of God" in a single event.** What specifically does the Cross reveal about human nature that nothing else does? Why does it take an *execution* — not a betrayal or a denial or a theological argument — to show us who we really are?

3. **EGW writes that Paul's personal revelation of the Cross produced "a marvelous transformation... bringing all his plans and purposes into harmony with heaven."** Is transformation through beholding the Cross the normative Christian experience, or is Paul's Damascus-road encounter exceptional? What does it take for the Cross to produce ongoing transformation rather than settled intellectual assent?

4. **The Cross is described as *mōria* — not intellectual error but the posture of a creature in rebellion against God.** What is the difference between the person who says "I don't believe in the Cross" intellectually and the person who says "I believe" but lives in ways that functionally deny it? Which is more dangerous, and why?

### On Jews, Greeks, and Modern Audiences

5. **"Jews demand signs, Greeks seek wisdom" ([1 Cor. 1:22](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.22)) — two ways of demanding that God perform on human terms before receiving allegiance.** What are the modern equivalents in your community? Does your congregation primarily demand from religion — experiential proof, intellectual coherence, therapeutic benefit, social belonging? How does the Cross fail to deliver on those terms — until it radically exceeds them?

6. **The Cross was a *skandalon* for Jews because of [Deuteronomy 21:23](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut21.23) — the cursed one hung on a tree. Paul reframes the curse as the site of redemption, the tree of judgment as the tree of liberation.** Where in Adventist theology do we have similar reframes — doctrines that appear offensive or strange until the biblical logic becomes clear? How do we communicate those reframes without first removing the scandal that makes the reframe surprising?

7. **For the Greeks, a crucified God was philosophically incoherent — divine impassibility (God cannot suffer) was a foundational axiom. The God who dies is an oxymoron.** How do you articulate the Incarnation and the Cross to someone with a secular or philosophical worldview? What does it take for the Cross to move from "absurd" to "the most coherent thing I've ever heard"?

### On the Sociology of Grace

8. **"Not many wise, not many mighty, not many noble, are called" ([1 Cor. 1:26](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.26)) — Paul notes the demographic reality of the early church, then treats it as theological data.** Why might the Cross be more accessible — more believable, more saving — to those at the bottom of social hierarchies than to those at the top? What does this suggest about the relationship between power and receptivity to grace?

9. **The entire logic of the Cross — its upside-down economy — is designed so "that no flesh should boast" ([1 Cor. 1:29](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.29)).** Yet Adventist institutional culture can be highly status-conscious — credentials, conference positions, academic titles. How do we reconcile that with the Cross's leveling effect? Is there a version of institutional structure that is Cross-shaped, or does institutional structure inevitably drift toward the Corinthian status game?

10. **Paul's argument moves from theology (the Cross, 1:18–25) to sociology (the church's composition, 1:26–31) as *evidence* for how God works.** What would it mean to read your congregation's demographics as theological evidence right now? What would they reveal about where grace has been working — and where it hasn't?

### On Proclamation and Method

11. **Paul chose NOT to use "lofty speech or wisdom" at Corinth ([1 Cor. 2:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor2.1)), but DID use philosophical categories extensively at Athens ([Acts 17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts17)). He is clearly capable of both, and the difference is not competence.** What governs the choice? Is it audience, content, prior failure, spiritual discernment — or something else? What does that teach us about contextualization and its limits?

12. **The Cross is described as *both* foolishness *and* power — depending on where the hearer stands. The message is identical; the reception differs entirely.** What does this say about the role of human will versus divine grace in the hearing of the gospel? Can we "make" the Cross sound persuasive to someone who is perishing, or is the transformation of reception itself a work of the Spirit that precedes understanding?

13. **[Acts 13:16–47](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts13.16) shows Paul preaching the Cross in Pisidian Antioch by doing exegesis of Israel's entire history — the Exodus, the wilderness, the judges, David — and arriving at the Cross as its fulfillment.** How important is the biblical-narrative framework for making the Cross intelligible? Can the Cross be proclaimed effectively to people with no biblical background at all, or does the story have to precede the climax?

### On the Meaning of the Cross

14. **[Colossians 1:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Col1.20) says Christ reconciled "all things" — things in heaven and on earth — through the blood of his Cross. The scope is explicitly cosmic, not merely individual.** How does this cosmic dimension change the way we talk about the Cross? Does Adventism adequately present the cosmic Cross, or do we tend to reduce it to "Jesus died for my sins" — which, while true, is less than Paul's full claim?

15. **[1 Peter 2:24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Pet2.24) deliberately echoes the curse of [Deuteronomy 21:23](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut21.23) (*xylon* = tree) and reframes it: the tree of cursing becomes the site of liberation.** This typological reinterpretation takes a text that pointed *against* the Cross and reads it as pointing *toward* it. Are we allowed to do the same kind of reading in our own proclamation? Where does legitimate typological reading end and eisegesis begin?

### Harder Questions

16. **EGW says Paul knew his message "would naturally call forth ridicule and opposition" — and he preached it anyway, entering Corinth "in weakness and fear and much trembling" ([1 Cor. 2:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor2.3)).** In an era of online reputation management, social pressure, and cultural gatekeeping, what does it mean to preach a crucified Christ without softening the scandal? Where do Adventists most consistently sand off the rough edges of the Cross to make it more socially acceptable?

17. **"The foolishness of God is wiser than men" ([1 Cor. 1:25](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.25)) — Paul frames this as a *comparative*, but seems to intend something stronger than degree: divine wisdom is different in *kind*, not just in quantity.** What is the qualitative difference between divine wisdom and human wisdom? Human wisdom solves problems within the existing framework of reality; what does divine wisdom do differently, and why does it require the Cross to demonstrate it?

18. **The Cross exposes the bankruptcy of two ancient knowledge systems — Jewish sign-seeking and Greek wisdom-seeking — and replaces them with a third epistemology entirely.** If you had to name the epistemological principle of the Cross in one sentence — how it "knows" and what it reveals — what would you say? And how does that Cross-epistemology change how you read your life?

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. On Why Paul Changed His Approach

> *"He determined to avoid elaborate arguments and discussions, and 'not to know anything' among the Corinthians 'save Jesus Christ, and Him crucified.'"*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 244

**Context:** EGW is explaining Paul's deliberate strategic shift after Athens. His philosophical approach there won few converts, and he entered Corinth with a different resolve — not less intelligent, but Cross-centered rather than argument-centered.

**Discussion prompt:** This is not anti-intellectualism — it is strategic Cross-centricity. What does it look like in a Sabbath School class that prides itself on theological sophistication to say, with Paul, "I resolved to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified"? What would actually have to change in how we study together?

---

### 2. On Paul's Personal Transformation through the Cross

> *"Ever since he had been arrested in his career of persecution against the followers of the crucified Nazarene he had never ceased to glory in the cross. At that time there had been given him a revelation of the infinite love of God, as revealed in the death of Christ; and a marvelous transformation had been wrought in his life, bringing all his plans and purposes into harmony with heaven."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Acts of the Apostles*, pp. 245, 246

**Context:** EGW is establishing why Paul had the authority to preach the Cross — not academic credentials but personal experience of its power. He was exhibit A of what the Cross does to a human life.

**Discussion prompt:** Paul's credibility as a preacher of the Cross was his own transformation — he was the evidence. What makes a Sabbath School teacher or church member credible when they speak about the Cross? Can the Cross be taught effectively by someone who has not personally been apprehended by it? What does being "apprehended by the Cross" actually look like?

---

### 3. On the Mechanism of Transformation

> *"He knew by personal experience that when a sinner once beholds the love of the Father, as seen in the sacrifice of His Son, and yields to the divine influence, a change of heart takes place, and henceforth Christ is all and in all."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 246

**Context:** EGW describes "beholding" — sustained contemplative attention to the Cross, not momentary emotional reaction — as the mechanism by which the Cross produces transformation. This connects to her broader theology of character formation through beholding (*[2 Cor. 3:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.2Cor3.18)*).

**Discussion prompt:** "Beholding" implies sustained, returning, deliberate attention — not a one-time crisis experience. What does it look like in practice to regularly "behold" the Cross in a way that produces ongoing transformation? How is this different from reading theology about the Cross, or celebrating it at communion, or referencing it in prayer?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

### Individual

- **Cross-contemplation exercise:** Set aside 20 minutes this week with no agenda except to sit with the description of the crucifixion in one Gospel (try [Mark 15:1–39](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mark15.1) — the shortest, starkest account). No commentary, no analysis. Just read it slowly and let the details land. Afterward, write one sentence about what you noticed that you usually rush past.
- **Locate your own *mōria*:** Paul identifies foolishness as the posture of a creature in rebellion against God. Where in your life are you currently organizing things on a basis other than the Cross — status, self-protection, reputation management, performance? Name it specifically and bring it to the Cross this week.
- **Demographics as theological data:** Make a list of the five most significant relationships in your life right now. Where did each of them come from — social similarity, shared history, shared class? What would the Cross-logic of [1 Cor. 1:26–29](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.26) suggest about who is missing?

### Sabbath School

- **"Empty the Cross" diagnosis:** Discuss as a class: in the past year, what has occupied the center of your Sabbath School conversations — the Cross, or the things that circle around it (prophecy timelines, church politics, theological debates, lifestyle issues)? Is there a way to run a lesson without the Cross being the explicit lens? What would "re-centering on the Cross" actually change?
- **Roleplay the audiences:** Assign class members to represent a 1st-century Jew, a Corinthian Greek philosopher, and a freed slave at Corinth. Have each one respond to the simple statement "The Messiah was crucified and rose from the dead." Then discuss: what does each person need to hear *next*? What does that reveal about contextualization?
- **EGW on beholding:** Read the three EGW quotes together. Ask: what is the common thread? (Answer: *encounter* — not information transfer but personal revelation.) How does that change how we structure a Sabbath School lesson? What would a lesson look like if its goal were beholding rather than covering content?

### Intellectual

- **The epistemology question:** [Hebrews 11:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Heb11.1) (last week's memory text) calls faith *elenchos* — evidence, legal proof. Now Paul calls the Cross *mōria* — foolishness — to those outside. How do these two claims coexist? Is the Cross's "foolishness" a failure of evidence, or is it that the evidence is structurally inaccessible to a certain posture of the knower? What would it take for the Cross to become "coherent" to someone who currently finds it absurd?
- **Kenosis as paradigm:** The verb *kenoō* (to empty) appears in both [Phil. 2:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Phil2.7) (Christ emptying himself in Incarnation) and implicitly in [1 Cor. 1:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.17) (rhetoric *emptying* the Cross). Paul's own ministry style — no lofty speech, entering in weakness and fear — is a further *kenōsis*. Trace the kenotic logic: how does self-emptying become the structural principle of both salvation and proclamation? What does a kenotic Sabbath School lesson look like?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **The Cross as epistemological event:** The Cross does not just forgive sin — it reorders what counts as wisdom and power. This is a challenge to every human system of value-making, including religious ones. Corinthian Christianity had already reinstated a pre-Cross hierarchy of status and eloquence; Paul's letter is the Cross challenging that reinstatement.
- **Kenosis and proclamation:** The Cross is the paradigm for ministry: self-emptying, not self-displaying. Paul's refusal to use rhetorical power is a cruciform pastoral act — the form of his ministry embodies its content.
- **Cosmic reconciliation:** [Colossians 1:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Col1.20) expands the Cross beyond individual salvation to cosmic restoration — the entire creation reconciled through Christ's blood. This connects to the Great Controversy framework: the Cross is not only about forgiven humans but about the vindication of God's character before the entire universe.
- **Grace as social force:** The Corinthian congregation's demographics (mostly lower class) are a sociological demonstration of the Cross's logic — grace bypasses the powerful to shame them, gathers the weak to reveal divine strength. The *church as community* is meant to be a visible argument for the Cross's reality.
- **The scandal as invitation:** The Cross's offensiveness is not a bug to be fixed through better communication — it is the feature that separates genuine response from mere social or intellectual agreement. A Cross without scandal has been emptied of its *kenōthē*.
- **Apollymi — self-destruction:** The Greek of [1 Cor. 1:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor1.18) (*apollymi*, "perishing") can also be translated "destroying themselves." Those who reject the Cross are not simply waiting for God's judgment — they are already in the process of self-destruction. The Cross is not an external imposition but the only alternative to a trajectory that is already in motion.
