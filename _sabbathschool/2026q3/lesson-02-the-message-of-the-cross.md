## Central Theme

The Cross is not simply one doctrine among many — it is Paul's answer to *every* problem in the Corinthian church. Divisions, immorality, lawsuits, spiritual pride: each disorder, in Paul's diagnosis, is a symptom of the same disease — the eclipse of the Cross. This lesson asks a question that cuts deeper than historical theology: **Why does the Cross seem like foolishness, and what does that reveal about the human condition?**

Paul is not arguing that the Cross is counterintuitive but ultimately rational once you understand it. He is arguing that the Cross exposes the limits of human rationality itself. The category of *mōria* (foolishness) is not an intellectual deficit but a moral and relational one — the posture of a creature in rebellion against its Creator, for whom the very idea of a crucified God is offensive rather than redemptive. The Corinthians' factionalism and status games were not incidental; they were the social expression of this deeper foolishness — the refusal to organize life around the shame and grace of the Cross.

## Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 1:17–18 — *Sophia Logou* and *Mōria*
- **"Not with wisdom of words (*sophia logou*)":** This phrase does not condemn eloquence per se but the substitution of rhetorical performance for the scandalous content of the Cross. Paul refuses to be a sophist of the gospel.
- **"Lest the cross of Christ be emptied (*kenōthē*) of its power":** The verb *kenoō* — to empty, to make void — is the same root as the famous *kenōsis* in Philippians 2:7. Elegant rhetoric *empties* the Cross the way Paul uses the word; Christ's self-emptying was the source of salvation, but rhetorical self-filling evacuates it.
- **"The message of the cross is foolishness (*mōria*)":** Paul uses this word six times in 1 Cor. 1:18–31. It is morally laden stupidity — the kind of thinking that leads to self-destruction.

### 1 Corinthians 1:22–24 — Jews, Greeks, and the Crucified Messiah
- **"Jews seek a sign, Greeks seek wisdom":** Two epistemological postures — miraculous proof and logical coherence. The Cross satisfies neither demand in the expected way.
- **"Christ crucified: a stumbling block (*skandalon*) to Jews":** Deuteronomy 21:23 decreed that "anyone hung on a tree is under God's curse." A cursed Messiah was a contradiction in terms.
- **"But to those who are called — Christ the power and wisdom of God":** The passive *dynatois* (to the called) indicates that calling is received, not achieved. The reversal: weakness is strength; foolishness is wisdom.

### 1 Corinthians 1:26–29 — The Sociology of Grace
- Paul invites the Corinthians to look at themselves as evidence: "not many wise, not many mighty, not many noble, are called" (v. 26). The church was mostly lower-class, freed slaves.
- **"God has chosen the foolish things of the world to shame the wise":** *Kataischynō* — to humiliate. God's methodology is consistently counterintuitive: the last first, the weak strong. Not romanticism about the poor, but a claim about how God eliminates human boasting.
- **"That no flesh should boast in His presence" (v. 29):** The entire economy of grace — Cross, calling, community — is designed to eliminate the competitive pride tearing Corinth apart.

## Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Theology of the Cross
1. **Paul says the Cross can be "emptied of its power" by eloquent proclamation (1 Cor. 1:17). Have you experienced preaching so polished that you engaged with ideas rather than with the crucified Christ? What is the difference between thinking *about* the Cross and actually encountering it?**

2. **The Cross reveals both "the worst of man and the best of God." What specifically does the Cross reveal about human nature that nothing else does? Why does it take an execution to show us who we really are?**

3. **EGW writes that Paul's personal encounter with the Cross produced "a marvelous transformation... bringing all his plans and purposes into harmony with heaven." Is transformation through beholding the Cross the normative Christian experience, or exceptional? What does it take for the Cross to produce transformation rather than just intellectual assent?**

4. **The Cross is described as *mōria* in a morally-laden sense — not intellectual error but the posture of a creature in rebellion. What is the difference between the person who says "I don't believe in the Cross" intellectually and the person who says "I believe" but lives in ways that functionally deny it?**

### On Jews, Greeks, and Modern Audiences
5. **"Jews demand signs, Greeks seek wisdom" (1 Cor. 1:22). What are the modern equivalents? What does your community demand from religion — experiential proof, intellectual coherence, therapeutic benefit, social belonging? And how does the Cross fail to deliver on those terms — until it doesn't?**

6. **The Cross was a *skandalon* for Jews because of the Deuteronomy 21:23 curse. Paul reframes the curse as the site of redemption. Where in Adventist theology do we have similar reframes — doctrines that appear strange until the biblical logic becomes clear?**

7. **For the Greeks, a crucified God was philosophically incoherent — divine impassibility was a foundational assumption. How do you articulate the Incarnation and the Cross to someone with a philosophical worldview? What makes it "cohere" for those to whom it has become power?**

### On the Sociology of Grace
8. **"Not many wise, not many mighty, not many noble, are called" (1 Cor. 1:26). Why might the Cross be more accessible — more believable, more saving — to those at the bottom of social hierarchies than to those at the top? What does this suggest about power and receptivity to grace?**

9. **The entire logic of the Cross is designed so "that no flesh should boast" (1 Cor. 1:29). Yet Adventist institutional culture can be highly status-conscious. How do we reconcile that with the Cross's leveling effect?**

10. **Paul treats the demographics of the Corinthian church as theological data — evidence of how grace works. What would a similar reading of your congregation's demographics reveal about how God has been working?**

### On Proclamation and Method
11. **Paul chose NOT to use "lofty speech or wisdom" at Corinth, but DID use philosophical categories at Athens. He is clearly capable of both. What governs the choice? What does that teach us about contextualization vs. content?**

12. **The Cross is *both* foolishness *and* power — depending on where you stand. Same message, different reception. What does this say about the role of human will and divine grace in the hearing of the gospel? Can we "make" the Cross sound persuasive to someone who is perishing, or is the transformation of reception itself a work of the Spirit?**

13. **Acts 13:16–47 shows Paul preaching the Cross through Old Testament narrative — Israel's history arriving at Christ. Can the Cross be proclaimed effectively to people with no biblical background at all? Or does the narrative framework matter?**

### Harder Questions
14. **Colossians 1:20 says Christ reconciled "all things" through the blood of his Cross. The scope is cosmic. How does this cosmic dimension change how we talk about the Cross? Does Adventism adequately present the cosmic Cross, or do we default to an individualistic framing?**

15. **EGW says Paul knew his message "would naturally call forth ridicule and opposition." He preached it anyway. In an era of social pressure and reputational risk, what does it mean to preach a crucified Christ without softening the scandal?**

16. **"The foolishness of God is wiser than men" (1 Cor. 1:25). Paul seems to argue that divine wisdom is qualitatively different from human wisdom, not just quantitatively superior. What is the qualitative difference?**

## Practical Application

**Individual:** Identify one place in your spiritual life where you have substituted thinking *about* the Cross for actually encountering it. What would it mean to let the raw, scandalous reality of the Cross speak to you directly — not through analysis, but through contemplation?

**In Sabbath School:** Paul's message produced ridicule from Greeks and offense from Jews. What would produce the same reaction in your community if you said it directly? That friction may indicate you are near the Cross. Discuss: are there ways your class softens the Cross to make it more palatable?

**Intellectually:** Read 1 Corinthians 1:18–31 as a sociological argument. Paul invites the Corinthians to look at the church's demographics as theological evidence. What would a similar reading of your congregation reveal?

## Theological Threads

- **The Cross as epistemological event:** The Cross reorders what counts as wisdom and power — a challenge to every human system of value-making.
- **Kenosis and proclamation:** The Cross is the paradigm for ministry: self-emptying, not self-displaying. Paul's refusal to use rhetorical power is a cruciform pastoral act.
- **Cosmic reconciliation:** Colossians 1:20 expands the Cross to cosmic restoration — all creation reconciled through Christ's blood.
- **Grace as social force:** The Corinthian congregation's demographics are a sociological demonstration of the Cross's logic.
- **The scandal as invitation:** The Cross's offensiveness is not a bug to be fixed through better communication — it is the feature that separates genuine response from mere agreement.

## Lesson Summary

**Core assertion:** The Cross is simultaneously foolishness (to those in rebellion against God) and the power of God (to those being saved) — not because the message changes, but because the hearer's posture does.

**Key tensions:**
1. The Cross is foolishness ↔ The Cross is the power of God
2. Eloquent proclamation ↔ Proclamation that empties the Cross of power
3. Individual salvation ↔ Cosmic reconciliation
4. The scandal as stumbling block ↔ The scandal as the mechanism of grace

**The bottom line:** Every Corinthian problem was a symptom of the eclipse of the Cross. Paul's answer was not better church management but re-centering all of church life on "Jesus Christ and Him crucified." It is the same answer for every church in every century, including ours.
