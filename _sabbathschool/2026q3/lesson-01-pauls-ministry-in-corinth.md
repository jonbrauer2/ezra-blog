## 🔑 Central Theme

This opening lesson establishes the interpretive key for the entire quarter: **the gospel of the Cross is the lens through which every challenge in the church must be evaluated.** Paul did not arrive at Corinth with a strategy of cultural accommodation or rhetorical brilliance — he arrived, in his own words, "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" (1 Cor. 2:3, NKJV), determined to know nothing except Christ crucified. This was not timidity; it was theological conviction. The Corinthian church was an ecclesiastical disaster zone — factionalism, sexual immorality, lawsuits between members, distorted worship, theological confusion about the resurrection. And yet Paul's answer to every pathology was not a management technique. It was the message of the Cross.

The lesson also introduces us to the courage geography of missions: Paul moving from Athens (limited fruit, philosophical engagement) to Corinth (extended stay, significant church planting), sustained not by results but by a divine vision — *"I have many people in this city"* (Acts 18:10, NKJV). The church exists before it gathers. God's elect are already present in every city; the missionary's task is to find them through proclamation. This prevenient grace — God's work in hearts before the preacher arrives — is the theological foundation of every evangelistic effort.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 1:1 — *Klētos apostolos* (Called Apostle)

- Paul opens with *klētos apostolos Iēsou Christou dia thelēmatos Theou* — "a called apostle of Jesus Christ through the will of God." The word *klētos* (called) is the same root as *ekklēsia* (church) — those who are "called out." Paul's apostleship and the church's identity share the same vocabulary of divine summons.
- **By contrast with Galatians 1:1:** In Galatians Paul emphasizes "not from men, nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father." The Corinthian opening is gentler — but the authority claim is identical. His calling is vertical, not horizontal; ecclesiastical endorsement is secondary.
- **Significance:** Paul models that authentic ministry derives from calling, not charisma, community approval, or credentials. This matters enormously in a church like Corinth, where status and eloquence were currency.

### Acts 18:9, 10 — The Night Vision at Corinth

- **Context:** This vision comes at a moment of near-despair. Paul had just been driven from Thessalonica (Acts 17:10), barely escaped Berea (17:13–14), preached with modest response at Athens (17:32–34), and arrived at Corinth "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" (1 Cor. 2:3). He was contemplating leaving — again.
- **"Do not be afraid, but speak" (*mē phobou, alla lalei*):** The construction is emphatic — the present imperative of *laleō* (keep on speaking), not just one speech. This is a commission to sustained proclamation, not a single sermon.
- **"I have many people in this city":** The verb is *eimi* (present tense — I have, right now), not "I will gather." God's election is already real before conversion is visible. Paul's preaching does not create God's people; it reveals them. This is a profound theological statement about prevenient grace.
- **Echo of Isaiah 41:10:** "Fear not, for I am with you" — the same divine *Immanuel* promise. Christ is repeating to Paul what God spoke to Israel in exile. Paul's missionary suffering is framed within Israel's story.

### Acts 17:16–34 — Athens and the Limits of Apologetics

- Paul's Areopagus address is his most culturally sophisticated apologetic — engaging Stoic and Epicurean philosophy, quoting Greek poets (Aratus: "For we are also his offspring"). The response: some mocked, a few believed.
- **Contrast with Corinth:** At Corinth, Paul resolves to know nothing except Christ crucified (1 Cor. 2:2) — a deliberate strategic shift away from the Athens approach. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is theological prioritization. The Cross is not one argument among many; it is the center around which all arguments orbit.
- **Lesson for methodology:** Cultural engagement has its place, but it cannot be the engine of gospel proclamation. Athens illustrates the ceiling of contextualization without *kerygma* (proclamation).

### 1 Corinthians 1:18 — *Ho logos tou staurou* (The Word of the Cross)

- *Tois men apollumenois mōria estin, tois de sōzomenois hēmin dunamis theou estin* — "To those who are perishing it is foolishness, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God." The participles are present tense — perishing and being saved are ongoing processes, not completed states.
- **"Foolishness" (*mōria*):** The root gives us the English "moron." To the Greek rhetorical culture of Corinth, which prized *sophia* (wisdom) and *logos* (eloquent speech), a crucified Messiah was intellectually contemptible. Paul refuses to soften the offense.
- **"Power of God" (*dunamis theou*):** The same word Paul uses in Romans 1:16 — "I am not ashamed of the gospel, for it is the *dunamis* of God for salvation." The Cross is not one topic alongside creation, prophecy, and lifestyle — it is the generative center.

### 1 Corinthians 5:9–11 — The Lost Letter

- Paul references an earlier letter ("I wrote to you in my letter") that is no longer extant — a reminder that canonical Scripture is not exhaustive of Paul's pastoral correspondence, and that the earliest churches operated with a developing sense of what "Scripture" meant.
- **Significance for the Corinthian correspondence:** 1 Corinthians is likely the second letter, not the first. The church's problems were chronic, not situational. Paul's pastoral patience is remarkable — he keeps writing, keeps engaging, keeps loving a congregation that repeatedly disappoints him.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On Calling and Identity

1. **Paul identifies himself as an apostle "through the will of God" — not through church appointment, community vote, or personal ambition. How does a Christian discern the difference between a God-initiated calling and a self-generated one?** What are the markers that distinguish authentic divine call from gifted self-direction?

2. **Paul knew exactly who he was and what he was called to do. Most Christians today do not have that clarity — and many have stopped looking. What is the primary obstacle: spiritual dullness, fear, lack of formation, or absence of community to confirm the call?** And what is the practical remedy?

3. **Paul's calling survived floggings, imprisonment, shipwreck, and rejection by his own people. What makes a calling durable at that level of opposition?** Is "call" something you settle once, or something you must return to again and again to keep from quitting?

### On the Cross as Hermeneutic

4. **Paul determined to know nothing among the Corinthians "except Jesus Christ and Him crucified" (1 Cor. 2:2). In practice, what does it mean to bring the Cross as the interpretive lens to the specific problems a local church faces — factionalism, immorality, lawsuits, theological confusion?** How does the Cross actually solve those problems, not just inspire people to try harder?

5. **The quarter's thesis is that the Cross is "the lens through which everything else is to be judged." What happens in a church when something else becomes that lens — cultural relevance, institutional survival, social justice, doctrinal purity?** What does Adventist history show us about which substitutes are most tempting for us?

6. **"I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Cor. 2:2, ESV). Is Paul being reductive?** Is there not more to Christian proclamation — resurrection, parousia, the law, the Sanctuary? How does the Cross relate to those other doctrines without collapsing them into a single note?

### On Missionary Courage and Fear

7. **Paul admitted he was in Corinth "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling." This is the greatest church-planting apostle in history. What does it mean that authentic, Spirit-empowered ministry can coexist with fear?** Does fear disqualify a person from mission, or does pressing through fear while afraid constitute the real act of courage?

8. **God's encouragement to Paul was not "the results will be spectacular" — it was "I have many people in this city." Why is it significant that God grounds Paul's courage in divine election rather than in promised outcomes?** How should this reframe the way we measure missionary success — in our own lives and in institutional Adventist mission?

9. **Paul nearly quit Corinth — and there are times a missionary should leave. How do you discern the difference between a Spirit-prompted repositioning and a fear-driven retreat?** What signals — from God, from the community, from your own interior state — would indicate it is time to dig in rather than move on?

### On Corinth as Mirror

10. **Corinth: wealthy, cosmopolitan, sexually licentious, religiously pluralistic, status-obsessed, with members suing each other in secular courts. In what specific ways is your church community — not "society in general" but your actual congregation — not unlike the Corinthian church?** What problems of Paul's church do you recognize closest to home?

11. **Paul saw Corinth's geographic and commercial centrality as a missionary opportunity rather than a threat. Are Adventists today inclined to view urban centers primarily as opportunity or primarily as moral danger?** What does that instinct — whichever direction it leans — reveal about our underlying theology of mission?

12. **The Corinthian members were shaped more by their surrounding culture's competitiveness, status games, and sexual ethics than by the gospel. Where do you see Adventist church culture today being shaped more by surrounding society than by the Cross?** What would it take to name that honestly — by name, not abstractly — in a local church setting?

### On the Pastoral Letters

13. **Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, at a distance, responding to reports from Chloe's household and a written list of questions. This is crisis pastoral care conducted by letter across hundreds of miles. What are the irreplaceable limits of remote pastoral ministry?** Is the contemporary church's heavy use of technology for pastoral care more like Paul's letters or more like a substitute for incarnational presence?

14. **Ellen White calls 1 Corinthians "one of the richest, most instructive, most powerful of all his letters" — and it was written to address a deeply dysfunctional church. What does that tell us about the relationship between crisis and theological depth?** Do our best theology and most honest questions arise in times of peace or times of struggle?

15. **Paul may have written 3–4 letters to the Corinthians; two are lost. The Bible is a selective, curated collection, not an archive of everything inspired people wrote. What is your theology of biblical inspiration in light of that fact?** Does it trouble you that God allowed inspired apostolic correspondence to be lost? Why or why not?

### Harder Questions

16. **Paul's strategy shifted dramatically from Athens to Corinth — from philosophical engagement to stark crucifixion proclamation. Does this mean the apologetic method is inherently less effective than direct kerygmatic preaching?** Or is the contrast situational rather than principled — and how do we know which approach a given moment calls for?

17. **"I have many people in this city" — God's elect are in Corinth before Paul arrives. If this is true of every city, what are the concrete implications for Adventist outreach strategy?** Are we primarily looking for "our people" (those already oriented toward our distinctives), or are we participating in God's already-active mission of calling His people to Himself from every cultural background?

18. **Paul's letters were written as situation-specific pastoral correspondence and became universal canon. How does that transformation shape how we should read them?** Should we read for timeless principles, for historical particularity, or for both — and how do we decide which application to make in a given case?

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. On the Cross as the Only Strategy

> *"In preaching the gospel in Corinth, the apostle followed a course different from that which had marked his labors at Athens. . . . He determined to avoid elaborate arguments and discussions, and 'not to know anything' among the Corinthians 'save Jesus Christ, and Him crucified.'"*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 244

**Context:** EGW draws the explicit contrast between Paul's Athens method (philosophical engagement) and his deliberate pivot at Corinth. The change is intentional — Athens showed him the ceiling of apologetics without proclamation.

**Discussion prompt:** Where does your own faith community invest its primary persuasive energy — in intellectual argument, in cultural relevance, or in cruciform witness? If Paul looked at your church's outreach calendar for the past year, which city would he say it most resembles: Athens or Corinth?

---

### 2. On God's Hidden People

> *"Paul understood this to be a command to remain in Corinth, and a guarantee that the Lord would give increase to the seed sown. . . . A large church was enrolled under the banner of Jesus Christ."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Sketches from the Life of Paul*, pp. 106, 107

**Context:** EGW describes the aftermath of the night vision — Paul's interpretation of it as both a command to stay and a divine guarantee of harvest, even when the field looked discouraging.

**Discussion prompt:** The vision didn't promise an easy ministry — it promised a fruitful one. God guaranteed increase, not comfort. Are there places in your own ministry — at work, in your neighborhood, in your family — where you have been tempted to declare a field unfruitful when God may simply be saying "stay"?

---

### 3. On Corinth's Strategic Importance

> *"An opportunity was thus presented for the spread of the gospel. Once established at Corinth, it would be readily communicated to all parts of the world."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Sketches From the Life of Paul*, p. 99

**Context:** EGW frames Paul's choice of Corinth missiologically — its commercial position made it a gospel hub from which news could travel the entire Mediterranean world via its merchant networks.

**Discussion prompt:** EGW sees Corinth's moral chaos as the *setting* for a strategic opportunity, not as a reason to avoid it. What is the Corinth in your geography — the difficult, morally complex, status-obsessed center — that your church has been treating as a threat rather than a hub? What would it mean to plant the Cross there?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

### Individual

- **The "I have many people" exercise:** Identify one context in your life — a workplace, a neighborhood, a family relationship — where fear has functioned as your reason to remain silent or withdraw from gospel witness. Sit with Acts 18:10 and rewrite the sentence with your specific context: "I have many people in ___________." What changes when you believe that God's elect are already there, waiting?
- **Calling inventory:** Write one sentence describing your calling — not your job, not your role, not your volunteer slot, but your calling. If you cannot do it, spend 15 minutes this week asking: why not? Bring that question to prayer before Thursday.
- **Athens/Corinth self-audit:** Honestly assess your last three significant faith conversations with someone outside the church. Did they follow the Athens pattern (establishing common ground, philosophical bridge-building) or the Corinth pattern (direct proclamation of what Christ has done)? Which felt more natural — and does that reveal something worth examining?

### Sabbath School

- **"What keeps us here?" discussion:** Paul stayed in Corinth a year and a half, not because it was easy, but because God said to. Open the floor to honest answers: what keeps *this* Sabbath School class faithful when the church feels difficult, discouraging, or broken? Is it duty, love, habit, community, conviction — or something else? Name it rather than assume it.
- **Corinth-mirror exercise:** List five characteristics of the Corinthian church from this week's lesson (from the text, not just the study guide). Then list five characteristics of your own congregation. Where do the lists overlap? Discuss: is Paul's remedy for Corinthians — Christ crucified — the remedy you need, or does your church need something additional?
- **Two-city reading:** Read Acts 17:22–31 (Athens) and Acts 18:4–11 (Corinth) aloud together. Ask: what did Paul keep constant between the two cities? What did he change? What does that model for contextual faithfulness in your own community's witness?

### Intellectual

- **The epistemology of the Cross:** Paul calls the word of the Cross "foolishness" to the perishing and "power of God" to the saved. How is the same message categorically different depending on the hearer — without the message itself changing? Is this a psychological phenomenon, a theological one, or both? What does it imply about the limits of rational persuasion in evangelism?
- **Canon and correspondence:** Paul wrote at minimum four letters to the Corinthians; we have two. Research how the early church determined which writings were canonical. What principles of selection governed the process? Does knowing that inspired letters were lost affect your doctrine of Scripture's sufficiency?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **Apostleship as divine calling:** Paul's self-identification grounds an entire theology of vocation — every believer is *klētos*, called, not just ordained clergy. The question is not whether you are called but to what. *Klētos* and *ekklēsia* share the same root: the church is the community of the called.
- **The Cross as hermeneutic:** 1 Corinthians 2:2 is not just a preaching strategy — it is Paul's framework for evaluating all of church life. Every problem in the Corinthian correspondence is ultimately addressed by returning to what the Cross means for community, for status, for power, for bodies. This thread runs the entire quarter.
- **Prevenient grace:** "I have many people in this city" assumes that God's gracious election precedes and enables human proclamation. This is not Calvinist determinism but the recognition that God prepares hearts before missionaries arrive — foundational to Adventist missionary theology.
- **Canonical incompleteness:** The lost Corinthian letters remind us that canonical Scripture is a theological selection, not an archive. The Spirit guided preservation, not just production — a distinction with implications for how we understand both inspiration and the canon's sufficiency.
- **Urban mission:** Corinth as mission hub anticipates a theology of cities — that strategic, complex, morally challenging urban centers are precisely where the gospel must be planted with deepest roots.
