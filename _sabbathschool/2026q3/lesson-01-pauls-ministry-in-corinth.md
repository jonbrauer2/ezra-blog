## Central Theme

This opening lesson establishes the interpretive key for the entire quarter: **the gospel of the Cross is the lens through which every challenge in the church must be evaluated.** Paul did not arrive at Corinth with a strategy of cultural accommodation or rhetorical brilliance — he arrived, in his own words, "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" (1 Cor. 2:3, NKJV), determined to know nothing except Christ crucified. This was not timidity; it was theological conviction. The Corinthian church was an ecclesiastical disaster zone — factionalism, sexual immorality, lawsuits between members, distorted worship, theological confusion about the resurrection. And yet Paul's answer to every pathology was not a management technique. It was the message of the Cross.

The lesson also introduces us to the courage geography of missions: Paul moving from Athens (limited fruit, philosophical engagement) to Corinth (extended stay, significant church planting), sustained not by results but by a divine vision — *"I have many people in this city"* (Acts 18:10, NKJV). The church exists before it gathers. God's elect are already present in every city; the missionary's task is to find them through proclamation.

## Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 1:1 — *Klētos apostolos* (Called Apostle)
- Paul opens with *klētos apostolos Iēsou Christou dia thelēmatos Theou* — "a called apostle of Jesus Christ through the will of God." The word *klētos* (called) is the same root as *ekklēsia* (church) — those who are "called out." Paul's apostleship and the church's identity share the same vocabulary of divine summons.
- **By contrast with Galatians 1:1:** In Galatians Paul emphasizes "not from men, nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father." The Corinthian opening is gentler — but the authority claim is identical. His calling is vertical, not horizontal; ecclesiastical endorsement is secondary.
- **Significance:** Paul models that authentic ministry derives from calling, not charisma, community approval, or credentials. This matters enormously in a church like Corinth, where status and eloquence were currency.

### Acts 18:9, 10 — The Night Vision at Corinth
- **Context:** This vision comes at a moment of near-despair. Paul had just been driven from Thessalonica (Acts 17:10), barely escaped Berea (17:13–14), preached with modest response at Athens (17:32–34), and arrived at Corinth "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" (1 Cor. 2:3). He was contemplating leaving — again.
- **"Do not be afraid, but speak":** The construction is emphatic: *mē phobou, alla lalei* — the present imperative of *laleō* (keep on speaking), not just one speech. This is a commission to sustained proclamation, not a single sermon.
- **"I have many people in this city":** The verb is *eimi* (present tense — I have, right now), not "I will gather." God's election is already real before conversion is visible. Paul's preaching does not create God's people; it reveals them. This is a profound theological statement about prevenient grace.
- **Echo of Isaiah 41:10:** "Fear not, for I am with you" — the same divine *Immanuel* promise. Christ is repeating to Paul what God spoke to Israel in exile. Paul's missionary suffering is framed within Israel's story.

### Acts 17:16–34 — Athens and the Limits of Apologetics
- Paul's Areopagus address is his most culturally sophisticated apologetic — engaging Stoic and Epicurean philosophy, quoting Greek poets (Aratus: "For we are also his offspring"). The response: some mocked, a few believed.
- **Contrast with Corinth:** At Corinth, Paul resolves to know nothing except Christ crucified (1 Cor. 2:2) — a deliberate strategic shift away from the Athens approach. This is not anti-intellectualism; it is theological prioritization. The Cross is not one argument among many; it is the center around which all arguments orbit.
- **Lesson for methodology:** Cultural engagement has its place, but it cannot be the engine of gospel proclamation. Athens illustrates the ceiling of contextualization without kerygma.

## Deep Discussion Questions

### On Calling and Identity
1. **Paul identifies himself as an apostle "through the will of God" (1 Cor. 1:1), not through church appointment or personal ambition. How does a Christian discern the difference between a God-initiated calling and a self-generated one? What are the markers?**

2. **Paul knew exactly who he was and what he was called to do. Most Christians do not have that clarity. What do you think is the primary obstacle — spiritual dullness, fear, lack of formation, or something else? And what is the remedy?**

3. **Paul's calling survived extraordinary opposition — floggings, imprisonment, shipwreck, rejection by his own people. What does it take for a calling to be durable in that way? Is "call" something you settle once, or something you must keep returning to?**

### On the Cross as Hermeneutic
4. **Paul determined "to know nothing among" the Corinthians "except Jesus Christ and Him crucified" (1 Cor. 2:2). In practice, what does it mean to bring the Cross as the interpretive lens to the specific problems a local church faces — factionalism, immorality, theological confusion? How does the Cross actually address those problems, not just inspire people to try harder?**

5. **The quarter's thesis is that the Cross is "the lens through which everything else is to be judged." What happens in a church when something else becomes the lens — cultural relevance, institutional survival, social justice, doctrinal purity? What does Adventist history show us about which substitutes are most tempting?**

6. **"I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (1 Cor. 2:2, ESV). Is Paul being reductive? Is there not more to Christian proclamation than crucifixion — resurrection, parousia, the law, the Sanctuary? How does the Cross relate to those other doctrines without collapsing them?**

### On Missionary Courage and Fear
7. **Paul admitted he was in Corinth "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" (1 Cor. 2:3). This is the great apostle. What does it mean that authentic, Spirit-empowered ministry can coexist with fear? Does fear disqualify a person from mission? What does it take to press through?**

8. **God's encouragement to Paul was not "the results will be spectacular" — it was "I have many people in this city." Why is it significant that God grounds Paul's courage in divine election rather than in promised outcomes? How should this reframe how we measure missionary success?**

9. **Paul nearly quit Corinth. The lesson suggests there is a legitimate question about when a missionary should stay and when they should leave. How do you discern that? What signals — from God, from the community, from your own state — would indicate it's time to move on versus time to dig in?**

### On Corinth as Mirror
10. **Tuesday's lesson describes Corinth: wealthy, cosmopolitan, sexually licentious, religiously pluralistic, status-obsessed. In what specific ways is your city — or your church community — not unlike Corinth? What problems of the Corinthian church do you recognize in your own congregation?**

11. **Paul saw Corinth's geographic and commercial centrality as a missionary opportunity: "An opportunity was thus presented for the spread of the gospel" (EGW, *Sketches From the Life of Paul*, p. 99). Are Adventists today inclined to view urban centers primarily as opportunity or primarily as threat? What does that instinct reveal?**

12. **The Corinthian church members were heavily influenced by surrounding culture — its competitiveness, its status games, its tolerance of sexual immorality. Where do you see Adventist church culture being shaped more by the surrounding society than by the gospel? What would it take to name that honestly in a local church setting?**

### On the Pastoral Letters
13. **Paul wrote 1 Corinthians from Ephesus, at a distance, responding to reports and a written list of questions. This is crisis pastoral care by letter. What are the limits of pastoral ministry at a distance? What is irreplaceable about physical presence?**

14. **Ellen White calls 1 Corinthians "one of the richest, most instructive, most powerful of all his letters" (*The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 301). Given that it was written to address a deeply dysfunctional church, what does that tell us about the relationship between crisis and theological depth?**

15. **Paul may have written 3–4 letters to the Corinthians; two are lost. The Bible is a selective, curated collection, not an archive. What is your theology of biblical inspiration in light of the fact that inspired apostolic correspondence was sometimes lost?**

### Harder Questions
16. **Paul's strategy at Corinth contrasts sharply with his strategy at Athens. He moved away from philosophical engagement toward stark crucifixion proclamation. Does this mean the apologetic method is less effective than direct proclamation? Or is the contrast more situational than strategic?**

17. **"I have many people in this city" (Acts 18:10) — God's elect are in Corinth before Paul arrives. If this is true of every city, what are the implications for Adventist outreach strategy? Are we primarily looking for "our people," or participating in God's already-active mission?**

18. **Paul's letter writing was pastoral necessity. Historically, his letters became Scripture. How does that transformation — from situation-specific pastoral correspondence to universal canon — shape how we should read them?**

## Practical Application

**Individual:** Paul was "in weakness, in fear, and in much trembling" — and kept going. Identify one arena in your life where fear has functioned as a reason to remain silent or withdraw. What would it mean to hear God say, "I have many people in this city" about that specific context?

**In Sabbath School:** Paul stayed in Corinth a year and a half, not because it was easy, but because God said to. Discuss: **What keeps this Sabbath School class faithful when the church feels difficult, discouraging, or broken?**

**Intellectually:** Read Acts 17 (Athens) and 18:1–18 (Corinth) side by side. Same apostle, same gospel, different methodology. What does contextual faithfulness look like without compromising the content of the Cross?

## Theological Threads

- **Apostleship as divine calling:** Every believer is *klētos* — called. The question is not whether, but to what.
- **The Cross as hermeneutic:** 1 Corinthians 2:2 is Paul's framework for evaluating all of church life — this thread runs the entire quarter.
- **Prevenient grace:** "I have many people in this city" assumes God's gracious election precedes human proclamation. God prepares hearts before missionaries arrive.
- **Canonical incompleteness:** Lost Corinthian letters remind us that canonical Scripture is theological selection, not archive.
- **Urban mission:** Corinth as mission hub anticipates a theology of cities — complex, morally challenging urban centers are precisely where the gospel must be planted.

## Lesson Summary

**Core assertion:** Paul's ministry in Corinth was grounded not in cultural fit, rhetorical sophistication, or favorable conditions — but in the message of the Cross and a divine promise that God already had people in the city.

**Key tensions:**
1. Strategic flexibility (Athens vs. Corinth) ↔ theological constancy (always the Cross)
2. Personal fear and weakness ↔ apostolic boldness and persistence
3. The church's disorder ↔ God's faithfulness and hidden people
4. Situation-specific pastoral letters ↔ universal canonical authority

**The bottom line:** The same Christ who told a frightened Paul "do not be afraid, but speak" speaks the same commission to every generation. The city may be different. The fear is the same. So is the promise.
