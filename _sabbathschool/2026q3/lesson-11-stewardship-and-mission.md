## 🔑 Central Theme

Stewardship and mission are not two separate disciplines — they are two sides of a single coin struck in the image of Jesus Christ. Second Corinthians 8–9, Paul's most sustained treatment of Christian generosity, places the entire logic of giving within the orbit of the Incarnation: "He was rich … yet for your sakes He became poor" (2 Cor. 8:9). Before the collection for Judea is a financial strategy, it is a Christological statement.

The week moves through five interlocking themes: the *example* of Jesus (the supreme model of self-giving), the *motivation* for giving (grace received produces grace extended), the *planning* of giving (proportional, pre-decided, not coerced), the *attitude* of giving (joy, generosity, voluntary consecration), and the *unity* that giving produces (Jew and Gentile, rich and poor, joined as one family through shared sacrifice).

Running beneath all five themes is the conviction that "the language of heaven is one of giving" — and that Christian stewardship is the earthly grammar of that language. Giving is not primarily a financial discipline but a spiritual practice that forms us in the image of the self-giving God.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 2 Corinthians 8:9 (Memory Text — The Incarnation as Model)

- **Context:** Paul is motivating the Corinthian church to complete their pledged contribution to the Jerusalem relief fund. His primary appeal is not social guilt or peer pressure but theology: look at what Jesus did.
- **"Though He was rich" (*plousios ōn*):** The present participle *ōn* indicates continuous, ongoing being — Jesus *was* and remains rich by nature. His richness refers to pre-existent divine glory (John 17:5), the "wealth" of omnipotence, omniscience, and eternal fellowship with the Father.
- **"He became poor" (*eptōcheusen*):** The aorist indicative marks a decisive historical act — a moment of voluntary dispossession. The verb *ptōcheuō* comes from *ptōchos* (beggar, one who crouches, completely destitute). This is not relative poverty but abject, self-chosen destitution — the Word made flesh, born in a borrowed stable, dying on a borrowed cross.
- **"That you through His poverty might become rich" (*ploutēsēte*):** The subjunctive mood expresses purpose. The poverty was *instrumental*. Christ's dispossession is the mechanism of our enrichment. The economy of grace is the inversion of the economy of accumulation: he empties so that we may be filled.
- **Theological note:** This verse is one of the most compact summaries of the Incarnation in Paul's letters, paralleling the Christ-hymn of Philippians 2:6–8 and the prologue of John 1:1–14.

### 2 Corinthians 9:7 — *Proaireō* and Planned Giving

- **"What he has decided in his heart" (*proaireō*):** The Greek verb *proaireō* is a compound: *pro* (beforehand) + *aireō* (to choose, decide). The word carries the sense of deliberate prior commitment, not impulsive reaction. Giving, like prayer and fasting, is a practiced spiritual discipline requiring forethought.
- **"Not reluctantly or under compulsion" (*mē ek lupēs ē ex anankēs*):** *Lupē* (grief, reluctance) and *anankē* (necessity, coercion) are both excluded. Freedom is constitutive of genuine stewardship.
- **"God loves a cheerful giver" (*hilaron dotēn agapāi ho theos*):** *Hilaros* (from which English "hilarious" derives) appears only here in the NT. It carries overtones of gladness, brightness, even festivity. Giving is envisioned as a celebratory act — the cheerfulness of one who has grasped that everything they give is already a return on divine generosity.

### 2 Corinthians 8:1–5 — The Macedonian Model

- **"A severe test of affliction" and "extreme poverty" (v.2):** The Macedonian churches (Philippi, Thessalonica, Berea) were economically marginalized and facing active persecution. Their poverty and affliction produced a spiritual crucible in which joy and generosity became possible.
- **"They gave themselves first to the Lord" (v.5):** The Greek reads *heautous edōkan prōton tō Kyriō* — they gave *themselves* first. Financial generosity is the overflow of prior self-surrender. This sequence is non-negotiable in Paul's logic: you cannot give money redemptively before you have given yourself.
- **Haplotētos (generosity/simplicity, v.2):** The noun *haplotēs* carries the dual sense of generosity and singleness of heart — giving uncomplicated by divided allegiance, transparent in motivation, free of performance.

### John 3:16 — The Language of Heaven

- **"God so loved … that He *gave*" (*hōste … edōken*):** The connective *hōste* expresses result — love of this magnitude *necessarily* results in giving. The lesson's claim is that John 3:16 is the celestial original of which every Christian offering is an earthly copy.
- **Hebrew parallel — *nātan* (to give):** The OT vocabulary of giving (*nātan*) pervades the Pentateuch's theology of offerings. Israel's system of tithes and offerings was always a response to prior divine gift: "Of your own have we given you" (1 Chr. 29:14).

### Romans 15:26–27 — Unity Through the Offering

- **"It pleased them" (*eudokēsan*):** The verb *eudokeō* carries the sense of glad, willing decision. The Greek churches *chose* this.
- **"If the Gentiles have shared in their spiritual blessings, they ought also to serve them in material blessings" (v.27):** Paul articulates a theology of mutual indebtedness — returning material support is not charity but *reciprocal covenant faithfulness*. The offering bridges ethnic and class divisions, embodying the unity of the body of Christ.

### Revelation 13:8 — The Eternal Covenant of Self-Giving

- **"The Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" (*apo katabolēs kosmou*):** Christ's self-giving is not a late contingency plan — it was the eternal purpose of the triune God. Every act of giving is a temporal echo of an eternal reality.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Theology of Giving

1. **Second Corinthians 8:9 presents the Incarnation as the primary motivation for Christian stewardship.** How does grounding giving in Christology rather than obligation or social need change how you experience the act of giving? Have you ever made an offering that felt genuinely Christological — not dutiful, but imitative?

2. **Paul says the Macedonians gave "according to their means, and beyond their means, of their own free will" (2 Cor. 8:3, ESV).** When, if ever, is giving "beyond your means" (*para dunamin*) an act of faith rather than irresponsibility? How do you discern the difference?

3. **The lesson identifies four motivations for giving in 2 Corinthians 8–9: gratitude for grace, desire to follow Jesus' example, desire to share blessings, and sincere love.** Which of these four has most animated your own giving historically? Which do you find hardest to genuinely feel?

4. **Revelation 13:8 describes Christ as "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world."** What does it mean to give within a universe where self-gift is the foundational cosmic act? Does that reframe what it feels like to be "generous"?

### On Joy, Attitude, and the Interior Life of Giving

5. **Paul says God loves a *hilaros* giver — a word related to "hilarious."** Most of us would describe our giving as dutiful, perhaps generous, but rarely joyful enough to share a root with hilarity. What would it take to experience giving as genuinely festive?

6. **"They gave themselves first to the Lord, and then by the will of God to us" (2 Cor. 8:5).** Is it possible to give consistently and cheerfully without this prior self-surrender? What happens to your giving when your relationship with God is strained?

7. **The Macedonians gave with "abundant joy" in the context of "extreme poverty and severe trial" (2 Cor. 8:2).** Have you experienced a season of hardship in which you gave more freely, not less? What does that inversion reveal about the source of Christian joy?

8. **Second Corinthians 9:7 rules out both reluctance (*lupē*) and compulsion (*anankē*).** Honestly assess your own giving: are there categories where reluctance or compulsion rather than joy characterizes your experience? What does that reveal about your understanding of God's character?

### On Planning, Proportionality, and Accountability

9. **The verb *proaireō* ("decided in his heart") suggests genuine stewardship requires deliberate prior planning.** How intentional is your giving? Do you have a plan — a proportion, a budget, a decision made in advance — or is it largely reactive?

10. **While tithing is a fixed biblical proportion, offerings are proportional but self-determined ("according to what a person has, not what he does not have," 2 Cor. 8:12).** How do you navigate the difference between proportional generosity and self-justifying minimum compliance?

11. **Paul entrusted the collection to Titus and "two other brothers chosen by the churches" (2 Cor. 8:19–23) for transparency and accountability.** Why does financial accountability within church community matter? What does it protect — both the giver and the mission?

### On Unity and the Body of Christ

12. **Paul's collection was designed to create visible, tangible unity between Gentile and Jewish Christians.** Have you experienced a shared financial sacrifice — a mission project, a relief effort — that genuinely created community in a way sermons alone could not?

13. **Romans 15:27 frames Gentile support for Jewish Jerusalem as reciprocal obligation, not charity.** How does this reframe the relationship between "mission-sending" and "mission-receiving" churches in a global Adventist context?

14. **The lesson observes that "money can be a great unifier" but also a source of division.** Think of a time when the handling of money damaged community trust in a church or family context. What does that experience reveal about the relationship between financial practices and spiritual health?

### On the Stewardship of All of Life

15. **Ellen White writes: "Money, time, influence — all the gifts they have received from God's hand, they will value only as a means of advancing the work of the gospel."** Which of these three do you guard most jealously? What would it look like to surrender that resource more intentionally?

16. **Paul calls himself and his fellow workers "stewards of the mysteries of God" (1 Cor. 4:1).** How does this metaphor expand your understanding of stewardship beyond financial categories? What "mysteries" have been entrusted to you?

17. **The lesson's title joins *stewardship* and *mission* as inseparable.** Has stewardship in your experience felt more like internal household management or outward mission engagement? Is there a way in which separating them impoverishes both?

### Synthesis and Personal Challenge

18. **Second Corinthians 8:9 compresses the entire gospel into financial language: wealth, poverty, exchange, enrichment.** Some Christians are uncomfortable with wealth-language applied to spiritual realities. Is this discomfort healthy, or does it risk disconnecting the material and spiritual in unhelpful ways?

19. **The Macedonians "begged earnestly for the favor of taking part in the relief of the saints" (2 Cor. 8:4, ESV) — treating giving as a *privilege* they asked to be included in.** When did you last experience giving as privilege rather than duty? What spiritual posture produces that perception?

20. **As you reflect on this lesson, identify one specific, concrete change you could make to your practice of stewardship — not in the category of "give more" generally, but in the quality of how you give: the planning, the attitude, the intentionality, the joy.** What would that change require from you internally?

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. The Self-Giving Heart and the Gospel

> *"Those whose hearts are filled with the love of Christ, will follow the example of Him who for our sake became poor, that through His poverty we might be made rich. Money, time, influence—all the gifts they have received from God's hand, they will value only as a means of advancing the work of the gospel. Thus it was in the early church; and when in the church of today it is seen that by the power of the Spirit the members have taken their affections from the things of the world, and that they are willing to make sacrifices in order that their fellow men may hear the gospel, the truths proclaimed will have a powerful influence upon the hearers."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 71

**Context:** Written in the context of the early church's generosity as described in Acts, Ellen White connects interior spiritual transformation (affections detached from the world) with exterior missional impact. The church's generosity becomes an embodied proclamation.

**Discussion prompt:** Ellen White ties the *effectiveness of gospel proclamation* to the visible generosity of the church. Could a church's financial tightness actually undermine the credibility of the message it preaches?

---

### 2. The Return of What Was Never Ours

> *"The Lord does not need our offerings. We cannot enrich Him by our gifts. Says the psalmist: 'All things come of Thee, and of Thine own have we given Thee.' Yet God permits us to show our appreciation of His mercies by self-sacrificing efforts to extend the same to others. This is the only way in which it is possible for us to manifest our gratitude and love to God. He has provided no other."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Counsels on Stewardship*, pp. 18–19

**Context:** Ellen White dismantles any notion that God benefits from human giving — the logic of gift runs entirely in one direction first. Giving back is not transactional but *confessional*: it declares that what we hold was never ours to begin with.

**Discussion prompt:** If giving is "the only way in which it is possible for us to manifest our gratitude and love to God," what does that say about seasons in which our giving has been minimal or joyless? How do you distinguish between healthy conviction and guilt-driven compliance?

---

### 3. The Liberality That Exceeds All Others

> *"How great was the gift of God to man, and how like our God to make it! With a liberality that can never be exceeded he gave, that he might save the rebellious sons of men and bring them to see his purpose and discern his love. Will you, by your gifts and offerings, show that you think nothing too good for Him who 'gave his only begotten Son that whosoever believeth in him should not perish, but have everlasting life'?"*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Review and Herald*, May 15, 1900

**Context:** Ellen White frames giving as a response to divine liberality that "can never be exceeded," asking the personal and urgent question: *will you show* — by your gifts — that you understand the scale of what was given for you?

**Discussion prompt:** The phrase "nothing too good for Him" is a searching standard. Is there something specific in your life — a category of spending, a financial goal, a possession — that you have unconsciously exempted from this calculus?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

### Individual

1. **The Incarnation Giving Exercise:** Write out 2 Corinthians 8:9 in your own words — expanding each phrase into two or three sentences describing what Jesus gave up and what we gained. Then sit with the question: *Given this, what is difficult about giving?* Let the answer surface without judgment and bring it honestly to God in prayer.
2. **Plan your proportional offering:** Using the principle of *proaireō* (decided beforehand), write down one freewill offering decision you will make this quarter — decide the amount, the recipient, and the date now, not in the moment.
3. **Audit the interior life of giving:** For the next month, before each act of giving, pause and name your dominant feeling: joy, duty, reluctance, habit, guilt, or freedom. Review the pattern at month's end: what does it reveal about your understanding of grace?

### Sabbath School Class

1. **The Macedonian roleplay:** Divide the class — one group plays the Macedonian church (extreme poverty, severe trial, overflowing joy), the other the Corinthian church (past commitment, relationship tensions with Paul, materially comfortable). Have each articulate from within their situation why they are or aren't giving. Then discuss: what is Paul's actual argument to Corinth, and does it land?
2. **"Language of Heaven" word study:** Together, trace the word *charis* (grace) through 2 Corinthians 8–9, noting each occurrence and how the meaning shifts (divine grace → human graciousness → gratitude → the gift itself). What does it tell us about Paul's theology that the same word covers all these meanings?
3. **Unity mapping exercise:** Identify three contexts in which your local church has recently given — within the congregation, to a conference project, to international mission. For each, discuss: what kind of unity was this giving meant to create? Did it? What would need to change for giving to more visibly build community?

### Intellectual Challenge

1. **The ethics of proportionality:** Paul's model requires giving "according to what a person has" — but a ten-percent tithe from someone earning $30,000 and someone earning $300,000 represents radically different sacrifice and impact. Is a strictly proportional system genuinely equitable? Research how progressive tithing has been discussed in Adventist theology and economic ethics, and bring your conclusions to the group.
2. **Stewardship and justice:** Second Corinthians 8:14 invokes *isotēs* — equality or fairness — as the goal: "your abundance at the present time should supply their need, so that their abundance may supply your need." This looks structurally like economic redistribution. How does Paul's theology of the offering relate to Christian engagement with economic inequality? Where does stewardship end and social justice begin?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **The Incarnation as paradigm:** 2 Corinthians 8:9 establishes that the pattern of Christian giving is derived from the *morphology of the Incarnation*. To give is to become structurally similar to Christ in his self-emptying. Stewardship is therefore a form of spiritual formation.
- **Grace as both gift and gratitude:** The Greek word *charis* appears in both senses throughout 2 Corinthians 8–9, creating a theological loop: God's grace produces human gratitude, which is itself called *charis*, which flows to others as grace.
- **The OT theology of *corban*:** Israel's offering system was built on the premise of 1 Chronicles 29:14 — "Of your own have we given you." Paul's Gentile collection retrieves this logic: giving acknowledges prior receiving.
- **Stewardship and eschatology:** Revelation 13:8 locates Christian stewardship within an eternal frame. Giving participates in the pre-temporal purpose of God and anticipates the eschatological community where self-giving is the governing principle.
- **Unity as ecclesiology:** Paul's collection was not primarily humanitarian; it was *ecclesiological* — a visible demonstration that Jew and Gentile, slave and free are one body in Christ. The church that gives together *is* the message.
- **Freedom as constitutive of authentic worship:** The repeated emphasis on voluntariness (*haplotēs*, *hilaros*, *proaireō*, "not under compulsion") suggests that coerced giving — even if financially identical — is theologically defective. Freedom is a theological requirement rooted in the character of God.
