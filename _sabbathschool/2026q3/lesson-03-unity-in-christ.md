## 🔑 Central Theme

The problem of factionalism in Corinth was not primarily organizational — it was Christological. When a church organizes itself around human personalities ("I am of Paul," "I am of Apollos"), it displaces Christ from the center. Paul's appeal in 1 Corinthians 1–4 is not "can't we all just get along?" It is a theological argument: **the Cross makes cliques impossible, because the Cross is the great leveler of status, the destroyer of competitive pride, the source of a unity that no human leader can generate.**

The lesson moves from diagnosis (cliques as spiritual immaturity — *sarkinos*, fleshly, rather than *pneumatikos*, spiritual) to remedy (the mind of Christ, the servanthood of the Cross) to model (Paul's own suffering as the template for cruciform leadership). Unity in Christ is not achieved through management techniques or better communication — it is the fruit of a community genuinely centered on a crucified Lord.

The radical claim running through 1 Corinthians 1–4 is that the Cross itself produces unity by making boasting impossible. Where the Cross is genuinely received, competitive pride cannot survive. This is not merely ethical advice but a theological consequence: *because* Christ alone was crucified, *because* baptism is into His name alone, any community that grasps this cannot simultaneously organize around human status hierarchies. Faction is not primarily a relational failure — it is a theological one.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 Corinthians 1:10 — *Katartizō* (United/Restored)

- **Context:** This appeal comes immediately after the thanksgiving section (1:4–9), where Paul thanks God for the genuine spiritual gifts already present in Corinth. The gifts are real — but they have become weapons of status competition rather than instruments of edification.
- **"*Katartizō*" (be united):** Translated "be united" (ESV) or "be perfectly joined together" (NKJV). The word means to restore something to its proper condition — it is used for mending fishing nets in Matt. 4:21. Unity is not a new construction; it is the *restoration* of what the church was designed to be.
- **"Same mind (*nous*) and same judgment (*gnōmē*)":** Not intellectual uniformity — *nous* refers to the orientation of the will and understanding toward God; *gnōmē* is considered opinion or purpose. Paul is asking for alignment of fundamental commitments, not identical views on every matter.

### 1 Corinthians 1:12–13 — "Is Christ Divided?"

- The four parties: "I am of Paul," "I am of Apollos," "I am of Cephas," "I am of Christ." Even the Christ-party was problematic — claiming Christ as their exclusive property was its own form of factionalism.
- **"Is Christ divided? (*memeristai ho Christos*)":** The perfect tense is significant — Paul is asking whether Christ has been *permanently* split. The rhetorical answer is No, but the Corinthians were acting as if He had been.
- **"Was Paul crucified for you? Were you baptized in the name of Paul?":** The two defining events of Christian identity — crucifixion and baptism — are both anchored in Christ alone. To pledge allegiance to a human leader is to misunderstand what you were saved by and whom you were baptized into.

### 1 Corinthians 3:1–4 — *Sarkinos* vs. *Pneumatikos*

- **"Carnal (*sarkinos*) ... infants in Christ":** Paul uses two different words for "fleshly." *Sarkinos* (3:1) means made of flesh — the raw material, not yet transformed. *Sarkikos* (3:3) means dominated by fleshly impulses — the active choice to follow flesh rather than Spirit.
- **"Milk ... solid food":** The distinction is not about doctrinal complexity. Mature Christians eat solid food — they can handle the full weight of the Cross's implications, including its call to servanthood, suffering, and the death of self-promotion. The Corinthians' factionalism reveals they are still on milk.
- **"You are still fleshly" (*sarkikoi este*):** The present tense is an indictment — this is ongoing, not past. Their quarreling is current evidence of spiritual immaturity.

### Philippians 2:5–8 — The Mind of Christ as Unity's Foundation

- **"Have this mind (*touto phronēte*) among yourselves":** *Phronēte* is the verb from *phronēsis* — practical wisdom, orientation of mind toward an object. The "mind of Christ" is not a mystical experience but a practical orientation: others over self.
- **"He emptied himself (*eauton ekenōsen*)":** The *kenōsis* — Christ's voluntary relinquishment of divine prerogatives. This is the paradigm for leadership. Cruciform leaders don't accumulate status and privilege; they pour themselves out.
- **The descent is deliberate:** From equality with God → human form → servanthood → death → the particular death of the cross (the most shameful possible). The arc of Christ's trajectory is the opposite of every human social aspiration. This is the "mind" Christians are to have.

### 2 Corinthians 11:23–28 — Paul's Credentials for Ministry

- Paul's list of sufferings is not self-pity — it is a credential argument. In Corinth, credibility came from rhetorical power, status, and social connections. Paul's counterargument: authentic apostolic ministry looks like the Cross — suffering, weakness, dependence on God.
- **The ironic "fool's boast" (2 Cor. 11:21–12:13):** Paul reluctantly "boasts" of his weaknesses, because that is the only honest account of what Cross-shaped ministry produces. The Corinthians wanted power and glory; Paul gave them thorns in the flesh.

### Colossians 1:24 — Filling Up What Is Lacking

- **"I fill up in my flesh what is lacking in Christ's afflictions":** One of the most debated Pauline verses. Paul does not mean Christ's *atoning* work is incomplete — that is finished (Col. 1:20). He means that the *suffering mission* of Christ continues in His body, the church. The apostle's physical suffering extends Christ's presence into the world. This is not substitutionary but participatory — the body bears marks of its Head.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Nature of Factionalism

1. **Paul identifies four parties in Corinth — including one that says "I am of Christ," which sounds unimpeachable.** How can even a Christ-loyalty claim become a form of factionalism? What does it look like when people use explicit devotion to Jesus as a way of positioning themselves above other believers?

2. **Paul uses the word *schisma* (division) in 1 Cor. 1:10 — and places *eris* (strife) alongside murder and malice in Romans 1:29.** Does Adventist church culture take factionalism as seriously as Paul did? Where do we quietly tolerate division that Paul would have treated as a pastoral emergency?

3. **The Corinthian divisions formed around charismatic teachers — Paul, Apollos, Cephas.** Today the equivalents might be theologians, conference leaders, YouTube pastors, or popular authors. What are the specific Adventist versions of the "I am of Paul" problem? Where do you see this happening in your own circles?

4. **Paul says the root of division is pride (1 Cor. 4:6 — "puffed up for one against the other"), and pride is precisely what the Cross is designed to destroy.** Why does the gospel of a crucified Christ, if genuinely believed, make competitive religious pride impossible? Where do people manage to hold the Cross intellectually while maintaining pride practically?

5. **The Corinthians couldn't even celebrate the Lord's Supper together without faction (1 Cor. 11:17–22).** Paul says "I do not commend you" — strong words for the eucharist. Is there a contemporary equivalent — a moment of worship or community life that our divisions have poisoned — that we tend not to examine?

### On Unity and Uniformity

6. ***Katartizō* implies unity is the restoration of a proper condition, not the construction of a new one.** What does this imply about the church's original design? If unity is the restored state, what is the force that disrupts it — and what is the mechanism of restoration?

7. **"Be united in the same mind and the same judgment" (1 Cor. 1:10) cannot mean identical opinions — Paul himself disagreed sharply with Peter at Antioch (Gal. 2:11).** What is the difference between the "same mind" Paul calls for and intellectual conformity? Where is the line between healthy diversity and dangerous faction?

8. **Monday's lesson distinguishes cliques (dangerous) from small groups (potentially healthy).** What is the theological marker of the distinction? When does a community of mutual support become a self-reinforcing faction? What is the early warning sign?

### On Spiritual Maturity and Leadership

9. **Paul describes the Corinthians as "infants in Christ" still on milk — yet they had been Christians for years and had remarkable spiritual gifts (1 Cor. 1:5–7).** How is it possible to have abundant spiritual gifts and still be spiritually immature? What does that imply about the relationship between gifts and character?

10. **"Wisdom and maturity" in this lesson are defined Christologically — mature believers embrace the wisdom of the Cross, including its call to suffering and self-denial.** Is there a form of Adventist theological sophistication that is actually a refined version of immaturity — knowing doctrine deeply while remaining resistant to Cross-shaped living?

11. **Paul defines proper leadership as stewardship (*oikonomos* — household manager of someone else's property).** Leaders do not own the church; they manage it for its true Owner. How does this reshape how church leaders should think about their authority, and how members should think about their expectations of leaders?

### On Christlike Servanthood and Suffering

12. **The "mind of Christ" (Phil. 2:5) is operationally defined as self-emptying — not mystical attunement but practical downward mobility.** In your local church context, what would it look like for a board of elders, a church school committee, or a worship team to have "the mind of Christ" in their decision-making?

13. **Paul's credential for apostolic authenticity is his suffering (2 Cor. 11:23–28). Contemporary Christian ministry often uses opposite credentials — education, attendance growth, social media following.** How do we evaluate this tension? Is suffering still a marker of authentic ministry, or has the context changed enough that different credentials are now valid?

14. **"Imitate me" (1 Cor. 4:16) — Paul tells the Corinthians to follow his example, yet this is the same letter that condemns personality cults.** How can a leader legitimately say "follow my example" without becoming the thing he is warning against? What is the difference between modeling and idolizing?

### On Unity's Witness

15. **EGW writes that unity among God's people "carries powerful conviction to the world that they have the truth." John 17:21 says the same — unity is the world's evidence that the Father sent the Son.** If that is true, what does Adventist disunity communicate to the watching world? How seriously should we take denominational divisions and congregational conflict as a missional problem — not just a pastoral one?

16. **EGW also says the enemy is "determined that unity shall not exist."** How do you recognize the specific tactics of disunity in a local congregation? Are they primarily theological, relational, or structural — and does that distinction matter for how you respond?

17. **Thursday's lesson says leaders who "display a lifestyle that reflects the submission represented by the Cross are worthy of being heard and followed."** By that standard, how should we evaluate leaders — Adventist or otherwise — who hold correct theology but exercise power in self-serving ways? Is cruciform character a sufficient check on doctrinal orthodoxy, or can it be performed?

### Harder Questions

18. **Colossians 1:24 says Paul "fills up in his flesh what is lacking in Christ's afflictions."** This is one of the most contested Pauline verses. What does he mean? Is Christ's atoning work incomplete? How does this verse relate to the theology of the church as Christ's body continuing His mission — and what does that mean for the suffering of ordinary believers today?

19. **Paul refers to himself and Apollos as "men condemned to death" (1 Cor. 4:9) — using the imagery of prisoners paraded before execution.** Is there a form of Christian ministry today where the minister genuinely occupies this position of public shame? Where do Adventist ministers or laypeople face this kind of cruciform public exposure?

20. **The Corinthians were impressive by almost every measure — gifted, knowledgeable, enthusiastic. Paul calls them immature.** What is the relationship between spiritual vitality (as the Corinthians experienced it) and the kind of deep-rootedness that produces unity? Can a church be spiritually exciting and spiritually shallow at the same time?

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. Unity as Missional Evidence

> *"The oneness and unity of God's truth-believing remnant people carries powerful conviction to the world that they have the truth, and are the peculiar, chosen people of God. This oneness and unity disconcerts the enemy, and he is determined that it shall not exist. The present truth, believed in the heart and exemplified in the life, makes God's people one, and gives them a powerful influence."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 1, p. 327

**Context:** Written in the early years of the Adventist movement when organizational fractures were threatening the fledgling church. EGW frames unity not as a social nicety but as the church's most powerful apologetic to a skeptical world.

**Discussion prompt:** If the watching world evaluates the truth of our message partly by the quality of our unity, what are they currently concluding? What would it take for your local congregation to be genuinely "disconcerting" to the enemy this week?

---

### 2. The Scope of Christ's Prayer for Unity

> *"God designs that His people should all come into the unity of the faith. The prayer of Christ just prior to His crucifixion was that His disciples might be one, even as He was one with the Father, that the world might believe that the Father had sent Him. This most touching and wonderful prayer reaches down the ages, even to our day; for His words were: 'Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on Me through their word.'"*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 4, p. 17

**Context:** EGW is responding to sectarian tendencies within Adventism that were producing camps and factions, and pointing to Jesus' John 17 prayer as the standard — not comfortable agreement but the unity of the Father and Son themselves.

**Discussion prompt:** The standard Jesus prays for is not polite disagreement managed well — it is the ontological bond between the Father and Son. Does that standard convict or inspire you? What would it require of your relationships within your church community to move toward that standard, even partially?

---

### 3. The Refining Process and the Platform of Truth

> *"God is leading out a people to stand in perfect unity upon the platform of eternal truth. Christ gave Himself to the world that He might 'purify unto Himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works.' This refining process is designed to purge the church from all unrighteousness and the spirit of discord and contention, that they may build up instead of tear down, and concentrate their energies on the great work before them."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 4, p. 17

**Context:** EGW uses the language of refinement — the church's trials and tensions are not failures but the purifying process by which God produces a people genuinely capable of unity. Division is not the church's final state; it is what is being refined away.

**Discussion prompt:** Where is God currently doing "refining work" in your congregation — surfacing tensions that need to be resolved rather than suppressed? Is there a conflict in your church that, if handled in the Spirit, could produce deeper unity rather than further division?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

### Individual

- **The *katartizō* inventory:** Identify one relationship in your church community that has fractured around a personality conflict, doctrinal disagreement, or leadership dispute. Write out specifically what "restoring it to proper condition" would require of *you* — not the other party. What is the first concrete step?
- **A week of Philippians 2:** Read Philippians 2:1–11 each morning for seven days, slowly. Each day, identify one decision you will face that day and ask: what does "the mind of Christ" — kenotic, other-preferring, non-status-seeking — look like in that specific situation? Log what you notice.
- **Examine your loyalties:** Make a list of the theological voices, pastors, authors, or movements you follow most consistently. For each, ask honestly: am I following them because they sharpen my encounter with Christ, or because they confirm what I already believe and make me feel part of a tribe?

### Sabbath School

- **"I am of ___" exercise:** Begin class by asking each person to silently complete the sentence "In terms of my theological identity, I am most aligned with ___." Don't share the answers — simply sit with the awareness. Then ask: Is that a *katartizō* alignment (toward Christ) or a *schisma* alignment (toward a human camp)?
- **Corinthian roleplay:** Assign different class members the four parties (Paul, Apollos, Cephas, Christ). Have each articulate what their "camp" values most. Then read 1 Corinthians 1:13 together and debrief: what does each camp look like from the other side? What does it look like to Christ?
- **Pray for a name:** Before class, briefly name someone in the broader church — a conference leader, a theological opponent, a congregation your church has friction with — and pray for them by name. Discuss afterward: what did it feel like? What made it hard? This is a small act of *katartizō*.

### Intellectual

- **The epistemology of unity:** Paul grounds unity in a theological claim — Christ cannot be divided. He does not appeal to relational skills or cultural competence. Is theological grounding sufficient to produce actual behavioral unity, or does doctrine need to be accompanied by something else (community practices, sacraments, accountability structures)? What does the Corinthian situation suggest about the relationship between correct doctrine and lived unity?
- **Kenosis and power:** Philippians 2:5–8 presents *kenōsis* — self-emptying — as the defining movement of Christian leadership. But every institution (including the church) requires authority, decision-making, and sometimes coercion for its survival. Is kenotic leadership a practical ecclesiology, or an eschatological ideal that must be approximated? What would a genuinely kenotic leadership structure look like in a local Adventist congregation?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **Unity as Christological:** The church's unity is grounded not in agreement or affinity but in the singular Lordship of Christ — one baptism, one crucified Savior. This makes human faction-formation a theological category error, not merely a relational failure.
- **Kenosis as leadership model:** Philippians 2:5–8 provides the template for all Christian leadership — downward mobility, self-emptying, suffering as credential. The cross-shaped leader accumulates weakness, not status. This inverts every ambient cultural marker of leadership success.
- **Gifts vs. character:** Corinth demonstrates that spiritual gifts and spiritual maturity are not the same thing. Gifts are distributed sovereignly by the Spirit; character is formed through sustained cruciform living. A church can be charismatically vibrant and morally shallow simultaneously.
- **Suffering as apostolic identity:** 2 Corinthians 11 and Colossians 1:24 frame Paul's suffering as participation in Christ's afflictions — not punishment but identification. The apostle's body bears the marks of Jesus. Cruciform leadership cannot be separated from actual cost.
- **Unity's eschatological dimension:** EGW frames unity as part of the "platform of eternal truth" on which the remnant stands. Division is not just a pastoral problem — it has end-time dimensions. The purifying process toward unity is part of God's preparation of a people for the final crisis.
- **The Cross as unity's source:** The deepest thread in 1 Corinthians 1–4 is that the Cross itself — not better communication or conflict resolution skills — is the engine of Christian unity. Where the Cross is genuinely received, competitive boasting collapses. This is not sentiment but Pauline theology: the instrument of shame becomes the only possible basis of a community that includes Jew and Greek, slave and free, Paul's people and Apollos's people.
