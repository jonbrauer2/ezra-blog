## 🔑 Central Theme

The Emmaus road is one of the most pedagogically rich passages in the New Testament. Two disciples walk home heartbroken, and Jesus joins them — without revealing His identity — and *teaches them how to read*. "Beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them the things written about Himself in all the Scriptures" ([Luke 24:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke24.27)).

The disciples' problem was not lack of biblical knowledge. They knew the texts. Their problem was *false expectation* — a one-sided messianism that filtered out everything in the Hebrew Bible about a suffering Servant. They had read the prophets and missed the cross. Jesus walks them through the same texts they had always read, and the texts come alive.

This week the lesson is hermeneutics — the principles by which we read prophetic writing rightly. The stakes are pastoral: "A false understanding of prophecy can lead to spiritual disaster."

Five hermeneutical principles structure the week:

- **Scripture interprets Scripture.** The Bible's underlying unity allows it to function as its own interpreter. [Daniel 2](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan2) interprets itself within the chapter. [Matthew 13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt13) explains its own parables. The New Testament interprets the Old. Difficult texts are read in light of clear ones, not the reverse.

- **Historical-cultural context matters.** [Isaiah 65:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa65.20)'s "child shall die a hundred years old" is poetic, not literal — read within Isaiah's symbolism, not flattened against Revelation's eschatology. "Greet one another with a holy kiss" ([1 Cor. 16:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor16.20)) is *principle* (warm welcome) embodied in *practice* (first-century kiss) — applied today as handshake, hug, or bow.

- **Literary context guards against distortion.** Satan's two great deceptions — Eve in the garden and Christ in the wilderness — both involved *misquoting Scripture out of context*. Genuine truth never needs to be ripped from its setting to make its point.

- **Words have meanings; meanings have contexts.** [Luke 10:26](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke10.26) — Jesus to the lawyer: "What is written in the law? How do you read it?" *Interpretation is unavoidable*. Even the simplest reading is already a reading. The question is whether one's hermeneutic is conscious and disciplined or unconscious and ad hoc.

- **Christ is the interpretive center.** The Emmaus pattern is normative: Christ is the meaning of "Moses and all the Prophets." Any reading that does not arrive at Him has missed the point of the texts.

Friday's pivot to Ellen White is methodologically crucial. The same principles that apply to reading Scripture apply to reading her: open and prayerful posture, context, principle over particular, comprehensive study of her treatment of a topic, and verification that she actually said it. The principles protect against both *under-reading* (dismissing her) and *over-reading* (weaponizing fragments). This is mature Adventist hermeneutics.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### [Luke 24:27](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke24.27) (Memory Text — The Emmaus Hermeneutic)

- **"Beginning with Moses and with all the Prophets":** The two-fold canon division of the OT (Torah, Prophets) — sometimes called by Jews "the Law and the Prophets" or "Moses and the Prophets." Jesus uses the whole OT as His commentary.
- **"He explained (*diērmēneusen*)":** The same root as *hermeneutics*. Christ *interprets* the texts for them. He is the interpretive key and the interpretive method at once.
- **"The things concerning Himself":** Not just *some* texts. The pattern is comprehensive — all the Prophets are read Christologically. The OT is, in Luke's narration, *about* Christ.
- **The pedagogical genius:** Jesus does not reveal His identity first and then teach. He teaches first; the recognition comes at the breaking of bread (v. 35). The reading rightly *prepares* for the encounter.

### [Luke 24:21, 25, 32](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke24.21) (Sabbath Intro — Heart-Posture)

- **"We were hoping" (*ēlpizomen*):** Past tense, defeated tone. Their hope has been buried with Jesus. The right teaching cannot occur until the wrong hope is acknowledged.
- **"Foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe":** Jesus' rebuke is over their *slow-heartedness*, not their ignorance. Reading the Bible rightly is a *heart* problem as much as a *mind* problem.
- **"Did not our heart burn within us":** The same heart that was slow becomes burning. The Spirit's confirmation comes through the rightly-interpreted Word.

### [Matthew 5:17–48](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt5.17) (Sunday — Jesus and the Law)

- **"Do not think that I came to destroy... but to fulfill":** The verb *plērōsai* means *to fill up* — to bring to fullness. Jesus is not setting aside the OT but *exposing its full depth*.
- **The six "you have heard... but I say" antitheses:** Each begins with a popular reading and moves to the deeper intent. "You shall not murder" → "do not be angry without cause." "You shall not commit adultery" → "do not look with lust." The pattern is *internalization* — moving from outward act to inward disposition.
- **Connection to [Jer. 31:33](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jer31.33):** The new covenant promise is the law *written on the heart*. Jesus' Sermon on the Mount is the *enacted* form of that promise — the law being taught at its heart-level.

### [Isaiah 65:17–20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa65.17) (Monday — Historical-Cultural Context)

- **"A child shall die a hundred years old":** Cannot be flat-eschatological — the rest of Isaiah says death itself will be abolished (25:8). The passage is *prophetic-poetic* in a near-fulfilment register, looking at restored Israel as a *foretaste* of the new creation.
- **The principle:** Apocalyptic and prophetic texts often work on multiple horizons — near fulfilment, far fulfilment, ultimate fulfilment — sometimes layered. Reading the near horizon as the ultimate one creates contradictions that disappear when the layering is recognized.

### [Matthew 4:1–11](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt4.1) (Tuesday — Satan's Hermeneutic)

- **"It is written" — three times:** Jesus quotes [Deut. 8:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut8.3); [6:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut6.16); [6:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut6.13). Satan quotes [Ps. 91:11, 12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps91.11) — but *out of context*.
- **The misquotation analysis:** [Ps. 91:11, 12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps91.11) promises protection in the way of obedience. Satan removes the obedience condition and reapplies the promise to presumption. The verses are real; the application is the lie.
- **Jesus' counter-move:** "It is written *again*" — Scripture must interpret Scripture. One text cannot be used to break another. The whole counsel must shape the reading of each part.

### [Luke 10:25–28](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke10.25) (Wednesday — The Lawyer's Interpretation)

- **"What is written in the law? What is your reading (*pōs anaginōskeis*)?":** Two questions, not one. Jesus distinguishes the *text* from the *reading*. Both matter. The lawyer's reading happens to be correct (love God and neighbor), but Jesus' next question — "Who is my neighbor?" — exposes that the right *summary* can coexist with the wrong *application*.

### [Acts 8:30–35](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts8.30) (Thursday — Philip Explains)

- **"Do you understand what you are reading?":** Philip's first question. The Ethiopian's answer ("How can I, unless someone guides me?") is the honest acknowledgment that the text needs an interpreter.
- **"Beginning at this Scripture":** Philip uses the [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) passage as a *launching point* — opening the Christological meaning of the Servant Song.
- **Result:** Baptism. Rightly-explained Scripture leads to obedient response. The hermeneutical loop closes in life.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On Hermeneutical Self-Awareness

1. **[Luke 24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke24) shows that the disciples *knew* the Hebrew Bible but read it through expectations that filtered out the suffering Servant. What expectations are *you* currently bringing to Scripture that might be filtering out something Jesus would have you see? Be honest — every reader brings expectations.**

2. **Some people claim "the Bible doesn't need interpretation; just read it." Yet Jesus Himself, on the Emmaus road, did *interpretive* work — *diērmēneusen*, the root of "hermeneutics." Why is the "no-interpretation" claim itself an *interpretive* claim? Have you noticed where it is invoked in your circles?**

3. **The lawyer in [Luke 10:26](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke10.26) gives the right *summary* of the Law (love God, love neighbor) but his next question ("Who is my neighbor?") reveals he wants to limit the application. Where in your own reading do you correctly summarize Scripture but quietly limit its claims on you?**

### On the Bible as Its Own Interpreter

4. **The lesson notes that "the New Testament is the inspired interpreter of the Old Testament." How does this principle shape how you read difficult OT passages (the Conquest, dietary laws, capital sanctions)? What examples have you found where the NT *re-frames* an OT difficulty in helpful ways?**

5. **Jesus' Sermon on the Mount intensifies the law (you shall not murder → don't be angry; no adultery → no lust). Is this *new* teaching, or is it the *original* meaning of the OT made explicit? What does this suggest about whether the OT and NT are continuous or discontinuous?**

6. **[Daniel 2](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan2) interprets itself within the chapter. [Matt. 13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt13) explains its parables. Where in your study have you been stuck on a "difficult" passage that turned out to have its *answer* nearby in the text? What does this teach about the patience required for good reading?**

### On Historical-Cultural Context

7. **"Greet one another with a holy kiss" ([1 Cor. 16:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Cor16.20)). The principle (warm welcome) is universal; the practice (kiss) is cultural. How do you discern the difference between *principle* and *practice* in Scripture? Where have you seen the lines confused?**

8. **[Isaiah 65:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa65.20) — a child dying at 100 — is poetic restoration language, not literal eschatology. The lesson notes that flattening prophetic poetry into prose creates contradictions. Where in current Adventist hermeneutics have you noticed flattening — perhaps reading apocalyptic symbolism as detailed historical prediction?**

9. **Some Christians claim to read "every text the same way." But the Bible is composed of multiple genres requiring different reading conventions. What conventions does *legal* text require that *poetic* text does not? What does *apocalyptic* require that *epistolary* does not? Have you developed the literary range to handle each well?**

### On Literary Context

10. **Satan in the wilderness quoted [Psalm 91](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps91) correctly — and used it to invite suicide. Truth ripped from context becomes a lie. Where in current religious teaching have you seen verses used in ways that, on examination, ignore their literary context? What gave the deception its power?**

11. **"Truth does not need deception to be revealed." If you have to take a verse out of context to make your point, the point may not be the text's. Is there a position you currently hold that you defend with isolated verses? What happens when you read those verses in their full chapter?**

12. **Satan's hermeneutic in [Gen. 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gen3) was to first *generalize* God's word ("did God say...?") and then *misapply* it. What contemporary forms of generalization-then-misapplication do you notice in religious discourse? In yourself?**

### On Christ as the Interpretive Center

13. **The Emmaus pattern: Jesus reads "Moses and all the Prophets" Christologically. Any reading that arrives somewhere other than Christ has missed the point. How does this shape your reading of OT narratives (David and Goliath, Daniel in the lions' den, Esther) — as moral examples, as historical events, as Christological types, or some combination?**

14. **[Acts 8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts8) — the Ethiopian eunuch needed Philip to explain. The *simple reading* did not produce the *deeper understanding*. How does this challenge the assumption that personal Bible study alone is sufficient? What is the proper role of teachers, commentaries, and community in your reading?**

15. **Jesus quoted Scripture in His preaching ([Isa. 61](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa61) in [Luke 4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke4); [Deut. 8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Deut8) in [Matt. 4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt4)). His teaching was "thoroughly biblical." What does it mean for our own teaching, witness, and conversation to be biblical *in this way* — not just decorated with verses, but structurally anchored in the texts?**

### On Reading Ellen White Rightly

16. **EGW herself wrote: "If you search the Scriptures to vindicate your own opinions, you will never reach the truth." The same principle applies to reading her. Where in Adventist practice have her writings been mined for *ammunition* rather than read for *formation*?**

17. **"Time and place must be considered." EGW's counsel was sometimes context-specific (the 1894 letter on bicycles is the lesson's example). What does it look like to apply her general principles without absolutizing her specific applications? Where have you seen this distinction collapsed?**

18. **The lesson's final principle: "Make sure Ellen G. White said it." There are many fabrications attributed to her. What disciplines do you use (or should use) to verify EGW quotations before citing them? How does this care reflect respect for her actual voice?**

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. On the OT-NT relationship

> *"The Old Testament is the gospel in figures and symbols. The New Testament is the substance. One is as essential as the other."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Selected Messages*, book 2, p. 104

**Context:** EGW affirms a typological reading of the OT — the New is the substance the Old foreshadowed.

**Discussion prompt:** This is the same hermeneutic Christ employed on the Emmaus road. How does it differ from supersessionism (the church *replaces* Israel) on one side and dispensationalism (sharp discontinuity between Israel and church) on the other? What does this typological reading preserve that both alternatives risk losing?

### 2. On searching the Scriptures with humility

> *"If you search the Scriptures to vindicate your own opinions, you will never reach the truth. Search in order to learn what the Lord says."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Christ's Object Lessons*, p. 112

**Context:** EGW on the posture required for fruitful Bible study.

**Discussion prompt:** The two postures — *vindicating opinion* vs. *learning what the Lord says* — are subtly different in motivation but radically different in outcome. Which posture has been dominant in your recent Bible study? What would shifting to the other look like for the next 30 days?

### 3. On time and place

> *"Time and place must be considered."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 57

**Context:** EGW's own counsel on reading her counsels.

**Discussion prompt:** "Time and place must be considered" is hermeneutical maturity in five words. It is also the principle most often ignored when EGW is cited polemically. What does mature reading require that polemical reading skips? How would Adventist discourse change if "time and place" were taken seriously in every quotation?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

**Individual:**

1. **The Emmaus walk-through.** Take one OT passage you have read many times. Imagine Jesus walking with you, asking, "What does this passage say about Me?" Don't force a Christological reading — listen for it. What surfaces?

2. **The context check.** Pick one verse you have heard frequently quoted in your circles (e.g., [Jer. 29:11](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jer29.11), [Phil. 4:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Phil4.13), [Matt. 18:20](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt18.20)). Read the verse in its full chapter. Does the popular use match the chapter's intent, or has the verse been pulled in a direction the chapter does not authorize?

3. **The EGW verification exercise.** Take one EGW quote you have heard cited but never traced. Find the original source. Read the surrounding three pages. Note whether the popular use matches her own context, time, and intent.

**In Sabbath School:**

1. **The [Luke 24](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke24) dramatization.** Have one member read the Emmaus narrative aloud while the class follows. Pause at v. 27 ("beginning with Moses and all the Prophets"). Discuss: if Jesus walked into our class today and asked, "What do you think 'Moses and all the Prophets' say about Me?" — what would we answer? What might we be missing that He would want us to see?

2. **The hermeneutics-by-distortion exercise.** Take a familiar verse (e.g., [John 14:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn14.6) — "I am the way, the truth, and the life"). Pass out the verse alone vs. the verse with full chapter context. Discuss: how does the context change what the verse can be used for?

3. **The EGW reading guidelines workshop.** Read together the five guidelines on this week's Friday page. Pick an EGW quote that has caused controversy in your community. Apply each guideline. What changes about how the quote should be read or applied?

**Intellectually:**

1. **The Sermon on the Mount and Torah study.** Read [Matt. 5:17–48](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt5.17) alongside the OT texts Jesus cites ([Exod. 20:13, 14](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex20.13); [Lev. 19:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Lev19.18)). What is Jesus' relationship to Torah — abolition, addition, intensification, internalization, or something else? How does this affect your reading of OT law as a Christian?

2. **The typology study.** Read [Heb. 9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Heb9) and 10 together. Note how the writer reads OT temple, priesthood, and sacrifice as types of Christ. What is the discipline of typological reading — what does it require, and what does it forbid?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **Christ is the hermeneutical key.** The Emmaus road is the foundational text. Jesus reads "Moses and all the Prophets" as *about Himself*. The Christocentric reading is not an apologetic device imposed on the OT; it is the OT's own intention as Christ interpreted it.

- **The new covenant fulfils, not replaces, the law.** [Jeremiah 31:31–33](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jer31.31) promised the law written on hearts. Jesus' Sermon on the Mount is the enacted form of that promise. The new covenant intensifies the old, internalizes it, and centers it in Christ — without nullifying it.

- **Context as hermeneutical first principle.** Every misreading the lesson surveys (Eve in Eden, Christ in the wilderness, the bicycle-buying interpretation of EGW) is fundamentally a *context* failure. Reading the text in its setting is the first discipline. Without it, even orthodox words can be made to teach heresy.

- **Sound doctrine is corporate as well as personal.** The Ethiopian's question — "How can I unless someone guides me?" — names the necessity of community in Bible reading. Private interpretation untethered from teaching community has historically produced both spiritual revival and spiritual disaster. The community is itself a hermeneutical instrument.

- **EGW's writings are read by the same hermeneutic.** The five principles for reading her (prayerful posture, context, principle over particular, comprehensive study, verification) mirror the principles for reading Scripture. This is intentional. The "lesser light" honors the "greater light" by submitting to the same reading discipline.
