## 🔑 Central Theme

Last week's lesson laid the foundation: God speaks because He is a seeking God. This week the lesson narrows from the *fact* of revelation to the *means* — the human beings God calls to bear His word. And the lesson is unsentimental about what that calling costs. Prophets are "ignored, ridiculed, beaten, or jailed. Others were stoned or hunted like wild animals. Several were martyred."

The memory text is [Isaiah 6:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6.8) — "Here am I! Send me." It is one of the most-quoted verses in Adventist missions and one of the most misread. The line lands as eager volunteerism, but the chapter that frames it tells a different story: Isaiah saw the holiness of God, fell undone, and was *purged* by a coal from the altar before he could speak. The volunteering is the *result* of being unmade and remade. Prophets are not recruited; they are remade.

The lesson surveys five exemplars and ends with a sixth — Ellen White's first vision, given to a 17-year-old in the wreckage of the Great Disappointment. Each call narrative shows a different pattern, but every one bears a common signature: the call meets the person where they are, exposes who they are, and sends them with a word that is not their own.

Four threads run through the week:

- **The call exposes before it equips.** Abraham is asked to leave country and family before he is shown where to go. Elijah confronts a nation that has forgotten Yahweh's name. Isaiah is undone by holiness before he is commissioned. Daniel is exiled into a pagan court before he speaks to its kings. John lives in the wilderness eating locusts before announcing the Messiah. The pattern is unmistakable — calling involves stripping.
- **The message is rarely welcome.** Prophets are not popular. The lesson notes pointedly that "the wickedness of John's listeners was unmasked in a manner that caused Satan to quake." Faithful prophetic speech does not soothe its hearers; it diagnoses them.
- **The cross is the center of every prophetic word.** Whether the prophet is Abraham foreshadowing substitution on Moriah, Isaiah painting the Suffering Servant, or John pointing to "the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world" — every authentic prophet, in their own idiom, declares Christ crucified.
- **Faithfulness is more important than visibility.** John dies in prison without seeing the kingdom he announced. Isaiah, by tradition, dies sawn in two. Daniel never returns to Jerusalem. The vindication of the prophet's work belongs to God, not to the prophet's own seeing.

The aim of this lesson is to honor the cost of the prophetic vocation, ground its content in Christ, and quietly raise the question: if God is still calling — what would honest response look like?

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### [Isaiah 6:1–8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6.1) (Memory Text — the call narrative)

- **Context:** "In the year that King Uzziah died" (v. 1). A long, prosperous reign has ended; the political center has lost its anchor. Isaiah sees the throne that was empty in Jerusalem occupied by the LORD in heaven.
- **"High and lifted up":** The same phrase reappears in [Isa. 52:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa52.13) about the Suffering Servant. Isaiah's vision of God enthroned in chapter 6 will, by chapter 53, be inverted into a vision of God *lifted up on a cross*.
- **"Woe is me! For I am undone":** The verb is *nidmêṯî*, often translated "I am ruined / cut off." Encounter with holiness does not produce religious satisfaction; it produces *undoing*.
- **"A live coal... He touched my mouth":** The purgation is fire — costly, painful, specific. The same lips that will speak God's word must first be burned clean.
- **"Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?":** The plural "Us" — likely the divine council in heavenly assembly — issues an open question. Notice that the *commission* is offered before the *content* is given. Isaiah volunteers before he knows the message (vv. 9–13 reveal it is *hardening* preaching to a people who will not turn).

### [Genesis 15:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gen15.6); [Galatians 3:6–9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gal3.6) (Sunday — Abraham's Defining Verse)

- **"He believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness":** The first verse in Scripture where the technical language of *imputed righteousness* appears. Abraham is not declared righteous because of his obedience; he is declared righteous because of his trust.
- **Paul's reading in [Gal. 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gal3):** Paul argues that this verse — written 430 years before Sinai — establishes that justification has *always* been by faith. The Mosaic law did not change the salvation principle; it served the principle until Christ came.
- **"In you all the nations shall be blessed":** Abraham is not called *for himself*. His calling is missional from the start. The covenant is centripetal (drawing in) and centrifugal (sending out) at the same time.

### [1 Kings 18:19–39](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Kgs18.19) (Monday — Mount Carmel)

- **"How long will you falter between two opinions?":** The verb (*pōseaḥ*) means to *limp* — moving forward unevenly on two legs. Elijah names the syncretism of Israel as a kind of spiritual limping. They have not rejected Yahweh outright; they have made Him one option among others.
- **The water-soaked sacrifice:** Elijah triples the difficulty by drenching the wood. The drought has lasted three years; water is precious; the lavish use of it is an act of faith and a public commentary on Baal's powerlessness (Baal was the storm-god responsible for rain).
- **"The LORD, He is God! The LORD, He is God!":** The cry is *yhwh hû' hāʾelōhîm* — a sentence-form confession that becomes the climax of Elijah's name (Eli-jah = "Yahweh is my God"). The prophet's name has been the message all along.

### [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) (Tuesday — The Suffering Servant)

- **Written ca. 700 BC, ~700 years before Christ:** This is one of the most extended substitutionary atonement texts in the Old Testament. Every phrase has a New Testament echo.
- **"He was wounded for our transgressions" (v. 5):** The Hebrew preposition *min* with the noun *pešaʿ* can mean either "from" or "because of." Either way, the cause of the wounding is external — the Servant is hurt for sins not his own.
- **"The chastisement of our peace was upon Him":** The Hebrew word *šālôm* (peace, wholeness) is the *result* of the chastisement, not its absence. The peace believers enjoy is purchased at the cost of the Servant's pain.
- **"The LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all" (v. 6):** The verb *pāgaʿ* in this construction means to make to meet, to cause to fall upon. The full weight of human sin *falls upon* the Servant by divine appointment.
- **"He shall see His seed... He shall be satisfied" (v. 11):** After substitutionary death comes resurrection vindication. The chapter does not end in tragedy; it ends with the Servant satisfied with what He has accomplished.

### [Daniel 2:46–48](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan2.46) (Wednesday — Witness in a Pagan Court)

- **Nebuchadnezzar falls down to worship Daniel:** Daniel does not correct the king's posture in the moment (compare Paul and Barnabas in [Acts 14](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts14), who tore their clothes). The Aramaic text is careful, however: Nebuchadnezzar's confession that follows ("Truly your God is the God of gods") directs the worship to its proper object.
- **"God of gods... Lord of kings... Revealer of secrets":** Three titles that exactly match the three challenges of the chapter — pagan deities, royal authority, mystery. Each is answered in Daniel's God.
- **Daniel's elevation:** He is made "ruler over the whole province of Babylon" — but the narrative makes the *witness* the point of the elevation, not the elevation itself. Faithfulness brings him to the place where his witness can be heard.

### [Daniel 2:44](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan2.44) (Wednesday — The Stone Cut Without Hands)

- **"A stone was cut out without hands":** The contrast with the metal statue (handcrafted, golden-headed, idolatrous) is sharp. God's kingdom enters history *uncrafted by humans*, dismantling the imperial sequence by its own initiative.
- **"It shall stand forever":** The same phrase used of God's throne in [Ps. 45:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps45.6). Daniel sees the eschaton: not human kingdoms purified, but a kingdom that supplants and outlasts them.

### [Mark 1:2–5](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mark1.2); [Isaiah 40:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa40.3) (Thursday — The Wilderness Voice)

- **The composite quotation:** Mark stitches [Malachi 3:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Mal3.1) and [Isaiah 40:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa40.3) together. Both texts were originally about preparation for Yahweh's coming; Mark applies them to preparation for Jesus' coming — an implicit, breathtaking Christology in the first sentences of the gospel.
- **"A voice of one crying in the wilderness":** The wilderness is where Israel began as a nation; the wilderness is where John reconvenes them for renewal. The geography is theological.
- **"Prepare the way of the LORD":** John's task is *clearing*, not constructing. Repentance is not the building of righteousness — it is the removal of obstacles to grace.

### [Matthew 11:1–6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt11.1) (Thursday — John in Prison)

- **"Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?":** John, in prison, has begun to doubt. The strongest prophet since Moses entertains uncertainty about the one he baptized. Faith and doubt are not opposite postures; they often live in the same prophet.
- **Jesus' answer:** Not "Yes, of course." Instead, Jesus lists works that match [Isaiah 35](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa35) and 61 — the messianic signs of the coming age. He invites John to see the *evidence* of the kingdom, not just hear an assertion.
- **"Blessed is he who is not offended because of Me":** A pastoral coda for John, and for every disciple who finds the Messiah's path slower, smaller, and more painful than expected.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Cost of Being Called

1. **The lesson catalogues the suffering of prophets — ignored, ridiculed, beaten, jailed, stoned, hunted, martyred. We tend to romanticize prophetic ministry as visionary and prestigious. What changes when you take the suffering seriously? Would you still ask for the gift if you understood its cost?**

2. **[Isaiah 6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6) shows that the call begins with *undoing* — "Woe is me, for I am undone." Why does encounter with holiness produce ruin before commission? Have you ever experienced a moment of being *unmade* before God? What did the remaking look like?**

3. **God's question — "Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?" — is offered to the whole heavenly council. Isaiah volunteers before he knows the message. What does it mean to say "Send me" before you know what He will send you *to*?**

### On the Five Prophets

4. **Abraham was a "flawed man" — he lied about Sarah, took Hagar's son into his own hands, doubted the promise. Yet he is the father of the faithful. What does it mean that the prophetic life can include flaws without forfeiting faithfulness? Where does the line fall between *flawed* and *faithless*?**

5. **Elijah's confrontation on Carmel asks, "How long will you falter between two opinions?" The Hebrew word for "falter" means *to limp*. What forms of spiritual limping do you recognize in your own life — places where you have not chosen against God, but have refused to fully choose for Him?**

6. **[Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) was written ~700 years before Christ and describes the cross with astonishing specificity. The lesson asks: what should this teach us about the prophetic gift? Beyond predictive accuracy, what does [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) teach us about the *character* of God who would reveal His own suffering to a prophet centuries in advance?**

7. **Daniel served pagan kings faithfully without compromising his witness. He prayed three times daily in a kingdom where it was illegal. He refused royal food without making a scene. How do you read Daniel's example for a believer in a workplace, school, or community where Christian conviction is increasingly costly? What does faithful Daniel-style witness look like for you?**

8. **John the Baptist sat in prison and *doubted* the very Messiah he had baptized. How do you reconcile John's wilderness boldness ("Repent! The kingdom is at hand!") with his prison-cell question ("Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?")? Is doubt the opposite of prophetic faith, or part of it?**

### On the Common Pattern

9. **Every prophet's calling exposes them before it equips them. Abraham must leave; Elijah must hide; Isaiah is unmade; Daniel is exiled; John lives in the wilderness. What does this pattern suggest about how God prepares the messengers He sends? Has there been a "stripping" in your own life that, looking back, was preparation rather than punishment?**

10. **The lesson says the message of every prophet — however different in form — centers on Christ. Abraham foreshadows substitution at Moriah; Isaiah paints the Suffering Servant; John points to the Lamb. If Christ is the content of all true prophecy, what is the test of any modern voice claiming to speak for God?**

11. **Prophetic faithfulness is often invisible in its own lifetime. Isaiah did not see Jerusalem repent. John did not see the kingdom come. Daniel never returned to his homeland. How do you stay faithful to a calling whose fruit you may not see? What sustains you when the work feels unverified?**

### On the Modern Prophetic Voice

12. **The lesson notes that "today in a generation flooded with violence, greed, immorality, exploitation... the prophetic voice is often rejected or simply ignored." What kinds of prophetic voices does your context most need to hear? Are they being raised — and if so, how are they being received? If not, why not?**

13. **Ellen White's first vision came not as a triumphant declaration but as a *gentle encouragement* to 17-year-old Ellen and a community devastated by the Great Disappointment. What does it say about prophetic ministry that one of the most consequential modern manifestations of the gift began with comforting the disappointed rather than denouncing the unfaithful?**

14. **"A revival of true godliness among us is the greatest and most urgent of all our needs." Ellen White's words from *Selected Messages* are 150 years old. If you imagine the church she described and the church you sit in now, what has changed and what has not? Where does the urgency still apply, and where has the diagnosis shifted?**

### For Personal Reflection

15. **The "Here am I; send me" prayer assumes the speaker knows the cost. Take the prayer slowly. To what specifically might God be calling you that you have already half-imagined, half-refused? What is the cost you are quietly weighing against the call?**

16. **Daniel's prayer life was unspectacular — three times a day, with windows open toward Jerusalem. He did not pray more in crisis; he prayed *the same* in crisis. Is your devotional life crisis-driven or rhythm-based? If the lions came tomorrow, what would your "three times a day" be?**

17. **John in prison wondered whether Jesus was really the One. What in your present circumstance might be quietly raising the same question? Bring it honestly to the One who answered John not with rebuke but with evidence.**

18. **"As he did with John the Baptist, Satan may use uncertainty to make us doubt God's mission for our lives." What uncertainty has been most successful at making you question your calling? Where is that uncertainty showing up right now, and what would it look like to take it seriously without surrendering to it?**

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration

### 1. On the urgency of revival

> *"A revival of true godliness among us is the greatest and most urgent of all our needs. To seek this should be our first work. There must be earnest effort to obtain the blessing of the Lord, not because God is not willing to bestow His blessing upon us, but because we are unprepared to receive it."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Selected Messages*, book 1, p. 121

**Context:** Ellen White wrote of the church's spiritual condition with increasing urgency in the late 19th century. The diagnosis is not that God is withholding — it is that the church is unprepared to receive.

**Discussion prompt:** Connect this to Elijah's Mount Carmel confrontation. The fire fell because Elijah prepared the altar and the people made the choice. What "preparation" does our generation lack? Is the preparation institutional, personal, or both — and what does first work look like for you this week?

### 2. On the wilderness voice that shook Satan

> *"Satan trembled and Satan's hosts trembled, when through John the Baptist the prophecies of the Old Testament were sounded along the line of his preaching."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Desire of Ages*, p. 224 (paraphrased context)

**Context:** EGW describes John's preaching not as a religious phenomenon but as a *spiritual event* — the kingdoms of darkness were destabilized by the announcement of the Lamb.

**Discussion prompt:** What is the difference between religious teaching that is merely true and prophetic teaching that *shakes* the kingdom of darkness? Have you heard the latter? What did it sound like, and what made it different?

### 3. On Ellen White's first vision — eyes fixed on Jesus

> *"If they kept their eyes fixed on Jesus, who was just before them, leading them to the city, they were safe."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Spiritual Gifts*, vol. 2, p. 31

**Context:** Ellen Harmon's first vision in December 1844, given to comfort a community devastated by the Great Disappointment. The path was narrow; the danger was looking away; the safety was Christ.

**Discussion prompt:** The vision was given to the disappointed, not to the triumphant. What is it about disappointment that makes us most likely to take our eyes off Jesus? Where are you currently most disappointed — and what would "eyes fixed on Jesus" look like in *that* place specifically?

## 🛠️ Practical Application

**Individual:**

1. **The [Isaiah 6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6) prayer in slow form.** Read [Isaiah 6:1–8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6.1) aloud once a day for the next week. Stop at "Woe is me, for I am undone" before continuing to "Here am I." Let the order remain — undoing, then sending. Notice what surfaces when you refuse to hurry from one to the other.

2. **Identify your "limping" places.** Make a private list of areas where you are spiritually *limping* — not openly rejecting God, but not fully choosing Him either. For each, name what a single step toward (not full arrival at) wholeheartedness would look like this month.

3. **Read [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) as a love letter.** Spend one extended sitting (30+ minutes) reading the chapter slowly. Underline every action attributed to the Servant. Write in the margin what each accomplished. The exercise is not analysis; it is *receiving*.

**In Sabbath School:**

1. **The five-prophet round-robin.** Assign each of the five prophets (Abraham, Elijah, Isaiah, Daniel, John) to a member. In two minutes each, they describe what made that prophet's calling *costly* and what kept them faithful. Discuss what common pattern emerges.

2. **The "Send me" question.** Ask the class: "What if the question 'Whom shall I send?' is being asked right now — in our community, in our congregation, in our city? What would it require for someone to answer?" Resist the urge to fill the silence too quickly.

3. **John in prison conversation.** Read [Matthew 11:1–6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt11.1) aloud. Ask: "Where in *our* lives are we John in prison — wondering if Jesus is really who we thought?" Let people answer if they wish. Do not rush to resolution; the chapter does not.

**Intellectually:**

1. **The prophetic call form-criticism exercise.** Compare the call narratives of Moses ([Exod. 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex3)), Isaiah ([Isa. 6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa6)), Jeremiah ([Jer. 1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jer1)), Ezekiel ([Ezek. 1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ezek1)–3), Hosea ([Hos. 1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Hos1)), Amos ([Amos 7:14, 15](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Amos7.14)), and Paul ([Acts 9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts9)). What elements recur? Where are they different? What does the variety teach about God's flexibility in calling?

2. **Trace [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) through the New Testament.** Look at how [Acts 8:32–35](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts8.32), [Romans 4:25, 1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom4.25) Peter 2:21–25, and [Matthew 8:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt8.17) use [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53). Each writer applies the chapter differently. What does the cumulative use tell you about how the early church read prophecy?

## 🧵 Theological Threads

- **The remnant principle.** From Genesis to Revelation, God has had "a faithful remnant who have accepted the call to obedience in response to His grace." The prophets are part of the remnant, not separate from it. Their calling is the calling of the remnant intensified.

- **Imputed righteousness in the Patriarchs.** [Genesis 15:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gen15.6) establishes that justification by faith is not a New Testament invention. Paul's argument in [Galatians 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gal3) depends on this verse: the gospel was preached to Abraham in advance ([Gal. 3:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gal3.8)). The Reformation's *sola fide* is the Bible's deep grammar, not a sixteenth-century overlay.

- **Substitutionary atonement in [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53).** The text resists every attempt to soften it into solidarity-suffering or moral example only. The Servant bears what others deserved; the Servant heals by His wounds. Adventist theology has historically held substitution as central, and [Isaiah 53](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa53) is one of its OT anchors.

- **Apocalyptic prophecy and the kingdom.** Daniel's vision of the stone cut without hands ([Dan. 2:44](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan2.44)) and the Son of Man receiving the kingdom ([Dan. 7:13, 14](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Dan7.13)) establish the eschatological horizon. The kingdoms of this world will be displaced — not reformed — by a kingdom not of human making.

- **The forerunner pattern.** John the Baptist is the climactic Old Testament prophet, even though he ministers in the New Testament era. Jesus calls him the greatest born of women ([Matt. 11:11](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt11.11)). His role — preparing the way — is the model for prophetic ministry in any age: not declaring oneself, but pointing to Christ.
