## 🔑 Central Theme

Repentance and forgiveness are the two halves of a single movement: we *turn*, and God *receives*. Neither half stands alone. Repentance without forgiveness produces despair; forgiveness offered to those who refuse to turn produces presumption. The biblical pattern holds them in covenant rhythm — God draws, we turn, He receives, we walk.

The lesson continues the previous week's diagnosis (sin) and remedy (the gospel) by mapping the *experience* of receiving grace. Last week named what sin *is* and what the gospel *accomplished*; this week traces what happens *in the believer* when the gospel's accomplishment becomes the believer's possession.

Four threads run through the week:

- **Repentance is response, not cause** — [Romans 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4): "the goodness of God leads you to repentance." Repentance does not produce God's kindness; God's kindness produces repentance. The order is decisive against any view that makes our turning the precondition of His grace.
- **Repentance is the Spirit's work, exercised by our will** — [John 16:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn16.8); [Acts 11:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.18): "God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life." Repentance is *given*. Yet [Acts 17:30](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts17.30) says God "commands all men everywhere to repent." Both are true; the Spirit moves us, and we move.
- **Forgiveness is real, full, and finished** — [1 John 1:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Jn1.9): God is *faithful* and *just* to forgive. Not reluctant. Not partial. Forgiveness rests on the cross, not on the quality of our confession.
- **The garment is given, not woven** — [Zech 3:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3.4); [Isa 61:10](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa61.10); [Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8); [Matt 22:11–12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22.11). The wedding garment is provided by the host. Worthiness is not earned by self-cleansing but received by accepting the robe.

The Sabbath introduction's image — Mary at Jesus' feet choosing "the one thing needful" — sets the orientation. Repentance is the choice to stop the busy self-justification of Martha-mode and sit, empty-handed, before the only One who can fill us. Forgiveness is what He says when we do.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### [1 John 1:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Jn1.9) (Memory Text)

- **"If we confess our sins":** "Confess" means to "say the same thing as" — to agree with God about what is true. It is not informing Him; it is matching our description to His diagnosis.
- **"He is faithful and just":** Two surprising attributes. Faithfulness, yes — God keeps His promises. But *just* to forgive? Justice would seem to *punish* sin. How is forgiveness "just"? Because Christ has borne the penalty. God can forgive justly because justice has been satisfied at the cross. Forgiveness is not God overlooking sin; it is God dealing with sin in another place.
- **"To forgive us our sins":** The word "forgive" means to send away, release, dismiss. Forgiveness sends the sin away from the sinner ([Ps 103:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps103.12)).
- **"To cleanse us from all unrighteousness":** Two distinct gifts: forgiveness (the record erased) and cleansing (the stain removed). Justification and sanctification, in seed.

### [Luke 10:40–42](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Luke10.40) (Sunday — The One Thing Needful)

- **Context:** Mary and Martha. Jesus enters their home; Martha is "distracted with much serving" (*periespato peri pollēn diakonian*) while Mary sits at His feet.
- **"Distracted":** The word literally means "pulled around" — yanked in multiple directions. Martha's good service has become anxious busyness.
- **"You are worried and troubled about many things":** Jesus' gentle diagnosis names both layers — the cognitive (anxiety, a divided mind) and the affective (tumult, inner agitation).
- **"One thing is needed" (*henos de estin chreia*):** The contrast is "many" (the things Martha is anxious about) vs. "one" (what Mary has chosen). Repentance is, at its root, *choosing the one thing* when the many demand attention.
- **"Mary has chosen the good part, which will not be taken from her":** *Tēn agathēn merida* — "the good portion." Echoes [Ps 73:26](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps73.26): "God is my portion forever." Mary has chosen what cannot be lost. This is the architecture of repentance — letting go of what cannot be kept to receive what cannot be taken.

### [Exodus 34:6–7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex34.6) (Wednesday — The Thirteen Attributes)

- **Context:** After the golden calf disaster, Moses asks to see God's glory ([Exod 33:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex33.18)). God responds by *passing before him and proclaiming His name*. The proclamation of [Exod 34:6–7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex34.6) is God's self-disclosure — the most foundational statement of divine character in the Hebrew Bible.
- **"The LORD, the LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, by no means clearing the guilty":** Rabbinic tradition counts thirteen attributes of mercy in this verse. They are quoted, paraphrased, and echoed throughout the Hebrew Bible ([Num 14:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Num14.18); [Neh 9:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Neh9.17); [Ps 86:15](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps86.15); [Ps 103:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps103.8); [Ps 145:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps145.8); [Joel 2:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Joel2.13); [Jonah 4:2](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jonah4.2)) — more than any other Old Testament passage.
- **"Merciful and gracious":** The Hebrew word for "merciful" (*rachum*) comes from the root for "womb" — God's compassion has the felt quality of a mother's love for the child she has carried. "Gracious" is favor given without prior claim.
- **"Longsuffering":** Literally "long of nostrils" in Hebrew — slow to anger. The idiom pictures anger as something that flares through the nose; God's patience means the flare takes a very long time.
- **"Abounding in goodness and truth":** *Chesed* (steadfast love) and faithfulness — God is loyal and dependable in covenant. These two words together appear throughout the Psalms as the twin heartbeat of God's character.
- **"By no means clearing the guilty":** The final phrase complicates a sentimental reading. God's mercy does not erase moral seriousness. The unrepentant find no cleansing here. Mercy and justice are not opposites; they meet at the cross.

### [John 16:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn16.8) (Monday — The Spirit's Conviction)

- **"And when He has come, He will convict the world of sin, and of righteousness, and of judgment":** *Elenxei* — the same legal-courtroom root as the "evidence" in [Heb 11:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Heb11.1). The Spirit acts as prosecutor, presenting the conclusive proof that disarms denial.
- **"Of sin, because they do not believe in Me" (v. 9):** The root sin is unbelief — refusing the One whom the Father sent. All other sins are downstream.
- **"Of righteousness, because I go to My Father" (v. 10):** Christ's ascension *vindicates* His righteousness. The cross looked like defeat; the resurrection and ascension reveal it as victory.
- **"Of judgment, because the ruler of this world is judged" (v. 11):** Calvary was Satan's judgment ([John 12:31](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn12.31)). The Spirit applies that finished judgment to human hearts, freeing them from the bondage of the accuser.
- **Pastoral implication:** Conviction of sin is not condemnation. The Spirit who convicts is the same Spirit who comforts ([John 14:16](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn14.16)). The voice of conviction draws toward Christ; the voice of condemnation drives away from Him.

### [Zechariah 3:1–5](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3.1) (Wednesday — Joshua's Change of Clothes)

- **Context:** Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the LORD, with Satan at his right hand to oppose him. Joshua is dressed in filthy garments — the Hebrew word is the strongest possible term for defilement, morally repulsive.
- **"The LORD rebuke you, Satan!" (v. 2):** God does not argue Satan's accusation; He silences it. The accuser is told to be quiet, not because the charges are false but because they have been answered elsewhere.
- **"Is this not a brand plucked from the fire?":** *Ud mutsal me'esh* — a charred stick rescued from the flames. The image is of something already saved, blackened but rescued. Joshua *belongs* to God; the accusation cannot reverse that.
- **"Take away the filthy garments from him... Behold, I have removed your iniquity from you, and I will clothe you with rich robes" (v. 4):** Two acts: removal (of filth) and replacement (with clean). The high priest does not change his own clothes; he is changed. This is the experience of forgiveness — passive reception of an active gift.
- **Theological pattern:** Justification (the rich robes given) and sanctification (the filthy garments removed) presented as a single divine action. Both are gift; neither is self-generated.

### [Matthew 22:1–14](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22.1) (Sabbath/Thursday — The Wedding Garment)

- **Context:** The parable of the wedding feast. The king invites guests; many refuse; servants are sent to bring in "both bad and good" from the highways. The wedding hall fills. Then the king sees a man without a wedding garment.
- **"How did you come in here without a wedding garment?" (v. 12):** In ancient Near Eastern hospitality, wedding garments were *provided by the host* to all guests, so that beggar and prince stood equal at the feast. The man's lack of garment was not poverty; it was *refusal of the gift*.
- **"He was speechless" (*ephimōthē*):** The verb means muzzled, silenced — the same word used of Jesus silencing the Sadducees ([Matt 22:34](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22.34)). The man has no defense because there is none. He could have had the garment for the asking.
- **"Many are called, but few are chosen" (v. 14):** A puzzling ending. The "calling" is universal (all are invited); the "chosen" are those who accept *on the king's terms*, including the garment. Election here is not arbitrary divine selection but the response of those who accept the gift.
- **Theological implication:** The wedding garment is the imputed righteousness of Christ ([Isa 61:10](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa61.10); [Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8)). To enter the feast in one's own clothing is to enter on one's own merit — and there is no place for that at this table.

### [Isaiah 61:10](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa61.10) (Sunday/Thursday — Robe of Righteousness)

- **"He has clothed me with the garments of salvation, He has covered me with the robe of righteousness":** Two clothing metaphors side by side — salvation as a garment, righteousness as a robe. Both images stress that the covering is provided, not self-made.
- **"As a bridegroom decks himself with ornaments, and as a bride adorns herself with her jewels":** The clothing is not utilitarian but *bridal*. The redeemed are not merely covered; they are *adorned*. The covering is festal.
- **Echo in Revelation:** [Rev 19:7–8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.7) — "the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready. And to her it was granted to be arrayed in fine linen, clean and bright, for the fine linen is the righteous acts of the saints." The arraying is *granted*; the fine linen is *acts*. Gift and response are inseparable.

### [Romans 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4) (Tuesday — The Goodness That Leads to Repentance)

- **"The goodness of God leads you to repentance":** The verb "leads" is present tense — God's goodness is *continually leading*, not just at the moment of conversion.
- **"Goodness":** The word means useful, practical kindness — the quality of being good *to* someone, not merely good in the abstract. God's kindness is personal and directed toward you.
- **"Despise":** To look down on, to think contemptuously. Paul accuses some hearers of treating God's kindness with contempt — taking it for granted or assuming it permits continued sin.
- **The architecture of repentance:** Not "repent so that God will be kind," but "God's kindness, recognized, produces repentance." The order shapes everything about evangelism, parenting, pastoring. Severity does not produce turning; *seen kindness* does.

### [Acts 11:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.18) (Tuesday — Repentance as Gift)

- **Context:** Peter has reported Cornelius' conversion to the Jerusalem church. The church's response: "Then God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life."
- **"Granted" (*edōken*):** Aorist — at a definite point, God *gave*. Repentance is a divine gift, parallel to the gift of faith ([Eph 2:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Eph2.8)) and the gift of the Spirit ([Acts 11:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.17)).
- **Theological implication:** Repentance cannot be commanded into existence by human effort. It can be commanded ([Acts 17:30](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts17.30)), preached ([Acts 2:38](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts2.38)), pleaded for — but its actual *occurrence* is the Spirit's work. This protects against both Pelagianism (we generate our own repentance) and despair (we cannot repent without divine help).

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On Repentance as Response

1. **[Romans 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4): "The goodness of God leads you to repentance." Yet much preaching has tried to produce repentance through severity — graphic descriptions of sin's consequences, warnings of judgment, fear. When have you experienced *kindness* moving you to repent? When has severity worked, and when has it backfired?** What does Paul's order suggest about how the church should preach?

2. **[Acts 11:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.18) describes repentance as something *God grants*. Yet [Acts 17:30](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts17.30) describes it as something God *commands*. How do these fit together?** Is repentance gift or response, divine or human, gracious or required? What is at stake pastorally in getting the answer wrong?

3. **The biblical words for repentance mean "change of mind" (Greek) and "return" (Hebrew). The English word "repent" often carries emotional weight — sorrow, contrition, tears. Where is the actual locus of biblical repentance: in the feelings, in the mind, in the will, in the action?** Have you ever felt deeply sorry and *not* repented? Have you ever truly repented with relatively little emotion?

4. **Mary chose the "one thing needful" while Martha was distracted with "many things." In your own life, what *many things* are most likely to keep you from the *one thing*?** Are the distractions sinful, or are they good things that have crowded out the better? How do you tell?

### On the Spirit's Work

5. **[John 16:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn16.8) — the Spirit convicts of sin. How do you distinguish the Spirit's conviction from the enemy's condemnation, or from your own internalized guilt?** Conviction leads toward Christ; condemnation drives away. Have you experienced both — and how did you eventually tell them apart?

6. **The Spirit convicts of "sin, righteousness, and judgment" ([John 16:8–11](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn16.8)) — three things, not one. Adventist preaching has often emphasized conviction of sin while saying less about conviction of righteousness (Christ's vindication) and judgment (Satan's defeat). What changes when all three convictions are preached together?**

7. **[Hosea 6:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Hos6.3) — "Let us pursue the knowledge of the LORD. His going forth is established as the morning." Yet [Hosea 6:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Hos6.4) has God lamenting that Israel's love is "like a morning cloud, and like the early dew it goes away." Have you experienced this rhythm in your own walk — moments of vivid devotion that evaporate?** What practices have helped move from cloud-faith to morning-faith?

8. **[Eph 4:30](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Eph4.30) — "Do not grieve the Holy Spirit." The Spirit can be *grieved*. What does it mean to grieve someone who loves you, and how does that change how you think about ongoing sin in a believer's life?** Is the relationship between believer and Spirit fragile or robust — and what's the textual basis for the answer?

### On Forgiveness

9. **[1 John 1:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Jn1.9): God is "faithful and just" to forgive. *Just* — not merciful in spite of justice, but justly forgiving. How does the cross make forgiveness *just*?** Is it possible to preach mercy without atonement, or atonement without mercy, and still preach the gospel?

10. **[Zechariah 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3) — Joshua's filthy garments are removed and replaced with rich robes. He does not change his own clothes. He is changed. What does this say about the believer's role in sanctification?** Is it pure passivity? If not, what is our active part, and what is God's?

11. **The wedding garment parable ([Matt 22:11–12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22.11)) describes a man cast out for entering without a garment — even though he was already at the feast. What does this story warn against?** Can you be in the church, in the feast, *and* still be without the garment? What would that look like?

12. **[Exod 34:6–7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex34.6) — God's most-quoted self-description in the Hebrew Bible. But the passage ends, "by no means clearing the guilty." How does the same passage proclaim both abundant mercy and the refusal to clear the guilty?** What is the integration?

### On Worthiness and Preparation

13. **The lesson's Thursday topic — preparation and worthiness for the marriage supper. Adventist preaching has historically emphasized "perfecting character" before Christ's return. Is this perfection *our* work, *His* work in us, or both? Where is the line?** How do you preach this without producing either anxiety (works) or complacency (nothing more required)?

14. **[Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8) — the bride is "granted" to be arrayed in fine linen, *which is the righteous acts of the saints*. Granted *and* righteous acts. How do you hold gift and works together in this verse without one swallowing the other?**

15. **[Isa 64:6](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Isa64.6) — "All our righteousnesses are like filthy rags." Yet [Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8) calls the saints' righteous acts "fine linen." Same kind of righteousness, two opposite verdicts? Or two different kinds of righteousness?** What is the resolution?

16. **The lesson notes [Gen 3:7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gen3.7) — Adam and Eve's fig leaves were inadequate; God clothed them in skins (an animal had to die). The biblical pattern of inadequate self-clothing replaced by God's provision runs from Eden to the wedding feast. What is the modern equivalent of fig leaves in our spiritual lives?** Where are we still trying to cover ourselves?

### On Practice

17. **[Hosea 10:12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Hos10.12) — "Break up your fallow ground, for it is time to seek the LORD." Fallow ground is land that has been allowed to harden through neglect. Where is the fallow ground in your spiritual life right now?** What "breaking up" would the Spirit be doing, if you let Him?

18. **[Phil 2:12–13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Phil2.12) — "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling, for it is God who works in you." Note the paradox: *you* work, *God* works. How does this collaborative architecture differ from both works-righteousness and quietism?** What does it look like in practice?

19. **Repentance is often imagined as a one-time event at conversion. But Scripture speaks of ongoing repentance — daily turning, regular confession ([1 John 1:9](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Jn1.9) is in present tense). What is the difference between conversion repentance and ongoing repentance?** Why do we lose the second so easily?

20. **The lesson closes with [Matthew 6:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt6.13) — "Do not lead us into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one." Why does Jesus include this petition in *the* prayer He taught? What does it imply about our continued vulnerability even after we have repented and been forgiven?**

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration Points

*Used sparingly, as amplification of Scripture rather than substitute.*

### 1. On the priority of God's drawing

> *"Looking unto Jesus we obtain brighter and more distinct views of God, and by beholding we become changed. Goodness, love for our fellow men, becomes our natural instinct. We develop a character which is the counterpart of the divine character."*
> — *Steps to Christ*, p. 71

**Discussion:** Connect to [Rom 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4) and to Mary's choice of "the one thing needful." Sanctification, in Ellen White's reading, is not first about effort to change but about *beholding*. By looking at Jesus, the seeing itself becomes transformative. What does it mean practically to "look unto Jesus"? Where in your own week is there time for actual gazing — Scripture, prayer, contemplation — and where is gazing crowded out by serving?

### 2. On repentance as gift

> *"Repentance is no less the gift of God than are pardon and justification, and it cannot be experienced except as it is given to the soul by Christ."*
> — *Selected Messages*, vol. 1, p. 393

**Discussion:** Ellen White directly affirms what [Acts 11:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.18) teaches. Repentance is *gift*, not achievement. This protects against the despair of "I cannot repent" and against the pride of "I have repented well." When you next pray for someone far from God, what would it mean to ask not just that they would repent but that *God would grant them* repentance?

### 3. On the wedding garment

> *"The wedding garment in the parable represents the pure, spotless character which Christ's true followers will possess. To the church it is given 'that she should be arrayed in fine linen, clean and white' [[Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8)]... This robe, woven in the loom of heaven, has in it not one thread of human devising."*
> — *Christ's Object Lessons*, p. 311

**Discussion:** "Not one thread of human devising" is a striking phrase. The garment is *entirely* of heaven's weaving. Yet in the same chapter Ellen White also speaks of character developed through choices. How do you hold both? Is the garment imputed righteousness only, or imputed + imparted? What does each emphasis preserve, and what does each emphasis risk losing?

## 🌐 Practical Application for Modern Context

**Individual:**

- **The kindness ledger:** This week, before bed each night, name three specific kindnesses of God you noticed that day. Not abstract gratitude — specific kindness. After a week, look at the list and ask: *To what is His kindness leading me?* [Romans 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4) makes this the architecture of repentance. The kindness must be seen for the turning to happen.

- **The Mary moment:** Choose one fifteen-minute window each day this week — not first thing in the morning, not last thing at night, but a *real* window — and sit at Jesus' feet. No agenda. No reading plan. Just presence. Notice what arises. The point is not productivity but practice. Martha's hospitality was *good*; Mary's was *better*. Choose the better.

- **The wardrobe inventory:** Read [Zechariah 3:1–5](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3.1) slowly. Imagine yourself in Joshua's place — filthy garments, accuser at your right hand, the angel of the LORD standing by. Ask three questions: (1) What are *my* filthy garments — the things the accuser names that are true? (2) Where am I trying to change my own clothes rather than letting God change them? (3) What would it look like to stand still and *receive* the rich robes today?

**In Sabbath School:**

- **The thirteen attributes:** Spend twenty minutes on [Exodus 34:6–7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex34.6). Read it aloud together. Pause on each attribute. Ask: which of these have you personally experienced this year? Which have you preached this year? Which are present in your community's preaching, and which are missing? The passage is the OT's most-quoted text *within the OT itself*; that should tell us something about its weight.

- **Conviction vs. condemnation:** Discuss the difference between conviction (Spirit's work, draws to Christ) and condemnation (accuser's work, drives from Christ). Have members share — anonymously if needed — examples of times they could not tell the difference. What helped them sort it out? What is the role of community in helping believers discern the voices?

- **The wedding garment, locally:** Discuss [Matt 22:11–12](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22.11) in concrete terms. What does it look like in *your* congregation to be at the feast without a wedding garment? Be specific without being accusatory. The point is not to expose anyone; it is to recognize that proximity to the table is not the same as accepting what the host offers.

**Intellectually:**

- **The Old Testament's most-quoted text:** Trace [Exod 34:6–7](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ex34.6) through the Hebrew Bible — [Num 14:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Num14.18); [Neh 9:17](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Neh9.17); [Ps 86:15](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps86.15); [Ps 103:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps103.8); [Ps 145:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Ps145.8); [Joel 2:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Joel2.13); [Jonah 4:2](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jonah4.2). Each citation emphasizes a different attribute. What does this pattern say about how the OT theologians understood the heart of God? Where is this passage's weight in NT preaching, and where is it underweighted?

- **The contrition controversy:** Reformed theology distinguishes *attrition* (sorrow for sin out of fear of punishment) from *contrition* (sorrow for sin because it has wounded God). Catholic theology accepts attrition as sufficient with the sacrament; Protestant theology requires contrition. What does the Bible say about the role of fear in repentance? Is fear-sorrow *real* repentance, or only the first stirring? [2 Cor 7:10](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.2Cor7.10): "godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death."

- **The *teshuvah* tradition:** Jewish theology has rich resources on repentance — Maimonides' four conditions of complete *teshuvah* (regret, ceasing the sin, confession, resolve), the Days of Awe between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, the practice of seeking forgiveness from those harmed before seeking it from God. What does Christian theology gain by engaging this tradition, and where does it diverge?

## 🧵 Theological Threads to Follow

- **Repentance and the new birth:** [John 3:3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Jn3.3); Tit 3:5 — the new birth is the Spirit's work, beyond the believer's capacity to produce. Repentance accompanies regeneration. Trace how these two — turning and being-made-new — relate to each other in Scripture. Are they simultaneous, sequential, or two aspects of one event?

- **Forgiveness and the high priestly ministry of Christ:** The Adventist sanctuary doctrine places ongoing forgiveness in the framework of Christ's heavenly priesthood ([Heb 7:25](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Heb7.25); [1 John 2:1](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.1Jn2.1)). Justification at the cross opened the way; intercession at the throne applies it daily. Study how this shapes the believer's confidence — and humility — in approaching God after sin.

- **Forgiveness and human relationships:** [Matt 6:12, 14–15](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt6.12); [Matt 18:21–35](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt18.21); [Eph 4:32](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Eph4.32); [Col 3:13](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Col3.13) — receiving God's forgiveness obligates extending it to others. The two are not transactional ("I forgive so God will forgive") but cardiogenic (the heart that receives mercy gives it). Study where the church most struggles with this — and where you most struggle personally.

- **The robe of Christ's righteousness:** A motif from Eden ([Gen 3:21](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Gen3.21)) to Joshua ([Zech 3](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3)) to the wedding feast ([Matt 22](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Matt22)) to the marriage of the Lamb ([Rev 19:8](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rev19.8)). God clothes those who cannot clothe themselves. This is the visual gospel — and the visual answer to the perennial Adventist question, "Am I ready?"

- **Repentance and revival:** Historic revivals — Wesleyan, Cane Ridge, the Welsh revival, 1888 — have been marked by deep conviction of sin, broken confession, and tangible forgiveness. The Adventist movement's own revival hopes ([Joel 2:28–29](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Joel2.28); the "latter rain") presume widespread repentance preceding the outpouring. What role does individual and corporate repentance play in your own community's hope for awakening?

## 📝 Lesson Summary for Quick Reference

**Core assertion:** Repentance is the Spirit-given turning of the heart back to God; forgiveness is God's faithful and just reception of those who turn. The two move together. Neither is produced by human effort alone; both are received from God and exercised by us. The architecture of grace is: God draws → we turn → He receives → we walk.

**Key tensions the lesson navigates:**

1. **Repentance as gift vs. repentance as response** — Both. God grants repentance ([Acts 11:18](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Acts11.18)); we exercise it. The Spirit moves; the will responds.
2. **Goodness vs. severity in producing repentance** — [Romans 2:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Rom2.4) sides clearly with goodness. Fear can startle; only love transforms.
3. **Justification vs. sanctification** — Joshua's filthy garments removed *and* rich robes added ([Zech 3:4](https://ref.ly/logosref/Bible.Zech3.4)). Two distinct gifts of the same divine act.
4. **Worthiness given vs. worthiness earned** — The wedding garment is provided by the host. Refusing the garment, not lacking the funds for one, is what excludes.

**The bottom line:** You do not have to earn your way to God's table. The garment is hanging in the closet of heaven, woven by Christ's life and death, offered to anyone who will receive it. Repentance is the open hand that lets the garment be placed on you. Forgiveness is what God has been doing all along — and is doing now, in this moment, for everyone who turns. The kindness of God is, even right now, leading toward repentance. The question is whether the kindness will be despised, taken for granted, or recognized.
