## 🔑 Central Theme

Repentance is not primarily a human achievement — it is a divine gift elicited by God's own character. When God proclaims His name to Moses in Exodus 34:6–7 ("merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abundant in lovingkindness") He is not simply consoling Moses: He is revealing the engine of repentance. We repent because of *who God is*, not because we have worked ourselves into an appropriate emotional state. Paul makes this explicit: "the goodness of God leads you to repentance" (Rom 2:4).

The visual center of this lesson is the **garment motif**, a thread that runs from Genesis 3 to Revelation 19. Adam and Eve sew fig leaves (human covering for sin); God replaces them with animal skins (divine covering, foreshadowing sacrifice). Isaiah describes our righteousness as "a polluted garment" (Isa 64:6). Zechariah 3 shows the high priest Joshua stripped of filthy garments and reclothed — the prophetic image of justification. Isaiah 61:10 rejoices in being "covered with the robe of righteousness." Revelation 19:8 describes the Bride of Christ in "fine linen, bright and pure." The lesson's invitation: stop trying to fix your own garments. Receive His.

## 📚 Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### 1 John 1:9 (Memory Text)
- **Homologeō (ὁμολογέω):** "to confess" literally means "to say the same thing" — to agree with God's assessment of our sin. This is not self-flagellation or emotional breakdown; it is honest alignment with reality as God sees it.
- **Pistos kai dikaios (πιστὸς καὶ δίκαιος):** "faithful and just" — two distinct divine attributes. *Faithful* because God cannot deny His own covenant promise; *just* because the cross has already satisfied the legal requirement for forgiveness. God doesn't forgive by overlooking sin; He forgives because Christ bore its penalty.
- **Katharizō (καθαρίζω):** "to cleanse" — not merely to pardon but to purify. The forgiveness here is both forensic (declared clean) and transformative (made clean). This single verse holds justification and sanctification together.

### Exodus 34:1–10 (Sabbath and Wednesday anchor)
- **Context:** Moses encounters God *after* the golden calf catastrophe (Exod 32). The first tablets were broken; the covenant appeared shattered. God could have abandoned Israel. Instead He calls Moses back up Sinai, carves new tablets, and *reinitiates* the covenant.
- **The Name Proclamation (v.6–7):** This is the most quoted divine self-description in the Hebrew Bible — echoed in Num 14:18, Neh 9:17, Ps 86:15, 103:8, Joel 2:13, Jonah 4:2. The sevenfold character of God:
  - *Rahum* (רַחוּם) — compassionate/merciful (from *rechem*, womb)
  - *Hannun* (חַנּוּן) — gracious
  - *Erek appayim* (אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם) — slow to anger (lit. "long of nostrils")
  - *Rab hesed* (רַב חֶסֶד) — abundant in steadfast love
  - *Emet* (אֱמֶת) — faithful/truth
  - *Nosei avon* (נֹשֵׁא עָוֺן) — bearing iniquity
  - *Nakeh lo yenakeh* (נַקֵּה לֹא יְנַקֶּה) — will by no means clear the guilty
- **The tension of v.7:** God "forgives iniquity, transgression, and sin" AND "will by no means clear the guilty." This is not a contradiction — it is the tension the cross resolves. Paul unpacks this in Romans 3:25–26: God is both just and the justifier.

### Zechariah 3:4 (Sunday and Wednesday)
- **Context:** The prophet sees Joshua the high priest standing before the Angel of the LORD "clothed with filthy garments." Satan stands at his right hand to accuse him.
- **Filthy garments (*tsō'im*):** The Hebrew suggests fecal contamination — the most extreme uncleanness possible for a high priest. This is not mild moral failure; it is comprehensive ritual disqualification.
- **The exchange:** Without Joshua doing anything, the Angel commands: "Take the filthy garments from him." Then to Joshua: "Behold, I have caused your iniquity to pass from you, and I will clothe you with rich apparel." Joshua's role is entirely passive in the transaction of forgiveness. This is the visual grammar of justification: our contribution is zero; God's is total.
- **Relevance to the lesson:** Every day we stand before God as Joshua stood — filthy garments, accuser at our right hand. The response to accusation is not better self-management; it is to look to the One who exchanges garments.

### Romans 2:4 (Tuesday)
- **"The goodness of God leads you to repentance":** The Greek *agei* (ἄγει) is present tense — it *is leading*, continually. The goodness of God is not a past event that once triggered repentance; it is an ongoing divine initiative that keeps beckoning.
- **The context:** Paul is addressing people who judge others while committing the same sins. The real scandal here is presuming on God's patience without letting it work its intended effect: *repentance*. Patience and forbearance (*anochē*) are not divine indifference; they are a prolonged invitation.

### Philippians 2:12–13 (Friday)
- **The synergism question:** "Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; *for* it is God who works in you." The *for* (ὅτι) is crucial — human effort is grounded in and made possible by divine operation. The grammar does not say "work, and then God works." It says: work *because* God is already working.
- **Katergazomai (κατεργάζομαι):** "Work out" — not "work for." The salvation being worked out is one already received. This is progressive sanctification, not meritorious earning.
- **Fear and trembling:** Not terror but *seriousness* — the opposite of spiritual casualness. Knowing that the God of the universe is working within us should produce a kind of awed engagement with the process of growth.

## 💡 Deep Discussion Questions

### On the Nature of Repentance
1. **Romans 2:4 says God's goodness leads to repentance. But experientially, many people are brought to repentance through suffering, loss, or fear. What is the relationship between the "goodness" route and the "hard road" route? Are they the same dynamic, or fundamentally different?**

2. **The lesson makes a distinction between genuine repentance and mere remorse. What markers distinguish them? Is it possible to have authentic sorrow over sin without genuine change — and if so, what does that reveal about the human condition?**

3. **Tuesday's texts show repentance as central to every stage of the gospel: John's preaching, Jesus' preaching, the apostolic commission (Luke 24:46–47), Paul's ministry (Acts 20:21). Why has repentance become less central in many contemporary Christian presentations of the gospel? What is lost when it is minimized?**

4. **Matthew 3:8 commands "fruit worthy of repentance." What does repentance-fruit actually look like in a modern Adventist context? Give a concrete example — not a theological principle, but an observable change in behavior or relationship.**

### On the Garment Imagery
5. **The robe metaphor runs from Genesis 3 (fig leaves) through Zechariah 3 (filthy garments replaced) to Revelation 19 (fine linen of the saints). How does this thread change your understanding of what salvation is? If you had to explain justification to someone using only this imagery, how would you?**

6. **In the wedding feast parable (Matt 22:11–12), the man without a wedding garment is speechless — he has no defense. What does his silence mean? Is he guilty because he refuses the garment, or because he thinks his own clothing is adequate?**

7. **Revelation 19:8 says the fine linen "is the righteous acts of the saints," while Revelation 7:14 says the saints "washed their robes in the blood of the Lamb." How do we hold those two descriptions together without collapsing into either pure passive justification or salvation-by-works?**

### On the Holy Spirit's Role
8. **John 16:8 says the Spirit "convicts the world in respect of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment." Note: the conviction is not just of sin, but of *righteousness* and *judgment* too. What does it mean for the Spirit to convict us of righteousness? Of judgment? Why are all three needed for genuine repentance?**

9. **Ephesians 4:30 warns against "grieving" the Holy Spirit. How does someone grieve the Spirit without necessarily committing a dramatic outward sin? What are the subtler forms of resisting the Spirit's work of conviction?**

10. **The lesson says we should respond to the Spirit's promptings rather than justify wrongdoing. But in pastoral practice, how do we distinguish between the Spirit's conviction and shame/guilt from other sources — wounds, perfectionism, manipulation? Is all conviction good?**

### On God's Character and Forgiveness
11. **Exodus 34:6–7 holds together radical forgiveness ("forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin") and radical justice ("will by no means clear the guilty"). Every Adventist quotes verse 6; almost no one quotes verse 7 in the same breath. What theological damage results from treating these as separate rather than unified aspects of God's character?**

12. **The publican in Luke 18:13 beats his chest and says "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" — and Jesus says he went home *justified*. The Pharisee had a better track record by any measurable standard. What does this story tell us about the relationship between self-knowledge and justification? Is ignorance of your own sinfulness itself a spiritual problem?**

13. **Romans 5:20 says "where sin abounded, grace abounded all the more." Paul immediately anticipates the objection in 6:1: "Shall we continue in sin so that grace may increase?" The abounding-grace truth is apparently dangerous enough that the Spirit prompted Paul to preemptively answer the abuse. How do we preach radical grace without producing that response? Is it even possible — or is the antinomian misuse of grace a sign the message is being preached correctly?**

### On Practical Repentance and Sanctification
14. **Hosea 10:12 says "break up your fallow ground" — an agricultural image of hardened, unworked soil. What conditions of the heart produce spiritual fallow ground? What does "breaking it up" actually look like in your devotional life this week?**

15. **Philippians 2:12–13 says to work out salvation "with fear and trembling" because God is working in you. What does that reverential seriousness (*not* anxiety) look like in daily life? How does it shape decisions, conversations, and the way you respond to spiritual promptings?**

16. **Deuteronomy 4:4 says those who "cleaved" to Jehovah are alive today. What does *cleaving* look like in the twenty-first century — when the constant pressure is toward distraction, fragmentation, and surface-level spirituality? What is the modern equivalent of Israel's temptation to turn from Jehovah toward Baal-peor?**

### Harder/Edge Questions
17. **The golden calf incident (Exod 32–34) ends with covenant renewal — but also 3,000 people killed by the Levites (Exod 32:28) and a plague (32:35). Forgiveness and consequences coexist. How does this shape how Adventists should understand the relationship between justification and temporal consequences of sin? Does forgiveness erase consequences?**

18. **Revelation 3:19 says, "As many as I love, I reprove and chasten: be zealous therefore, and repent." Chastening is an expression of love. How does this reframe suffering that drives us toward repentance? Is there a form of "loving chastening" that looks indistinguishable from natural consequence?**

19. **Matthew 22:14 — "many are called, but few are chosen." In context, the man without the wedding garment was present at the feast; he accepted the invitation but refused the garment. What does this say about the possibility of being part of the church, engaged with the gospel, and yet fundamentally unprepared? What is the "garment" that Adventism needs to put on corporately?**

20. **Hosea 6 (referenced twice in this lesson's texts) records God's famous lament: "Your love is like morning mist, like the early dew that disappears." Israel's repentance was thin and transient. How do we move from *transient* repentance (emotional, momentary) to the *cleaving* of Deuteronomy 4? What sustains repentance-turned-transformation over years, not moments?**

## ✍️ Ellen White Integration Points

Use sparingly — as amplification of scriptural truth, not replacement for it.

**1. On what genuine repentance is:**
> *"Repentance includes sorrow for sin and a turning away from it. We shall not renounce sin unless we see its sinfulness; until we turn away from it in heart, there will be no real change in the life."*
> — *Steps to Christ*, p. 23

*Discussion use:* This answers the question of why behavior doesn't change even after repeated confessions. The emotion of sorrow without the *turning* of the will is incomplete. Pairs with Tuesday's texts on "fruit worthy of repentance" (Matt 3:8).

**2. On the wedding garment:**
> *"The guests at the gospel feast are those who profess to serve God, those whose names are on the church roll. The wedding garment in the parable represents the character — the pure, spotless character which Christ's true followers will possess."*
> — *Christ's Object Lessons*, p. 307

*Discussion use:* This grounds Thursday's parable. The garment is not a one-time transaction but a character formed through ongoing relationship. Raises the question: how does character become the garment? (Phil 2:12-13 answers.)

**3. On God initiating repentance:**
> *"It is the grace of Christ that calls forth in us repentance for sin. Our very first step toward repentance, even the very first impulse toward goodness, is awakened by the Spirit of Christ working upon our hearts."*
> — *Steps to Christ*, p. 26

*Discussion use:* Directly addresses the common misunderstanding that we must generate repentance in order to qualify for grace. Repentance is itself a gift — which means even our turning toward God is evidence He is already at work. Connects to Romans 2:4 and the Holy Spirit's role in Monday's texts.

## 🌐 Practical Application for Modern Context

**Individual:**
- **Audit your garment:** This week, identify one area of your life where you have been wearing "fig leaves" — your own solution to a spiritual problem — rather than letting God recloth you. Name it specifically; bring it explicitly to 1 John 1:9.
- **Practice *homologeō*:** In your prayer time, try agreeing with God about one specific sin rather than making general confessions. "I took credit for what belonged to You." "I chose comfort over honesty." Specificity is not morbid self-focus; it is what genuine agreement with God's assessment looks like.
- **Hosea 10:12 exercise:** Identify one area of spiritual hardness — a topic you've stopped praying about, a relationship you've stopped hoping could change, a conviction you've stopped pursuing. That's fallow ground. Choose one concrete action to break it up this week.

**In Sabbath School:**
- Discuss: What is the difference between guilt that leads to repentance and guilt that leads to shame? How do you tell them apart in your own experience?
- Share: Has there been a time when God's goodness (rather than fear of consequences) led you to repentance? What made you aware of His goodness in that moment?

**Intellectually:**
- The tension in Phil 2:12–13 maps onto one of philosophy's oldest questions: compatibilism (human freedom and divine sovereignty coexisting). Adventism navigates this differently from Calvinism and Arminianism. Be able to articulate the Adventist position — not as a debate point but as a resource for pastoral encouragement when someone asks: "Is it me or God who makes me change?"

## 🧵 Theological Threads to Follow

- **Imputed vs. Imparted Righteousness:** Adventism has historically held both — justification (Christ's righteousness imputed/credited to us) and sanctification (Christ's righteousness imparted/developed in us). The garment motif holds both: the robe is *given* (justification), but Revelation 19:8 says the fine linen is "the righteous acts of the saints" (sanctification). How these relate without collapsing is the ongoing Adventist conversation.
- **The Exodus 34 Character Proclamation as Canonical Center:** This self-description of God is quoted or echoed more than any other divine self-disclosure in Scripture. Understanding it deeply reshapes how we read repentance, judgment, and forgiveness across both Testaments.
- **The High Priest Typology:** Zechariah 3 and Leviticus 16 together form the backdrop for Hebrews 4:14–16 — Jesus as our High Priest who sympathizes with our weakness. The filthy garments of Zechariah 3 are what Jesus takes on; the clean garments He provides are what we receive.
- **Working Toward Revelation 19:** The Bride of Christ in Revelation 19 is clothed in fine linen — a direct echo of the individual repentance-and-reclothing narrative of this lesson, now resolved corporately. The judgment-hour message is not just personal readiness; it is a collective *becoming* the Bride.

## 📝 Lesson Summary for Quick Reference

**Core assertion:** Repentance is not a human achievement that earns God's forgiveness — it is a response to God's own character, initiated by His Spirit, issuing in genuine turning (not just sorrow), and made possible by the exchange that Zechariah 3, Isaiah 61, and 1 John 1:9 describe: His robe for our filthy garments.

**Key tensions the lesson navigates:**
1. God's radical mercy ↔ God's absolute justice (Exod 34:6–7)
2. Passive reception of forgiveness ↔ Active "working out" of salvation (Phil 2:12–13)
3. The immediacy of justification ↔ The ongoing process of sanctification
4. Individual repentance ↔ Corporate readiness (the wedding feast parable)

**The bottom line:** You cannot grow in a relationship with God while holding on to your fig leaves. The lesson's invitation is not moral improvement — it is *exchange*. Bring your filthy garments to Him. He has been waiting with clean ones since before you noticed yours were dirty.
