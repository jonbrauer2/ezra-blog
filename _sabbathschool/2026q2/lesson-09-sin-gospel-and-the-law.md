## Central Theme

This lesson arrives at the theological center of Adventist faith — the triangular relationship between sin, the gospel, and the law. These three are not competing doctrines but a unified relational grammar: sin disrupts the relationship with God that the quarter's theme calls us to grow in (*Isaiah 59:2*); the gospel restores it through Christ's substitutionary work (*Romans 3:24–28*); and the law describes what love looks like in that restored relationship (*Matthew 22:40*). To grow in relationship with God requires honest reckoning with all three.

The lesson uses Samson as a sustained negative case study running beneath the week's readings. He was called, gifted, and Spirit-empowered — yet lost his relationship with God not through dramatic apostasy but through accumulated small compromises: wrong relationships, ignored warnings, half-kept secrets, and the fatal misreading of his own spiritual state. The Laodicean church mirrors this: "I am rich, prosperous, need nothing" — yet Christ stands *outside* the door of his own people's assembly. The week's diagnostic question is not "Am I saved?" but "What has actually taken root between me and God?"

The resolution is not self-reformation. Wednesday's reading anchors the week in *justification by faith* — God declares righteous the one who trusts in Christ, not the one who performs adequately. The law's role is then rightly restored: not a ladder to earn relationship but a portrait of the God we are being conformed to, the shape of love in practice, the grammar of the kingdom the wise builder is constructing word by word on the rock of Christ.

## Key Passages — Exegetical Notes

### Psalm 119:93–94 (Memory Text)

- **Context:** The longest psalm is an acrostic meditation on *tôrâ* (instruction, revelation, law). The psalmist's love for the law is categorically different from moralism — it is relational. Verse 94 ("I am Yours") provides the interpretive key: law is loved because it is the Word of the covenant Partner.
- **"You have given me life" (*ḥiyyîtānî*):** The Piel of *ḥāyâ* — not merely "preserved" but *quickened, enlivened*. Torah is life-giving not because rule-following extends life but because it reveals the Life-Giver's character. The law has the same vivifying quality as the Creator's breath in Genesis 2:7.
- **"I am Yours, save me" (*lekā ʾănî hôšîʿēnî*):** Covenant identity precedes petition. "I am Yours" is not an achievement claim but a possessive statement of belonging — the basis on which the plea for salvation rests. Relationship comes before rescue, not rescue before relationship.
- **Implication:** The psalmist's love of law is neither legalism nor Pharisaism — it is the natural response of one who knows whose they are. Law-keeping that flows from "I am Yours" is categorically different from law-keeping to *earn* belonging.

### Romans 3:20 (Tuesday — The Law's Diagnostic Function)

- **"Through the law comes knowledge of sin" (*epignōsis*):** The Greek *epignōsis* is intensive — not mere awareness but *precise, targeted recognition*. The law does not create sin; it names it accurately. A medical diagnosis does not cause the disease — it identifies what was already there.
- **The law's proper function vs. its limits:** Paul is not diminishing the law — he calls it "holy, righteous, and good" in Romans 7:12. He is clarifying its proper role: the law reveals the problem; only grace provides the solution. Confusing the diagnosis with the cure is the root error of every form of moralistic religion.

### Matthew 5:17–18 (Wednesday — Jesus Fulfills the Law)

- **"I have not come to abolish (*kataluō*) but to fulfill (*plēroō*):"** *Kataluō* means to break down, demolish, destroy — the same word used of demolishing the Temple (Matt 24:2). *Plēroō* means to fill full, complete, bring to its intended meaning. Jesus does not replace the law; he reveals its full depth. The antitheses that follow ("you have heard... but I say unto you") do not relax the law — they penetrate to its root intention.
- **"Not one jot or tittle will pass from the Law" (*iōta hēn ē mia keraia*):** The iota is the smallest Hebrew letter (yod); a tittle is a tiny calligraphic stroke distinguishing letters. Jesus's language is emphatic: the moral structure inscribed in the law is as permanent as the creation order itself.
- **The Adventist hermeneutical stake:** This text is foundational to the Adventist defense of Sabbath observance. If not one tittle passes until heaven and earth pass away, the fourth commandment stands. Fulfillment-as-termination arguments must account for Jesus's own language here.

### 1 John 3:4 (Tuesday — Sin Defined)

- **"Sin is lawlessness" (*hē hamartia estin hē anomia*):** The equation is absolute and bidirectional — every sin is an act of lawlessness; every act of lawlessness is sin. John does not say sin *produces* lawlessness; they are definitionally identical.
- **"Lawlessness" (*anomia*):** From *a* (without) + *nomos* (law). The sinner acts as though there is no law — no moral order, no Creator's claim, no standard above the self.
- **Implication:** If sin = lawlessness, then the continuing validity of the law is built into the New Testament's most concise definition of sin. Abolish the law and you abolish the definition of sin along with it.

### Romans 3:28 (Wednesday — Justification by Faith)

- **"A person is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (*chōris ergōn nomou*):** The passive *dikaiousthai* — "to be declared righteous" — is a forensic term. God as judge pronounces a verdict of acquittal based on Christ's righteousness credited to the believer's account.
- **Apparent tension with James 2:24:** James says "not by faith alone." Resolution: Paul uses "justified" forensically (declared righteous before God's tribunal); James uses it demonstratively (shown/evidenced as righteous by fruit). Paul addresses how we *receive* righteousness; James addresses how we *display* it.

### Galatians 3:13 (Wednesday — Redemption from the Curse)

- **"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (*genomenos hyper hēmōn katara*):** Paul quotes Deuteronomy 21:23 about criminals hung on trees — the most shameful death in the ancient world — and applies it to the Son of God.
- **The logic of substitution:** Law condemns lawbreakers → we are lawbreakers → Christ became our substitute, taking the condemnation → we receive his righteousness by faith. The transaction works precisely *because* the law's verdict was real and genuinely executed — on him.
- **The law's integrity preserved:** Substitutionary atonement is not God ignoring the law's demands; it is God satisfying them on our behalf. This is why the gospel does not abolish the law — it upholds it (*Rom 3:31*).

### Mark 9:42–48 (Sabbath/Monday — Radical Removal of Stumbling Blocks)

- **"Whoever causes one of these little ones to sin (*skandalisē*):"** The word *skandalon* is a trap-trigger, a device that causes someone to stumble and fall.
- **The hyperbole of cutting off hand or eye:** Jesus uses graphic exaggeration (*hyperbole*) to force moral seriousness: "treat the things that lead you to sin as if they were a limb — no attachment is too precious if it is pulling you away from God."
- **Connection to Samson:** Samson did not lose his calling in one catastrophic decision. He ignored small warning signs, made pragmatic compromises, and told himself each threshold was not the real one. Mark 9's radical language is the antidote to Samson's casualness about what he allowed into his life.

### Revelation 3:14–22 (Sunday — The Laodicean Church)

- **"You are neither cold nor hot" (*oute psychros ei oute zestos*):** Laodicea's water supply was notoriously lukewarm — tepid aqueduct vs. the hot springs of Hierapolis and cold fresh springs of Colossae. The church had become like its water: what should have been medicinal or refreshing had become useless.
- **"I will spit you out of my mouth" (*emesai se*):** The lukewarm church has made itself nauseating. The self-satisfied condition ("I am rich and have need of nothing") is precisely the condition that blinds itself to its own spiritual state.
- **"Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (v.20):** This text is addressed to the *church*, not to unbelievers. The Risen Christ is outside his own people's assembly — they have replaced his presence with wealth, programs, self-congratulation. The invitation to "open the door" is a call to restored relationship from the outside.

### Matthew 7:24–29 (Thursday — Wise and Foolish Builders)

- **"Everyone who hears these words of mine and does them (*poiōn*):"** *Poiōn* is present active participle — ongoing, habitual doing. The wise builder is not someone who once committed to obedience; he is someone who keeps building day by day on Christ's words.
- **"It had been founded on the rock" (*tethemeliōto gar epi tēn petran*):** The Greek perfect passive emphasizes completed foundation — the work of establishing it is *done*. Foundations are laid in ordinary, unspectacular seasons of discipleship, not during the storm.
- **The closing logic of the Sermon on the Mount:** This parable closes an address that began with character (Beatitudes), moved through ethics (anger, lust, oaths, love of enemies), and now concludes with the integration of hearing and doing. "These words of mine" = the entire Sermon = the law fulfilled in Jesus's teaching.

## Deep Discussion Questions

### On Sin and Self-Deception

1. **Samson is described as having "the Spirit of the LORD" move upon him (Judges 14:6; 15:14) even while pursuing Philistine relationships that would eventually destroy him.** How do we account for spiritual gifts and divine empowerment coexisting with profound moral compromise? Does the presence of spiritual gifts tell us anything reliable about a person's spiritual state?

2. **The Laodicean church (Revelation 3:17) says "I am rich, prosperous, and need nothing" — yet is simultaneously "wretched, pitiable, poor, blind, and naked."** How does spiritual self-deception work at this scale? What conditions make a community this wrong about themselves? What does it take to break through such self-deception in a congregation?

3. **Isaiah 59:2 says sin "separates you from God."** Does sin merely *damage* the relationship with God, or does it genuinely *sever* it? Is there a difference between broken fellowship and broken relationship? What difference does it make practically to answer this question one way or the other?

4. **Monday's reading focuses on "strongholds" — entrenched patterns of sin.** 2 Corinthians 10:4-5 describes strongholds as "arguments and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God." What is the connection between intellectual strongholds (wrong ideas about God) and moral strongholds (sinful patterns)? Which produces which?

### On the Law

5. **1 John 3:4 defines sin as *anomia* — lawlessness.** This makes sin fundamentally about God's moral order, not just personal harm or social disruption. How does this definition change your working understanding of sin? Can you identify behaviors that are widely tolerated socially but are *anomia* by this definition?

6. **Romans 3:20 says the law gives *epignōsis* — precise, targeted knowledge — of sin.** If conscience already tells us right from wrong (Romans 2:14-15), what does the law *add*? What is the difference between conscience-level knowledge of sin and law-level knowledge?

7. **The Ten Commandments begin with "I am the LORD your God who brought you out of Egypt" — redemptive identity before any command.** What does it mean that law-giving follows deliverance rather than preceding it? How does this sequence reframe law as relational gift rather than conditional requirement?

8. **The Sermon on the Mount radicalizes the law — anger equals murder, lust equals adultery — making it seemingly harder to keep.** Yet Jesus says his yoke is "easy" and his burden "light" (Matthew 11:30). How do you hold both claims together?

### On Justification

9. **Galatians 3:13 says "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" — quoting Deuteronomy 21:23 about criminals hung on trees.** Paul applies the most shameful death-text in the Old Testament to the Son of God. What does this choice of texts tell us about how deeply God was willing to enter human degradation to redeem us?

10. **Romans 3:28 says justification is "by faith apart from works of the law."** Does the forgiveness of law-breaking make the law irrelevant, or does it do the opposite — demonstrate that the law's verdict was real and had to be genuinely paid? How does substitutionary atonement *uphold* the law (Romans 3:31) rather than abolish it?

11. **Philippians 3:9 contrasts "a righteousness of my own that comes from the law" with "that which comes through faith in Christ."** These are genuinely alternative forms of righteousness. What is the practical difference between *imputed* righteousness and *earned* righteousness? How do you detect which form you are actually operating from?

### On Knowing and Doing

12. **John 17:3 defines eternal life as *knowing* God — not obeying God, not knowing *about* God.** Hosea 4:6 says God's people "are destroyed for lack of knowledge." How does *knowing God* relate to *keeping commandments*? Can you keep commandments without knowing God? Can you genuinely know God and not keep commandments?

13. **The wise builder and the foolish builder both *heard* Jesus's words.** What is it about hearing without doing that is spiritually dangerous? Is nominal Christianity more dangerous than honest unbelief?

14. **The Greek NT uses *ginōskō* (relational, experiential knowing) vs. *eidō* (informational knowing).** Jesus says in Matthew 7:23 "I never *knew* you" (*egnōn*, from *ginōskō*). What distinguishes these two kinds of knowledge in your own faith? How does relational knowing of God produce obedience organically rather than as duty?

### On Law and Gospel Together

15. **Matthew 5:17 says Jesus came to *fulfill* (*plēroō*) the law.** One interpretation: fulfillment = completion = termination. Another: fulfillment = filling to full depth. Which is more consistent with Matthew 5:21–48, where Jesus *intensifies* the law's demands?

16. **Revelation 14:12 describes end-time saints as those who "keep the commandments of God and the faith of Jesus."** Law-keeping and faith appear as unified marks of the same community. Does faith *produce* commandment-keeping, or do they grow simultaneously from the same root?

17. **The lesson title places sin, the gospel, and the law in a single frame.** How would you describe the genuine Adventist integration of these three to someone who sees them as contradictory?

### Synthesis

18. **Samson, the Laodicean church, and the foolish builder share a pattern: they all had access to divine resources and all collapsed under pressure.** Is there an area of your own life where you have been relying on spiritual history, theological knowledge, or ministry activity as a substitute for an actual relationship with Christ?

19. **Mark 9's graphic language about cutting off hands and feet is the New Testament's most urgent description of how seriously to treat spiritual stumbling blocks.** What single thing, if removed from your life, would most accelerate your relationship with God?

20. **After this week's lesson — sin accurately diagnosed, the gospel fully received, the law understood as life-giving — what one concrete thing would you do differently starting this Sabbath?**

## Ellen White Integration

### 1. The Law as God's Character

> *"The law of God is as sacred as God Himself. It is a revelation of His will, a transcript of His character, the expression of divine love and wisdom."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Great Controversy*, p. 467

**Discussion prompt:** If the law is a portrait of God's character rather than an arbitrary rule-set, how does that change your emotional relationship to law-keeping? Does it feel like a burden or like a description of the person you're becoming?

---

### 2. Substitution: The Heart of the Gospel

> *"Christ was treated as we deserve, that we might be treated as He deserves. He was condemned for our sins, in which He had no share, that we might be justified by His righteousness, in which we had no share. He suffered the death which was ours, that we might receive the life which was His."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *The Desire of Ages*, p. 25

**Discussion prompt:** Substitutionary atonement means the law's verdict was real, the penalty was genuinely paid, and the righteousness transferred is genuinely Christ's. Which of these three elements do you find hardest to actually believe about yourself?

---

### 3. Obedience as Love's Natural Language

> *"When we submit ourselves to Christ, the heart is united with His heart, the will is merged in His will, the mind becomes one with His mind, the thoughts are brought into captivity to Him; we live His life. This is what it means to be clothed with the garment of His righteousness."*
> — **Ellen G. White**, *Steps to Christ*, p. 62

**Discussion prompt:** Ellen White describes obedience not as compliance with rules but as the natural overflow of union with Christ. Is there a part of your Christian life that still feels like compliance rather than overflow? What would have to change for it to become the second?

## Practical Application

### Individual

- **Laodicean audit:** Read Revelation 3:14–22 slowly. Write down three specific areas where you are tempted toward the Laodicean condition — where self-sufficiency, comfort, or accumulated routine has insulated you from dependence on Christ.
- **Law as portrait exercise:** Read the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:3–17) as a character description of God, not a requirement list. Each commandment points to something God *is*: faithful, non-manipulable, truthful, rest-giving, honoring, life-protecting, faithful in covenant, fair, truthful, content. Which attribute of God do you most need to internalize this week?
- **Samson inventory:** Identify one "small compromise" you have made in the last month that you have not taken seriously. Bring it before God in the mode of Psalm 51:1–4.

### Sabbath School Class

- **Three-column exercise:** Draw three columns: *Sin* (what is broken), *Gospel* (what God did about it), *Law* (how it looks when it's working). Have the class fill in rows from each day's readings. Discuss: which column do we as a class spend the most time in? Which do we neglect?
- **Samson vs. the Centurion (Lesson 8 callback):** The centurion's faith was built on recognizing Jesus's divine authority. Samson's failure was built on authority confusion — he lived as though his gifting made him exempt from accountability. What structures of accountability keep you from the Samson pattern?
- **Laodicean self-assessment:** As a class, describe what a Laodicean church looks like from the outside vs. what it believes about itself. Then ask: which description fits us more than we're comfortable admitting?

### Intellectual Challenge

- **The epistemology of guilt:** Romans 3:20 says the law gives *epignōsis* of sin. Secular ethics and psychology have alternative accounts of why humans feel guilt — social conditioning, evolutionary aversion to group sanction. What does Paul's claim add that these alternatives do not capture? Is the law's *epignōsis* different in *kind* from socially conditioned guilt, or only in *content*?

## Theological Threads

- **Law and gospel as unified relational grammar:** Sin = violation of the covenant relationship; gospel = restoration of it; law = description of its healthy expression. The three belong together and become distorted when any one is treated in isolation from the others.
- **The three uses of the law:** Civil (restraining sin in society), convicting (revealing sin in the conscience), and normative (guiding the life of the regenerate). This week presses especially on the third use — law as life-giving guidance for those already justified.
- **Substitutionary atonement upholds the law:** Romans 3:31 says "we uphold the law" through justification by faith. The cross demonstrates that the law's penalty is real, not dismissed. God's forgiveness is not amnesty (overlooking the penalty) but satisfaction (paying it himself).
- **Knowing as relational ontology:** John 17:3 defines eternal life as *knowing* God. This is not propositional information-holding but the Hebrew *yāḏaʿ* — the intimate, covenantal knowing that characterizes the closest human relationships. The entire law-and-gospel project aims at this: creatures who *know* and are *known by* the Creator.
- **The Samson/Laodicea pattern:** Spiritual decline rarely announces itself. It works through small tolerances, accumulated habits of inattention, the slow displacement of Christ's authority by the comfort of one's own spiritual track record. Both Samson and Laodicea had genuine resources — his gifts, their wealth — and both confused the resource with the Resourcer.
