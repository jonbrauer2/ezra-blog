**CHOP!**

Grayson swung the stone tool at a fallen branch.

The branch did not notice.

**CHOP!**

A tiny piece of bark came off.

Grayson examined his progress.

“I think this could take a while.”

“How long?” Colton asked.

“Approximately until I am eighty.”

Colton picked up another stone.

“Let me try.”

“Absolutely not,” Hadley said.

“Why?”

“Because you just threw that stone in the air and tried to catch it with your foot.”

“I almost did.”

“That is the problem.”

Uncle Jonathan stood beside a stack of logs on the bank of the Jordan River.

Around them, young men were measuring, lifting and cutting timber.

They were students of the prophets—men who wanted to learn God’s Word and serve Him.

And they had a problem.

Their meeting place was too small.

“There are worse problems to have,” Uncle Jonathan said. “It means more people want to learn.”

So they had come to the Jordan to build a larger place.

Everyone was working.

Everyone except a small orange kitten sitting on a stump.

The kitten watched the builders with great seriousness.

Hadley scratched behind its ears.

“You look like a supervisor.”

The kitten blinked.

“He is probably making sure we do it correctly,” Grayson said.

Colton saluted. “Yes, Captain Splinters.”

The kitten’s name was immediately settled.

Then a gray-haired man walked down the path.

The builders greeted him warmly.

Elisha had come with them.

---

Elisha was a prophet of the LORD.

Kings asked him for help.

Armies listened when he spoke.

God had used him in astonishing ways.

But that morning, Elisha was not standing before a king.

He was beside a river with builders, children and Captain Splinters.

Grayson liked him immediately.

Elisha noticed the stone in Grayson’s hand.

“What are you building?”

“Eventually?” Grayson said. “A beam.”

Elisha smiled. “Eventually?”

“I need an axe.”

One of the young prophets lifted his own axe.

It had a wooden handle and a dark iron head.

Iron was valuable.

Very valuable.

The man had not been able to afford one of his own, so he had borrowed it.

He checked the head carefully.

Then he swung.

**THUNK!**

The blade bit deep into the wood.

Grayson’s eyes widened.

“That is much better.”

**THUNK!**

Another clean cut.

**THUNK!**

Wood chips flew.

Colton tried to catch them.

Hadley moved him farther away.

The young man worked near the edge of the river.

Swing.

**THUNK!**

Swing.

**THUNK!**

Swing—

**WHIRRRR!**

Something dark shot through the air.

**SPLOOSH!**

Everyone stopped.

The man stared at the empty wooden handle in his hands.

Then he stared at the river.

The iron axe head was gone.

---

For one second, nobody moved.

Then the young man rushed to the water.

“No!”

He searched the surface.

Nothing.

He stepped into the shallows and felt around with his feet.

The riverbed was covered with stones, mud and moving water.

The iron had sunk immediately.

“Oh no, my lord!” he cried to Elisha. “It was borrowed!”

Hadley understood why his voice sounded so frightened.

Losing his own tool would have been bad.

Losing someone else’s was worse.

He had promised to return it.

Now an expensive piece of iron was somewhere beneath the Jordan.

Colton lay on his stomach and peered into the water.

“I can see a rock.”

“Is it shaped like an axe?” Grayson asked.

“No.”

“Then it is not helping.”

Hadley took off her sandals and stepped carefully into the shallows.

The water clouded around her ankles.

Captain Splinters walked onto a log and looked down too.

“Mrrrp?”

“We lost the axe,” Colton explained.

The kitten reached one paw toward the water.

“Do not rescue it,” Hadley said.

Elisha came to the bank.

He did not laugh at the young man.

He did not say, “It’s only an axe.”

He asked one simple question.

“Where did it fall?”

The builder pointed.

“There.”

Elisha looked at the flowing water.

Then he cut a stick.

Grayson watched closely.

Maybe Elisha would use it to feel along the bottom.

Maybe the stick would mark the spot.

Instead, Elisha threw it into the river.

**Splash.**

Colton blinked.

“Now we lost a stick too.”

Hadley gave him a look.

Ripples spread across the surface.

The stick floated.

Of course it floated.

Wood floats.

Then something moved beneath it.

Something dark.

Something heavy.

Something that absolutely did not float.

The iron axe head rose through the water.

It broke the surface.

And there it stayed.

Floating.

---

Grayson forgot to breathe.

Hadley’s mouth fell open.

Colton pointed with both hands.

“THE AXE IS SWIMMING!”

The iron bobbed beside the stick.

Iron sinks.

Everybody knew it.

A dropped leaf floated.

A branch floated.

Captain Splinters, if he made a very poor decision, might float.

But iron did not.

Elisha pointed to it.

“Lift it out.”

The young man stepped into the water.

He reached down.

His fingers closed around the iron.

He lifted it from the river.

Heavy.

Solid.

Real.

The builder held it against his chest.

Relief spread across his face.

“Thank You, LORD.”

Grayson hurried over.

“May I see it?”

The man handed it to him.

Grayson nearly dropped it.

“That is definitely iron.”

Colton poked it.

“Maybe it learned to float.”

“Iron does not learn,” Hadley said.

“Maybe this one is very smart.”

Captain Splinters meowed from the log.

“He agrees with me.”

“He wants a fish.”

“He can want two things.”

---

The builders fitted the iron head back onto the handle.

This time they secured it very carefully.

Very, very carefully.

The work continued.

**THUNK!**

**SCRAPE!**

**LIFT!**

**HEAVE!**

Soon the first beams began to take shape.

Grayson found a safer task carrying wooden pegs.

Hadley helped bring water.

Colton carried one peg at a time with great importance.

Captain Splinters continued supervising.

Later, the children sat with Elisha beneath a tree.

Hadley looked toward the river.

“You’ve helped kings and armies,” she said. “Why would God do a miracle for one borrowed tool?”

Elisha looked at the builders.

“Was it small to the man who lost it?”

Hadley shook her head.

It had mattered enormously to him.

Uncle Jonathan sat beside them.

“Sometimes we decide that our problem is too ordinary for God,” he said. “We think He cares about nations and enormous emergencies, but not about the thing that is keeping us awake.”

Grayson turned the wooden peg in his hands.

“So we should ask God for everything we lose?”

“We can talk to Him about everything,” Uncle Jonathan said. “That doesn’t mean every lost object will miraculously float back to us. God is not a machine that gives us whatever we request.”

Hadley nodded.

The miracle was not a promise that nothing would ever stay lost.

It was a picture of God’s care.

He had seen one worried builder beside one muddy river.

He had cared about honesty.

He had cared about a debt.

He had cared about a borrowed tool.

Colton leaned against Uncle Jonathan.

“What if the man had been too embarrassed to tell Elisha?”

“Then he would have stood there staring at an empty handle,” Uncle Jonathan said.

The man had cried out.

He had admitted what happened.

He had shown Elisha where it fell.

And help began with honesty.

---

By evening, several strong beams stood beside the river.

The builders gathered their tools.

The young prophet held the borrowed axe with both hands.

He would return it.

Not because he had solved the impossible problem.

Not because he had dived deep enough.

Not because iron had suddenly changed its nature.

He would return it because God had shown kindness.

As the group started home, Captain Splinters followed the children.

“I think he belongs to us now,” Colton said.

Hadley looked back toward the building site.

One of the workers was calling.

“Splinters! Here, kitty!”

Captain Splinters turned around and trotted back.

Colton sighed.

“Borrowed kitten.”

Grayson patted his shoulder. “At least we did not drop him in the river.”

“He would float.”

“Probably.”

“Because he is very smart.”

Hadley laughed.

They walked along the Jordan as the sunlight turned gold.

The river kept flowing over the place where the iron had disappeared.

There was no sign of the miracle now.

Only a stick drifting downstream.

But the children would remember.

No concern was too ordinary to bring to God.

No mistake had to be hidden from Him.

And no problem—not even one as heavy as iron—was too heavy for God to lift.

Based on 2 Kings 6:1–7.
