“Do we really need another jar?” Hadley asked.

Grayson was carrying two.

Colton was carrying one that was nearly as large as he was.

Uncle Jonathan had a jar under each arm.

An orange kitten sat inside a basket on top of the largest jar.

“We were told to borrow as many as possible,” Grayson said.

“We have already borrowed as many as possible.”

“That house has three more.”

Hadley looked down the street.

A boy was running toward them with two clay bowls and a pitcher.

Behind him, his older brother carried another jar.

“Apparently we have not.”

Colton’s jar slipped.

“Whoa—whoa—WHOA!”

Uncle Jonathan caught it with one knee.

Hadley caught Colton.

Grayson caught the basket.

The kitten caught nothing, but looked deeply offended.

“Everybody all right?” Uncle Jonathan asked.

“Yes,” said Hadley.

“Yes,” said Grayson.

“Yes,” said Colton.

“Mrrrrow,” said the kitten.

“Olive is upset,” Colton explained.

“You named the kitten Olive?”

“We are collecting oil jars.”

Hadley had to admit the name fit.

The children carried the vessels into a small house.

The room was already crowded.

Jars stood on the floor.

Pitchers covered the table.

Bowls lined the wall.

There were tall jars, short jars, wide jars and one very strange jar with two crooked handles.

And all of them were empty.

---

The woman who lived there was a widow.

Her husband had been one of the prophets who served the LORD.

But he had died.

Now she was alone with her two sons.

And she owed money she could not pay.

The creditor had come to take her sons away to work off the debt.

When Hadley heard that, her stomach tightened.

The widow was not worried about losing furniture.

She was terrified of losing her children.

So she had gone to Elisha.

“Your servant, my husband, is dead,” she cried. “You know that he honored the LORD. But now his creditor is coming to take my two boys as his slaves.”

Elisha listened.

Then he asked her two questions.

“How can I help you? Tell me, what do you have in your house?”

The widow looked around.

The answer was almost nothing.

“Your servant has nothing there at all,” she said, “except a small jar of olive oil.”

That was it.

One small jar.

Not enough to pay a debt.

Not enough to change their future.

Barely enough for a little cooking.

But Elisha said, “Go around and ask all your neighbors for empty jars.”

Then he added something important.

“Don’t ask for just a few.”

That was why Hadley, Grayson, Colton, Uncle Jonathan and both sons had spent the afternoon knocking on doors.

**KNOCK. KNOCK.**

“May we borrow an empty jar?”

Again.

**KNOCK. KNOCK.**

“Do you have another vessel?”

Again.

And again.

Some neighbors gave pitchers.

Some gave bowls.

Some gave large storage jars.

The house filled with emptiness.

Empty jar after empty jar.

---

At last the widow closed the door.

Elisha had told her to go inside with her sons.

This would not be a performance for the street.

It would be a quiet miracle in one family’s home.

Hadley and the others stood with the boys among the vessels.

The widow held her little jar of oil.

Colton whispered, “Which jar should we fill first?”

Grayson pointed to the smallest bowl.

“That one. We might have enough.”

The widow began to pour.

A golden stream flowed from the little jar.

It splashed into the bowl.

The oil rose.

Halfway.

Three-quarters.

Full.

The widow straightened the small jar.

Everyone stared at the filled bowl.

There should have been almost nothing left.

She tipped the jar over a pitcher.

The oil poured again.

**Glug-glug-glug.**

The pitcher filled.

The stream did not stop.

“Another jar,” the widow said.

Her older son pushed one forward.

Oil poured.

It filled.

“Another.”

Grayson brought a wider vessel.

Oil poured.

It filled.

“Another!”

Hadley slid a tall jar across the floor.

Oil poured.

It filled.

---

Soon everyone was moving.

Empty jars went in.

Full jars came out.

The widow kept pouring.

The little jar never ran dry.

Colton tried to look inside while she poured.

Hadley pulled him back.

“You are going to put your head in the oil.”

“I want to see where it is coming from.”

“It is coming from God.”

“I know, but where inside the jar?”

There was no hidden pipe.

No second container.

No enormous reservoir behind the wall.

Just one small jar in the widow’s hands.

And God’s provision.

Olive the kitten jumped onto the table.

He peered into an empty pot.

“Do not put the kitten in the jar,” Uncle Jonathan said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

Olive climbed into it by himself.

Only his ears and tail showed.

“That jar is occupied,” Grayson announced.

The widow laughed.

It may have been the first time she had laughed in days.

Colton lifted Olive out.

“We need this one.”

The widow filled it.

Then another.

Then another.

The room changed.

At first it had been full of empty vessels and fear.

Now it was full of oil and astonishment.

---

The widow kept pouring.

Her arms should have been tired.

Her jar should have been empty.

Instead, the oil continued.

At last she called to her son.

“Bring me another jar.”

The boys looked around.

Behind the door?

Full.

Under the table?

Full.

Beside the wall?

Full.

The crooked-handled jar?

Full.

The oldest son checked the final bowl.

“There isn’t one left.”

The stream stopped.

Not a drop sooner.

The widow held the little jar upright.

Silence filled the room.

Every borrowed vessel was full.

Hadley turned slowly.

Golden oil shone in jars all around them.

The miracle had stopped only when there was no empty space left to receive it.

The widow covered her face and began to cry.

Not frightened tears this time.

Relieved tears.

Her sons wrapped their arms around her.

Uncle Jonathan quietly led the children outside so the family could be together.

Olive followed.

For once, even Colton was silent.

---

The widow returned to Elisha.

She told him what had happened.

Elisha said, “Go, sell the oil and pay your debts. You and your sons can live on what is left.”

God had not given her just enough for one meal.

He had provided a way to pay what she owed.

He had protected her sons.

He had given the family a future.

The next morning, customers came.

One bought a pitcher of oil.

Another bought a jar.

Then another.

Coins replaced the debt.

The widow paid the creditor.

Her sons stayed home.

And there was still oil left.

Hadley watched the brothers carry an empty borrowed jar back to a neighbor.

“The neighbors helped too,” she said.

Uncle Jonathan nodded. “God provided the miracle, but the family had to ask for jars. And the neighbors had to be willing to lend them.”

Grayson looked at the row of houses.

“What if they had only borrowed three?”

“Then there would have been three full jars,” Hadley said.

Elisha had told them not to ask for just a few.

Faith had looked like knocking on another door.

And another.

And another.

Carrying empty jars before they had seen one drop of extra oil.

---

That afternoon, the children helped return the last vessels.

Colton carried the crooked-handled jar.

Olive rode in the basket again.

“Why didn’t God just make coins appear?” Colton asked.

Uncle Jonathan considered the question.

“He could have. But He began with something the widow already had.”

One little jar of oil.

It did not look like much.

But placed in God’s hands, it was the beginning of everything they needed.

Hadley thought about the empty vessels.

“Sometimes asking for help feels embarrassing.”

The widow had gone to Elisha.

Then she had gone to her neighbors.

She had admitted her need.

She had gathered emptiness.

And God had filled it.

Grayson looked back at the widow’s house.

“Does this mean God will always give us more money if we ask?”

“No,” Uncle Jonathan said. “This miracle belongs to this family’s story. God’s provision does not always look the same. Sometimes He changes a situation. Sometimes He gives strength, wisdom or people to walk through it with us.”

But the story showed what God was like.

He saw the widow.

He heard her cry.

He cared about her sons.

He was not frightened by her debt.

And He could begin with what seemed far too small.

Colton lifted Olive from the basket.

“This kitten is also small.”

“What are you expecting God to fill him with?” Hadley asked.

“Dinner.”

Olive meowed.

“Finally,” said Grayson, “something we all understand.”

They laughed and followed the widow’s sons toward the house.

On the table sat the little clay jar.

Ordinary.

Quiet.

Small enough to hold in two hands.

But no one in that family would ever look at an empty jar the same way again.

Based on 2 Kings 4:1–7.
